1The two-stage spine

The central bet of this course is that you should learn the graphics pipeline twice. First on the CPU, where you own every pixel and nothing is hidden. Then on the GPU, where every concept you meet is one you have already built by hand.

By the time SDL_GPU asks you to fill in a depth-stencil state, you will have written the depth test yourself and will know exactly what you are configuring. That is the whole point: recognition, not incantation.

2Who this is for

  • You can program in some language. C++ specifics — RAII, ownership and move semantics, references vs pointers, templates, const-correctness, headers and translation units — are taught in place, the first time each appears, never as a forward reference to a chapter you have not read.
  • No assumed background in graphics, linear algebra, or calculus. All maths is built from zero, geometrically. Perspective projection arrives via similar triangles before any matrix. Quaternions arrive via complex numbers in 2D. Barycentric coordinates arrive via signed areas.
  • A desktop or laptop — Windows, Linux, or macOS — and roughly 300–500 hours.

Exit profile. By the final lesson you can implement rendering techniques directly from papers and blog posts, debug GPU work in RenderDoc, reason about frame budgets and cache behaviour, design and defend engine architecture, and read real engine codebases without drowning. In short: hireable as a junior-to-mid engine or graphics programmer.

3How the course works

Every lesson follows the same shape, and ends with something visible on screen or measurably changed:

  1. The Problem — what is broken, missing, or impossible without today's idea.
  2. Building Intuition — pictures and analogies, before any formalism.
  3. The Theory — derivations, each followed by a worked example with real numbers.
  4. Implementation — short steps, narrated, every decision justified as it is made.
  5. Complete listings — every file touched, in full, with zero placeholders.
  6. Build & run, pitfalls, exercises, recap, further reading.

Three living pages

Conventions Handedness, matrices, NDC and depth, winding, units, colour. Read before Module 2. Convention mismatches are the number-one source of silent rendering bugs.

No build step — for the lessons

Every lesson is a plain HTML file. No server, no npm, no tooling: open it and read, even offline. Styling comes from one shared stylesheet the pages link, so keep the docs/ folder together — a lesson copied out on its own will look unstyled. The C++ obviously builds with CMake — that is the whole point — but the tutorial itself never asks you to build anything to read it.

Module 2 Software Rasterizer I: From Vertices to Screen CPU complete 12 lessons · ~54 h

The heart of the course. We build the entire 3D transformation pipeline from scratch and derive every matrix in it — including perspective projection, from similar triangles, before any 4×4 grid of numbers appears.

2.1Lines: DDA, then Bresenham publishedTwo algorithms derived from the pixel grid up — then measured, and the famous reason for preferring the second turns out to have expired. DDA is 2.2× faster on a 2026 CPU; we ship Bresenham anyway, for exactness, with the number written down.4 h
2.2The Triangle: Edge Functions publishedA triangle is three half-planes intersected — so "inside" is three independent yes/no questions, which is exactly why this and not scanline filling is what parallelises. Derives the edge function from 1.7's dot product, makes it incremental (75× measured), and lands the fill rule that closes 2.1's asymmetry.5 h
2.3Barycentric Coordinates from Signed Areas publishedDerived from the areas the edge functions already compute, not handed down. The defining property is that the weights reconstruct the point — which catches the vertex-pairing bug that “they sum to 1” cheerfully passes.5 h
2.4Interpolating Attributes Across a Triangle publishedThe weights finally carry something — and the formula is the easy part. Two of the three things that must go right are silent when they do not: the fill rule’s bias, still in the accumulators, displaces the whole attribute field by 1/‖e‖ pixels; and averaging stored colour values instead of light puts the centre of an R/G/B triangle at 85 where it belongs at 156.5 h
2.5Matrices as Basis Transforms publishedA matrix is where the basis vectors land — ask that one question and the multiplication rule, the rotation matrix and the determinant all fall out rather than needing to be memorised. The determinant turns out to be edge_function with its first point at the origin, and we check it by counting pixels.5 h
2.6Building mat4 by Hand publishedThe same question in 3-D, so nothing needs re-deriving — plus the three axis rotations built from the cycle x → y → z, which is what puts Ry’s minus sign where it actually belongs. Then the 4×4, whose fourth column moves a cube by exactly zero pixels. That is where the lesson stops.4 h
2.7Homogeneous Coordinates and What w Really Means publishedThe fourth component gets a job — and not one line of arithmetic changes. w = 1 is a position and gets translated; w = 0 is a direction and must not be. The same number, allowed to be anything else, turns out to be the mechanism behind perspective.4 h
2.8The Space Chain: Model to World publishedA coordinate is three floats and a room — and naming the room stops an entire class of bug. The model matrix is T · R · S, the order derived from what each transform means, and both wrong orders shown on screen: one deforms, one misplaces. One mesh becomes three objects in a visible world.4 h
2.9The View Matrix: Deriving Look-At publishedA camera is just an object with a transform, and the view matrix is the inverse of its placement — so moving the camera left is moving the world right. Introduces the cross product where it is first needed, inverts a rigid transform for free, and flies an orbit camera around the scene.5 h
2.10Perspective from Similar Triangles publishedThe keystone. Perspective is one divide by depth, derived from similar triangles before any matrix — then packaged as the projection matrix that writes -z into w. Where w finally stops being 1, the perspective divide gets its name, and depth's non-linearity is made a number. A [P] toggle flips perspective/orthographic on screen.6 h
2.11The Viewport Transform publishedThe last hop: NDC to framebuffer pixels. Three affine maps and one flip — the +y-up-to-+y-down sign that has drifted through the course since Lesson 2.5, finally derived and given one home in an engine::viewport that mirrors SDL_GPUViewport. The coordinate chain is now complete.3 h
2.12Milestone: A Spinning Wireframe Mesh publishedModule 2 complete. Indexed geometry (vertices + triangle indices — what the GPU wants), a regular icosahedron derived from three golden rectangles with φ forced by regularity, mesh validation via Euler's formula and winding, and the whole chain lit end to end. Plus an honest inventory of everything still missing, each handed to the Module 3 lesson that fixes it.4 h

Module 3 Software Rasterizer II: Depth, Light, Texture CPU complete 10 lessons · ~46 h

We turn a wireframe into a picture. Each lesson opens with the artifact you get without the idea — overdraw chaos, swimming textures, faces punched inside-out — because a bug you have seen is a concept you remember.

3.1The Painter's Problem and the Z-Buffer publishedSorting cannot be repaired, and three independent proofs are built rather than asserted: a triangle has no single depth, overlaps can form a cycle, and intersecting triangles change the answer halfway across. Then one float per pixel — and the derivation that says which depth to store, because device depth is exactly affine in screen space and view-space depth is a hyperbola that reads 50.5 where the truth is 1.98. Ends with a precision budget you can compute before you see the z-fighting.5 h
3.2Perspective-Correct Interpolation publishedA checkerboard on a floor buckles, and kinks along a diagonal that exists nowhere in the geometry — the signature of an entire console generation. The fix is three lines, and it follows from the bracket 3.1 already proved: 1/w is affine in screen space, so a/w is too. Interpolate both, divide at the end. Ends by measuring the escape route people took instead: subdivision converges quadratically and never arrives.5 h
3.3Near-Plane Clipping publishedWalk into the floor and it vanishes; delete the guard that makes it vanish and it detonates across the screen. Both are one bug: the perspective divide has a precondition, not a guard, and it must be enforced a stage earlier. Derives the near plane as z_clip = 0 from the projection’s own depth row, shows why w ≥ 0 is a different plane that admits coordinates 691,714 pixels wide, and implements Sutherland–Hodgman for polygons and segments. The camera can now move through the world.5 h
3.4Back-Face Culling publishedOne comparison — the sign of a signed area the rasterizer was already computing and then deliberately throwing away. Derives which sign from the viewport’s y-flip rather than finding it by trial; shows that dot(n, camera_forward) is a different question that misjudges 15% of triangles at our field of view and a third at 120°; and settles Lesson 3.1’s 29-pixel debt exactly as predicted. Two folk facts get measured and corrected on the way.3 h
3.5A Hand-Rolled OBJ Loader publishedParsing is an afternoon; the lesson is the sentence hiding in f 1/1/1. A file gives every face corner three independent indices and a vertex buffer has one, so a cube’s 8 positions become 24 vertices — the first time this course reshapes data to suit the hardware, which is what an asset pipeline is. Then the debts: you cannot assume a loaded mesh’s winding or its watertightness, so both get measured — Euler characteristic (0 for the torus, not 2), boundary and non-manifold edges, and signed volume by the divergence theorem. Round-tripped through our own writer: 0 pixels different.6 h
3.6Normals and Lambert’s Cosine Law publishedWhy cos θ — from a torch tilted at a table, where the beam does not dim but spreads over a footprint 1/cos θ wider. Then the result that catches everyone: a normal is not transformed by the model matrix. Derived from the one property that defines a normal — perpendicular to every tangent — the answer is the inverse transpose, and the naive version tilts a normal by up to 68° and gets 97.5% of a squashed object’s pixels wrong. It is also invisible under rotation and uniform scale, which is exactly why it ships.5 h
3.7Specular and Blinn-Phong publishedThe first shading term that changes when you move your head — and 3.6 had measured that nothing else did. The mirror direction from 1.8’s bounce with the arrow reversed; the halfway vector derived as exactly the normal a microfacet would need, which is the doorway to Module 6. Then the parts everyone repeats without checking: Blinn’s exponent must be ~4× Phong’s (fitted: 4.38× falling to 4.01×), and Phong’s cut-off is not the mirror ray dipping below the surface — it never does. Measured on a plane: at 35° sun elevation Phong highlights 0 of 30,806 lit pixels and Blinn all of them. Ends by showing, not claiming, that per-vertex is the wrong home for a highlight: cube.obj has none at all in 157 of 180 frames of a spin.5 h
3.8Flat, Gouraud and Per-Pixel Shading publishedLesson 3.6 shipped one enum holding two questions, and its own comment said so. They separate here: where a normal comes from is a property of the mesh, where the equation is evaluated is a property of the pipeline, and the 2×3 grid they span has cells that provably coincide — with a face normal all three evaluation points agree to 0 pixels, for exactly as long as the shading is view-independent. Gouraud turns out to be a name for what 2.4 and 3.6 already built. Mach bands turn out to be in your eye rather than the framebuffer. And the folklore about cost is wrong below three pixels per triangle, where per-pixel is measured at 0.91× — cheaper — rising to 2.15× at 4K.5 h
3.9Texture Mapping and Bilinear Filtering publishedEvery colour so far came from a rule. This one comes from an array, and the four lines that change in the fill loop are the least interesting part. Everything else follows from one sentence — a texel is a sample, not a square — including the half-texel offset, which nearest-neighbour sampling literally cannot see: 0 of 160,801 samples change without it, against 160,632 under bilinear, which is why the bug ships and gets blamed on the filter. The uv origin is settled by quoting SDL_gpu.h rather than by trying both, and the flip lands at the import step — not the parser, not the sampler. Bilinear is derived as two lerps then one; the sRGB decode goes before the filter, worth 0.286 of full light at the worst pair. The floor is finally lit, because a texture supplies the albedo a formula never could. And the obvious benchmark, kept and labelled, measures the wrong thing: 5.04× collapses to 1.12× once the encode is held constant.5 h
3.10Profiling, and the Module 3 Capstone publishedModule 3 finishes by measuring what it built — and the measurements disagree with everybody. The 2-triangle floor costs 8× what the 2,304-triangle torus does, because the fill is paid per pixel: 45.01 against 64.14 ns/px, while ns/triangle differ by 9,300×. Inside the fragment loop the perspective divide Lesson 3.2 warned about is free (−0.018 ns/px) and the largest single item is std::pow in the sRGB encode, at 6.09 ns/px — more than coverage, interpolation, the divide and the depth test combined. A fitted four-term approximation fixes it: 1.30× on the fill, 1.29× on the frame, checked against Amdahl's law, at a cost of 0.56% of pixels moving by one code out of 255. Along the way: a profiler that calibrates itself (this machine cannot resolve anything under 4.17 µs), why instrumenting the inner loop makes it 1.86× slower and under-reports, and one hypothesis that was measured and found false rather than quietly dropped. Capstone: a textured, lit, z-buffered scene, every pixel on the CPU, with a frame budget that accounts for all of it.5 h

Milestone: a textured, lit, depth-buffered 3D scene rendered entirely on the CPU — every stage written by you.

Module 4 To the GPU: SDL_GPU GPU complete 9 lessons · ~40 h

Everything you built on the CPU, now in silicon. Each SDL_GPU concept is introduced by mapping it back to the software counterpart you already wrote — so the API feels like a translation, not a mystery.

4.1How GPUs Actually Work publishedA GPU is not a fast CPU — per lane it is slower, and it wins anyway. Three reasons, all measured on the rasterizer you already own. Wide, not fast: eight interleaved chains of identical work run 7× faster than one dependent chain and 32 run 27.88×, which is what occupancy means. Lockstep: a warp runs both sides of a branch its lanes disagree about — measured 1.93× when incoherent, 1.00× when coherent, with the step falling exactly at the warp width of 32. 2×2 quads: the fragment shader runs on lanes the triangle misses, because ddx is lane 1 minus lane 0 — so efficiency falls from 96.3% on a 64-pixel triangle to 25.0% on a one-pixel one, and that is what “small triangles are expensive” actually means. Your rasterizer gains a quad traversal that produces a bit-identical image in colour and depth while shading 21,005 lanes whose results go nowhere. Ends by counting the eleven pipeline stages you have already written, and by measuring the usual justification for immutable pipeline objects and finding it false — the real reason is validation and compilation, not branch cost.4 h
4.2The SDL_GPU Mental Model publishedNine objects, and five of them are renames of things you already built — framebuffer to swapchain texture, sampler to sampler, fill_style to pipeline. The four that are new all exist because a call now records work instead of doing it: measured, 0.1879 ms of CPU calls describe 4.7815 ms of GPU work, a factor of 25. Read the download before the fence and it is wrong 64 times out of 64. Then the result that contradicts the advice: a full GPU sync every frame costs nothing measurable when the frame is display-bound (fence 0.761 ms, acquire falls by 0.730, frame unchanged) and 1.60× when it is not — because a sync costs the overlap you gave up, not the duration of the wait. Also settled three modules early, by measurement: an SDL_FColor clear of 0.5 lands as byte 128 or 188 depending only on the target’s format, and the sRGB one matches your own linear_to_srgb_u8 to the code. Ends with your software rasterizer’s picture on screen carried entirely by SDL_GPU, no shaders anywhere, plus a live frame graph drawn with fill_rect. And a benchmark that reported 763 GB/s on a 273 GB/s bus, kept in the lesson with both reasons it was wrong.5 h
4.3The Shader Toolchain publishedOne HLSL file must become three binaries, because Metal, Vulkan and D3D12 do not speak the same language — and SPIR-V is the hub, so losing the front end costs you every backend rather than one. Wired into CMake with a configure-time capability probe, because SDL_shadercross can be built without DirectXShaderCompiler and such a build cannot read HLSL at all — the machine this lesson was written on has exactly that, and the fallback route is in the build. Then the three ways a correct-looking shader goes wrong, each measured: a register space in the wrong place compiles, loads, runs and reads the wrong slot (verify it with spirv-dis, which is how Figure 2’s right-hand column was produced); the entry point is renamed behind your back, because main is reserved in MSL, and passing "main" is refused; and the four resource counts SDL demands are validated by nobody — 99 samplers on a shader with one was accepted without a murmur, which is why they are read from the compiler’s own reflection file and a missing file refuses the load. Ends with four HLSL files as objects on the GPU and a prediction written down before it is checked: SDL_CreateGPUShader takes 8 µs, which cannot include a compile — so where does it happen? 4.4 finds out.5 h
4.4The First Triangle publishedThe first pixel in this course whose colour was computed by hardware — and it takes a pipeline object, a vertex buffer and a layout, because a GPU draw call carries almost no arguments: SDL_DrawGPUPrimitives(pass, 3, 1, 0, 0) says “three vertices” and everything else was bound beforehand. Settles the debt 4.3 opened: the shader compile happens at pipeline creation, measured at about 2.4 ms per new state permutation against 0.031 for creating the shader. How much more depends entirely on the driver’s cache — which lives on disk, so a pipeline compiled in a previous run is already compiled in this one, and two separate wrong conclusions were drawn from this call in drafts of the lesson before that was noticed. Both are kept. Then the failures: creation refuses almost nothing (a mismatched colour format and a bogus attribute are both accepted; a shader in the wrong slot segfaults), and reversing two vertices does not flip the triangle, it deletes it — 20,808 pixels to 0, with the third panel proving the geometry never changed. Ends with the comparison Module 2 was for: the same triangle through fill_triangle and through the GPU, downloaded and compared pixel by pixel — zero disagreements in the interior, 102 on the boundary, and the difference is a documented fill rule rather than a bug.4 h
4.5Vertex Buffers and Layouts publishedReal geometry on the device at last: assets/torus.obj, 1,225 vertices interleaved into 32-byte records, uploaded once and drawn seven times. A vertex layout is three numbers and one formula — base + i×pitch + offset — evaluated by hardware with no way to know whether the numbers are right, and of six broken layouts handed to pipeline creation SDL refuses exactly one. So we build the check ourselves, out of the reflection JSON Lesson 4.3 has been emitting all along, and say plainly which three of five mistakes it catches. Then the failure modes as pictures, from real downloaded pixels: a pitch four bytes wrong shatters the mesh progressively (the error is i×4, so vertex 0 is always fine — which is how the bug survives a three-vertex test), while a wrong offset collapses it onto a unit sphere. A format turns out to answer two questions with different answers, which makes 4.4’s vertex 43% smaller with the shader untouched; index buffers are 4.17× smaller here and turn a fixed 6,912 vertex-shader invocations into a range; and instancing costs one enum value — 196 bytes a frame against 53,024 that never move again.4 h
4.6Uniform Data and the Matrix Upload publishedThe camera in 4.5’s shader was seven static const floats, frozen at compile time because there was no way to send it anything. Fixing that turns out to mean learning a mechanism unlike any other in SDL_GPU: there is no uniform buffer object — no UNIFORM usage bit exists — you push bytes onto the command buffer and every draw after them reads them. No lifetime, no binding, no cycling hazard. Then the two questions that decide whether your data arrives: the packing rule turns out to be HLSL’s, not the std140 SDL’s header cites, and a naive struct delivers a trailing float3 as (242, 243, 0) where (241, 242, 243) was written — shifted one float, silently, with every earlier field intact. And a claim this course has carried since Lesson 2.6 finally gets checked: all sixteen matrix elements pushed and read back individually, no transpose anywhere — even though the compiled SPIR-V says RowMajor, which is the toolchain describing your data in its own vocabulary. Ends with a camera you can fly, and the missing depth test becoming impossible to ignore.4 h
4.7Textures, Samplers, and Depth publishedBoth ports land almost exactly as Lessons 3.1 and 3.9 promised they would — the sampler is two static_casts with a regression test behind them, and the depth test is three pipeline fields plus an attachment. So the lesson spends its length on what is not a port. Depth precision falls off as the square of distance, because the perspective divide puts 1/d in the stored value: 70% of our range is spent in the first metre, and D16 cannot separate two walls 41 cm apart at ninety metres. The formula is derived and then measured — agreeing to a few per cent across two orders of magnitude — after two measurement bugs that are kept in the lesson, one of them aliasing in the probe itself. Reversed-Z is measured at 180× on a float format and exactly nothing on a UNORM one, which is the control that makes it believable. Along the way: the first third-party code in the project, with the test for when to hand-roll (is the hard part the subject?); why D24_UNORM does not exist on this machine; and the one enum that decides whether your lighting maths is right.5 h
4.8Porting the Module-3 Scene publishedThe payoff, and it is deliberately undramatic — then audited, because two modules of convention work were done on the promise that this would be an API change and not a maths change, and a promise nobody checks is a wish. Of fifteen pipeline stages: two are literally the same C++ function called from both sides, eight became fixed-function hardware, one was translated line for line — and two could not cross at all, because a cull mode and a material are pipeline state, which is 3.8’s predicted bill arriving. So one loop becomes N draws, and data gets grouped by rate of change rather than by subject. Two findings the port forced out: a vertex shader sees one vertex, so the face-normal fallback moves into the importer and 3.8’s flat/smooth key becomes a property of the vertex buffer (a cube’s 8 positions become 36); and the naive normal matrix is invisible on boxes by construction — 0 pixels on two non-uniformly scaled boxes, 2,160 on a squashed icosahedron — so a test scene made of crates proves nothing. Ends with both renderers drawing one scene side by side and compared per pixel: 87% byte-identical, the shading equation itself within one float ULP, and a one-code floor in the darks where two implementations of the sRGB curve part company. Never report a percentage without a magnitude.6 h
4.9Debugging a Frame with RenderDoc publishedThe screen is black, every call returned success, and the debugger from Lesson 0.6 has nothing to say — because recording and executing are separated by a submit, and the bug lives on the far side of it. So: the tool that can see it, and the engine work that makes a frame worth capturing. The awkward fact first, because burying it would waste your time — RenderDoc does not support Metal, so on a Mac the tool is Xcode’s Metal Debugger and every concept transfers; SDL_gpu.h has a Debugging section saying exactly that, on your disk. Then names (attached at creation, because SDL’s docs steer you away from the setter you find first — and a comment in this codebase spent four lessons confidently explaining an asymmetry that did not exist), debug groups as an immovable C++ scope (Metal scopes a group pushed inside a pass to that pass), and a frame log the engine prints itself: engine --trace, 33 events, cross-checked against draw_stats and agreeing. The measurements took three attempts and all three are in the lesson: the first reported a negative cost for adding work, which is the tell that you have measured noise. Measure the floor, then scale the workload until the effect clears it — 183.6 and 182.0 ns from two independent estimates, and the agreement is the evidence. Ends by declaring one debt unpayable on this hardware and modelling it instead, which turns up something better: vertex reuse is a property of the index order, and shuffling triangles costs 2.83× with the geometry untouched.4 h

Milestone: the Module-3 scene running on the GPU across Vulkan, D3D12, and Metal — and you can capture and read a frame in RenderDoc.

Module 5 Becoming an Engine GPU in progress 11 lessons · ~54 h

The transition from "a program that renders" to "an engine other programs are built on". The refactor is taught as a first-class architecture lesson, not rushed through as a chore — because the boundary it draws is law for the rest of the course.

5.1The Refactor: Engine, Demos, and the Public API publishedFour harnesses in a row had to transcribe the scene by hand, because build_scene was trapped in an anonymous namespace inside a 7,789-line main.cpp. So we draw the line: a static library whose public headers live under engine/include/engine/, and demos that may include nothing else — enforced by the include path, not the style guide, so the old spelling is now a compile error rather than a review comment. Publishing the pipeline is what finally forces it to be designed: collect_triangles goes from 15 parameters to 8, seven throwaway out-parameter variables vanish, and every default becomes the correct answer. Physical design gets measured rather than asserted — touching a private source costs 0.42 s, a public header 0.97 s, and 37/37 headers compile alone. 6.1Linear and sRGB: The Gamma Lesson publishedThe lesson every graphics programmer needs exactly once and then has for ever — and it opens the module because every lighting result in Module 6 is wrong until the colour space is settled, so there is no point deriving a BRDF first. It opens instead with a claim about the code you have been running since 4.8: every pixel the GPU path has drawn has been too dark, by 2.3× at mid grey and 13× in shadow. Two integers carry the whole subject — code 128 emits 0.2159 of white, not half, and half the light is code 188 — and the encoding that separates them is derived as a budget rather than a CRT artefact: of 256 codes, spending them evenly in light puts 26 in the darkest tenth and 128 in the brightest half, where the eye can barely tell two apart, while sRGB puts 90 in the darks. 8 bits of linear light would need about 12 to look as smooth. The piecewise definition is then derived rather than quoted: the slope of x^(1/2.4) at zero is infinite, which amplifies sensor noise without limit and cannot be inverted stably, so the bottom is replaced by a line of slope 12.92 — and skipping it, as every pow(x, 1/2.2) snippet does, costs 8 codes exactly where the eye has the most codes to notice. Then the part worth copying into your own work: an audit. 6.2Radiometry-Lite: What a BRDF Is published6.1 settled what the numbers mean at the output stage; this one asks what they are on the way there, and the honest answer is that nobody could say — shade() multiplied an albedo by a light and called the product “the light leaving the surface” without a unit anywhere in the sentence. So: four words for light, each with a unit, and the one that matters is radiance, because doubling the distance divides the irradiance by four and the subtended solid angle by four, so their ratio does not move — which is the entire reason a pixel can be one number with no distance term anywhere. Then a BRDF, stated as what it is: a ratio, radiance out over irradiance in, whose unit is inverse steradians — and that unit settles the question everyone asks once, because a surface reflecting 100% of the arriving light into a 20° cone has a BRDF of 2.7210 sr⁻¹ against Lambert’s 0.3183 and invents nothing; a mirror’s is unbounded. 6.3Microfacet Theory publishedLesson 6.2 ended on 1.1386 — a white surface with a white highlight reflecting more light than arrives — and this lesson opens on it, because the question it forces is not “which constant is wrong?” but if that light bounced off the surface, how can it also have gone into it? In its place, one sentence taken literally — a surface is a landscape of microscopic PERFECT mirrors — and shading collapses into a counting problem, because a facet reflects l to v only if its own normal is h. Lesson 3.7’s halfway vector, “the normal this surface would need”, stops being a way of putting it. So the highlight’s shape is a histogram of surface slopes and roughness is that histogram’s width — a thing a profilometer can measure. And because the histogram is a DISTRIBUTION it must satisfy ∫ D(h) cos θₕ dω = 1, which is the first equation in this shading arc with a right-hand side you can hold a model to; read backwards it says the microfacets’ projected areas add up to the flat area they stand on, so the cosine is not a convention. That test then does all the work. If yes it is per-draw data and two objects differing in it draw back to back; if no it is pipeline state, baked into a pipeline object, and two objects differing in it need two pipelines and a sort — which is exactly what Lesson 4.8 already paid for and measures with pipeline_binds. The texture reference becomes a handle, and the old comment defending the pointer is worth reading — “textures lives for the whole program, so there is nothing here that can dangle” was true, and was an argument about that demo rather than about the type. §C then moves the pool with 64 insertions (0x928c03500 → 0x928c16000) and the handle does not care, because it names a slot rather than an address; a stale one resolves to “no image” instead of freed memory, which is the case a pointer cannot express at all. bind_albedo is the named resolve between them — a handle is how you store, a pointer is how you use, and the conversion happens once per draw, because per pixel is exactly where a bounds check is unaffordable. The textured flag becomes derived, removing a state the program could previously represent: there is no longer any way to say “textured, but no image”, and a bug you cannot express beats one you detect — a point the lesson nearly undermined, because the first attempt at uniforms_of compiled cleanly and would have silently lost every GPU texture. Sharing is measured rather than asserted: 96 ring drones over 6 tints is 3,456 bytes against 600, a 5.8× reduction — and the better argument is that “make the drones rougher” goes from a loop over the registry to one write. Note the rule that falls out: scene_object still holds its material by value, because it has one. The rule is about sharing, not size, and “handles for big things” gets it backwards. Two comments that had been repeating for three modules turn out to be wrong. closed was never material state — a material is explicitly not pipeline state, and closed is not cull mode anyway but a fact about the mesh, which validate() already counts from its edges; the mesh supplies the fact, the caller the intent, cull_of is the one place the rule lives. And the compile-time argument for splitting out cull.hpp did not survive being measured: 5.1’s quoted 0.97 s came back as 0.258 s against material.hpp’s own 0.261 s, so the marginal saving is zero — and 5.1’s note had said in capitals that the ratio travels and the seconds do not. The physical-design rule never needed a performance argument, and the only false part of the case was the one added to strengthen it. A refactor, so there is exactly one claim: the reference render byte-identical at E917C06C, verified after each of the three stages separately — move without changing, then change without moving. 27 checks; scene_object grew 96 → 112 bytes and verify_56 caught it, with the 16-byte sampler named as the debt.4 h
6.6glTF 2.0 Loading publishedLesson 3.5 wrote an OBJ parser by hand and this one takes a library, and the whole interest is why the two answers differ. The usual test — “is it hard?” — is useless here, because the rasterizer was hard and we wrote it. The test this course has actually been using is whether the hard part is the subject, and for glTF it is not: the hard part is that an accessor may be any of five component types, normalized or not, tight or interleaved at a stride, dense or sparse — roughly forty legal encodings of the same eight positions, every one of which some exporter emits, and not one of them about graphics. But the deeper difference is structural: OBJ is a bag of triangles and glTF is a scene. A mesh is a list of primitives and each has its own material, so a car body and its windscreen must be two draws; flattening them gives geometry that is correct and unpaintable. Then the part that makes this lesson worth its position in the module — the import is a copy. metallicFactor and roughnessFactor go straight into 6.4’s microsurface with no conversion at all, because glTF’s alpha remap is roughness² and so is ours, its dielectric IOR is 1.5 and so is ours, and gold’s baseColorFactor of (1.00, 0.71, 0.29) comes out of f0_of verbatim while diffuse_albedo_of returns exactly black — the metallic workflow 6.4 derived, meeting the format that assumes it. Same story on conventions: handedness, winding and texture origin all agree, so gltf.cpp contains zero conversion code — no basis change, no index reversal, no 1 − v — and that is Module 2 having argued rather than guessed, since FBX is Z-up and Unity and Unreal are left-handed and any of those costs a mirroring basis change, which reverses winding, which needs a second correction. The trap runs the other way: flip_uv_v defaults to true and is correct for every mesh this engine has ever loaded and wrong for every one it loads next. Then the lesson settles a debt: 6.4’s ⚠ VERIFY, compared term by term against Khronos Appendix B. Five of six terms are identical, and the metallic blend is not even a difference — the spec lerps two whole BRDFs and this engine lerps the F0, and those commute exactly because Schlick is affine in f0, measured at 1.19e−07 over 4,851 points. Only the diffuse coupling differs, one Fresnel crossing against two, and the consequence is 1.036× at normal incidence and 5.62× at 88° — which is exactly why it hid for two lessons. The spec then answers its own question: BRDF implementations MAY vary, and a physically accurate one MUST be energy conserving — which ours is at 0.9255 and the spec’s sample form is not at 1.3395. On the architecture side, textures and materials become assets: a texture is derived from its image, so unloading the image takes it (2 released, and the name entry too, or find_texture hands out a recycled slot); and to_texture turns out to be a bridge that should have existed since 5.3 and did not, because every CPU texture was generated and every loaded image went straight to the GPU — two complete halves with no middle and no test that could see the gap. The one conformance gap is reported rather than hidden: glTF multiplies a base colour factor by its texture and this engine’s image replaces the tint (3.9), so a coloured factor plus a texture is counted and logged at the only point that knows both halves. 58 checks, and the golden byte-identical for the fifteenth lesson — a whole second asset format, and not one pixel moved.5 h
6.7Normal Mapping and the TBN Derivation publishedEvery surface this engine has drawn is exactly as flat as its triangles, and the obvious fix is unaffordable: shading has consulted the geometry’s normal since 3.6, so millimetre-scale detail means millimetre-scale triangles — tens of millions for one prop, every one smaller than a pixel. So lie about the normal instead. But a stored direction is meaningless without a frame, and the frame is the lesson. It arrives as two equations rather than a formula to copy: a triangle’s two edges describe one walk across the surface in METRES, the same two edges’ uv deltas describe that identical walk in TEXTURE UNITS, so T and B are simply whatever vectors make the two agree — two unknowns, solved by inverting a 2×2. The determinant turns out to be twice the signed area the triangle occupies in the chart, which is the same signed-area quantity 2.4 derived barycentric coordinates from, so zero is a real case rather than a degeneracy: an untextured face, a collapsed unwrap, and the face contributes nothing instead of an infinity. Then the question 6.6 had to defer, settled by one byte. 128 means 0.502 as DATA and 0.216 as a COLOUR, and read the wrong way “no tilt” becomes a tilt of 38.8° — in one direction, on every surface, and it does not look like a bug: the usual diagnosis is “this map was authored too strong” and the usual fix makes the picture less wrong without making it right. The colour space goes on the TEXTURE because that is where the hardware puts it — and the finding is that the GPU has had an srgb flag since 4.7 and the software renderer never had the concept at all: the same shape of gap 6.6 found between load_image and sample, one half complete and no code path crossing until a feature needed both. The tangent is a vec4, and the fourth component is not padding: every symmetric model has a mirrored uv chart, because an artist unwraps one arm and reflects it, and on that half the frame is left-handed — drop the sign and one side of the model is lit as the mirror image of the other, which reads as a modelling error. And a tangent is carried by the model matrix, not the inverse transpose, which is 3.6’s distinction arriving on its other side: a normal is defined by being PERPENDICULAR to the surface, and a tangent lies IN it, so it is a difference of positions and transforms the way positions do. Use the wrong one and the frame is skewed 36.9° — but both answers stay in the plane, so it reads as an asset authored at the wrong angle, and under a uniform scale the two agree to 8.4e−08. Three of the hardest bugs here share that shape: they produce a picture that is plausible, which is why each is a type or an assertion rather than a comment. The round trip is the test that catches all of them — a flat map must return the geometric normal, and a wrong colour space, decode range, orthonormality, handedness, multiply order or missing Gram-Schmidt each break it. It asserted zero and measured 5.55e−03, and the renderer was right: 0.5 is not an 8-bit code, so 128 decodes to 1/255 and every flat normal map in existence tilts its surface by 0.318°. Predicting that floor from the encoding is a strictly stronger test than the one that failed. Ported to both renderers in one commit; gpu_vertex_pnu 32 → 48 bytes, caught by 4.5’s static_assert, and an attribute-location collision with 4.6’s instancing caught by 4.5’s check_layout — which is why a vertex layout is now per-pipeline state. 33 checks; golden byte-identical for the sixteenth lesson.6 h
6.8Shadow Mapping: Bias, Acne, and PCF publishedEvery light this engine has ever had is unoccluded. lambert(n, l) asks whether a surface FACES the light — a fact about one surface’s orientation — where the question we want is whether it can SEE the light, which is a fact about that surface and every other object in the scene; shade()’s five arguments contain no way to discover that a wall is in the way, which is why every object in every picture so far floats. The fix is one sentence and the engine already owns it: render the scene from the light and keep only the depth — “nearest surface along every ray from a viewpoint” is not a new algorithm, it is a z-buffer, and on the CPU the whole pass is 3.1’s collect_triangles + draw_triangles with a different camera. A shadow map is not a new renderer; it is the renderer you already have, aimed somewhere else. What is genuinely hard is the comparison, and it gets the whole lesson. A directional light has no position, so there is no eye to hang a frustum on: what replaces the pyramid is a BOX, and orthographic projection is derived here from an interval remap. Its bottom row is (0, 0, 0, 1), so w comes out exactly 1 — and the lesson spends that fact three times: depth becomes affine so precision is uniform, the near plane may be NEGATIVE so the light’s eye can sit inside the scene, and a fragment’s light-space position can be recovered from 3.7’s interpolated world position for zero new varyings. Then the artefact, shown first: shadow acne, 36,786 pixels of moiré on a 480×270 frame, with not one line of code misbehaving. It is not a mystery. The map stores ONE depth per texel and the fragment is somewhere else inside it, so half of every texel’s footprint is downhill of its own sample — predicted 50%, measured 50.1%. The magnitude follows from the same picture: lateral travel × tan θ ÷ depth range, and the measured worst error reaches 92% of that bound, which is what makes it a derivation rather than a safe over-estimate. A constant bias must then peter-pan, exactly: it unshadows everything whose caster is within b × depth_range, which bites hardest where a caster TOUCHES its receiver — the one cue shadows were added for. Normal-offset bias needs the GEOMETRIC normal, and this is the first line in the engine that must tell it from 6.7’s shading normal: acne is a disagreement about where the triangles are, and a normal map does not move a triangle. PCF arrives with two numbers that settle its order — occluders at 0.3 and 0.9 average to a depth of 0.6, which reports a receiver at 0.5 fully lit, where comparing first gives 0 and 1 and a mean of 0.5 — which is the entire reason SamplerComparisonState exists. And the finding this lesson made by rendering: a 3×3 kernel reaches 2.12 texel-diagonals, not 0.71, so a correct bias stops being correct the moment PCF is switched on, and the acne returns looking like a filtering bug (79.6% on a controlled plane). The GPU port is a second render pass with num_color_targets = 0, a depth texture that can be sampled, and the third identity-element fallback in this class: 1.0 is the identity for a depth comparison. 53 checks; golden byte-identical for the seventeenth lesson.7 h
