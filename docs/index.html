1The two-stage spine

The central bet of this course is that you should learn the graphics pipeline twice. First on the CPU, where you own every pixel and nothing is hidden. Then on the GPU, where every concept you meet is one you have already built by hand.

By the time SDL_GPU asks you to fill in a depth-stencil state, you will have written the depth test yourself and will know exactly what you are configuring. That is the whole point: recognition, not incantation.

2Who this is for

  • You can program in some language. C++ specifics — RAII, ownership and move semantics, references vs pointers, templates, const-correctness, headers and translation units — are taught in place, the first time each appears, never as a forward reference to a chapter you have not read.
  • No assumed background in graphics, linear algebra, or calculus. All maths is built from zero, geometrically. Perspective projection arrives via similar triangles before any matrix. Quaternions arrive via complex numbers in 2D. Barycentric coordinates arrive via signed areas.
  • A desktop or laptop — Windows, Linux, or macOS — and roughly 300–500 hours.

Exit profile. By the final lesson you can implement rendering techniques directly from papers and blog posts, debug GPU work in RenderDoc, reason about frame budgets and cache behaviour, design and defend engine architecture, and read real engine codebases without drowning. In short: hireable as a junior-to-mid engine or graphics programmer.

3How the course works

Every lesson follows the same shape, and ends with something visible on screen or measurably changed:

  1. The Problem — what is broken, missing, or impossible without today's idea.
  2. Building Intuition — pictures and analogies, before any formalism.
  3. The Theory — derivations, each followed by a worked example with real numbers.
  4. Implementation — short steps, narrated, every decision justified as it is made.
  5. Complete listings — every file touched, in full, with zero placeholders.
  6. Build & run, pitfalls, exercises, recap, further reading.

Three living pages

Conventions Handedness, matrices, NDC and depth, winding, units, colour. Read before Module 2. Convention mismatches are the number-one source of silent rendering bugs.
Math Toolbox A cumulative appendix of every mathematical tool, added the moment the engine needs it.
C++ Style The rules the codebase obeys — naming, headers, ownership, error handling.
No build step — for the lessons

Every lesson is a plain HTML file. No server, no npm, no tooling: open it and read, even offline. Styling comes from one shared stylesheet the pages link, so keep the docs/ folder together — a lesson copied out on its own will look unstyled. The C++ obviously builds with CMake — that is the whole point — but the tutorial itself never asks you to build anything to read it.

What we build by hand, and what we do not

By hand — because building it is the pointThird-party — with a stated reason each time
Maths library (vec2/3/4, mat3/4, quaternions — no GLM), software rasterizer, OBJ parser, ECS, renderer and frame architecture, scene and asset systems, arena/pool allocators, collision and rigid-body basics. stb_image (image decoding), stb_truetype (font rasterization), Dear ImGui (debug UI and editor tooling only — never gameplay UI), cgltf (glTF, and only after the hand-rolled OBJ loader), SDL_shadercross.

Explicitly forbidden: GLM, assimp, bgfx/sokol, Bullet/PhysX/Jolt, EnTT/flecs — or any framework that would do the course's job for it.

Module 1 The Loop, the Pixel, and Time CPU complete 8 lessons · ~32 h

The heartbeat of every engine: events in, simulation forward, pixels out. We derive the fixed-timestep loop properly — accumulator and all — rather than pasting the folklore version, and we claim our first pixels.

1.1Events, Properly publishedSDL_Event as a tagged union (measured in memory), the event taxonomy by numeric category, and the arithmetic proving why draining one event per frame produces compounding input lag.3 h
1.2Input: State vs Events publishedLevels versus edges, both derived from one snapshot so they cannot disagree; why movement must use scancodes, not keycodes; and the inputs that cannot be polled at all.3 h
1.3Frames, Delta Time, and Why Naive Loops Lie publishedMonotonic clocks, nanoseconds, and integer time. Why dt-scaling is exact for constant velocity and only first-order for everything else — so the frame rate is still an input to your physics.4 h
1.4The Fixed Timestep with Interpolation, Derived publishedThe accumulator built from the problem up, through three broken attempts. Why the fixed step judders, how interpolation trades a lag that swings 0–h for one that is always h, and the arithmetic of the spiral of death.5 h
1.5The Framebuffer: Your First Owned Pixels publishedMemory as an image: index = y × width + x and the candy-stripe it causes when broken, what 32 bits of colour really are, streaming-texture presentation with pitch, and two ways to write a pixel — measured.4 h
1.6Colour, and an Honest Teaser of sRGB publishedThe stored value 128 emits 21.6% of white's light, and half the light is stored as 188. Which operations that breaks, why alpha is exempt, and what a real linear pipeline would cost.4 h
1.72D Vectors, Geometrically publishedArrows before components, and the dot product derived as a shadow — ax·bx + ay·by falls out of projecting onto the axes. Closes the 41.4% diagonal bug from 1.2.4 h
1.8Checkpoint: Pong publishedSeven lessons of parts become a game — and the assembly exposes a bug no demo could: a ball that tunnels through a paddle. Reflection derived from projection, collision asked about intervals instead of instants, and the exact speed at which a discrete test stops being valid.5 h

Milestone: a playable game with a correct fixed-timestep loop, drawn entirely into pixels you own.

Module 2 Software Rasterizer I: From Vertices to Screen CPU 12 lessons · ~54 h

The heart of the course. We build the entire 3D transformation pipeline from scratch and derive every matrix in it — including perspective projection, from similar triangles, before any 4×4 grid of numbers appears.

2.1Lines: DDA, then Bresenham publishedTwo algorithms derived from the pixel grid up — then measured, and the famous reason for preferring the second turns out to have expired. DDA is 2.2× faster on a 2026 CPU; we ship Bresenham anyway, for exactness, with the number written down.4 h
2.2The Triangle: Edge Functions publishedA triangle is three half-planes intersected — so "inside" is three independent yes/no questions, which is exactly why this and not scanline filling is what parallelises. Derives the edge function from 1.7's dot product, makes it incremental (75× measured), and lands the fill rule that closes 2.1's asymmetry.5 h
2.3Barycentric Coordinates from Signed Areas publishedDerived from the areas the edge functions already compute, not handed down. The defining property is that the weights reconstruct the point — which catches the vertex-pairing bug that “they sum to 1” cheerfully passes.5 h
2.4Interpolating Attributes Across a Triangle publishedThe weights finally carry something — and the formula is the easy part. Two of the three things that must go right are silent when they do not: the fill rule’s bias, still in the accumulators, displaces the whole attribute field by 1/‖e‖ pixels; and averaging stored colour values instead of light puts the centre of an R/G/B triangle at 85 where it belongs at 156.5 h
2.5Matrices as Basis Transforms publishedA matrix is where the basis vectors land — ask that one question and the multiplication rule, the rotation matrix and the determinant all fall out rather than needing to be memorised. The determinant turns out to be edge_function with its first point at the origin, and we check it by counting pixels.5 h
2.6Building mat4 by Hand publishedThe same question in 3-D, so nothing needs re-deriving — plus the three axis rotations built from the cycle x → y → z, which is what puts Ry’s minus sign where it actually belongs. Then the 4×4, whose fourth column moves a cube by exactly zero pixels. That is where the lesson stops.4 h
2.7Homogeneous Coordinates and What w Really Means publishedThe fourth component gets a job — and not one line of arithmetic changes. w = 1 is a position and gets translated; w = 0 is a direction and must not be. The same number, allowed to be anything else, turns out to be the mechanism behind perspective.4 h
2.8The Space Chain: Model to World publishedA coordinate is three floats and a room — and naming the room stops an entire class of bug. The model matrix is T · R · S, the order derived from what each transform means, and both wrong orders shown on screen: one deforms, one misplaces. One mesh becomes three objects in a visible world.4 h
2.9The View Matrix: Deriving Look-At publishedA camera is just an object with a transform, and the view matrix is the inverse of its placement — so moving the camera left is moving the world right. Introduces the cross product where it is first needed, inverts a rigid transform for free, and flies an orbit camera around the scene.5 h
2.10Perspective from Similar Triangles publishedThe keystone. Perspective is one divide by depth, derived from similar triangles before any matrix — then packaged as the projection matrix that writes -z into w. Where w finally stops being 1, the perspective divide gets its name, and depth's non-linearity is made a number. A [P] toggle flips perspective/orthographic on screen.6 h
2.11The Viewport Transform publishedThe last hop: NDC to framebuffer pixels. Three affine maps and one flip — the +y-up-to-+y-down sign that has drifted through the course since Lesson 2.5, finally derived and given one home in an engine::viewport that mirrors SDL_GPUViewport. The coordinate chain is now complete.3 h
2.12Milestone: A Spinning Wireframe Mesh publishedModule 2 complete. Indexed geometry (vertices + triangle indices — what the GPU wants), a regular icosahedron derived from three golden rectangles with φ forced by regularity, mesh validation via Euler's formula and winding, and the whole chain lit end to end. Plus an honest inventory of everything still missing, each handed to the Module 3 lesson that fixes it.4 h

Milestone: wireframe 3D meshes spinning on screen, with no library doing any of the maths.

Module 3 Software Rasterizer II: Depth, Light, Texture CPU in progress 10 lessons · ~46 h

We turn a wireframe into a picture. Each lesson opens with the artifact you get without the idea — overdraw chaos, swimming textures, faces punched inside-out — because a bug you have seen is a concept you remember.

3.1The Painter's Problem and the Z-Buffer publishedSorting cannot be repaired, and three independent proofs are built rather than asserted: a triangle has no single depth, overlaps can form a cycle, and intersecting triangles change the answer halfway across. Then one float per pixel — and the derivation that says which depth to store, because device depth is exactly affine in screen space and view-space depth is a hyperbola that reads 50.5 where the truth is 1.98. Ends with a precision budget you can compute before you see the z-fighting.5 h
3.2Perspective-Correct Interpolation publishedA checkerboard on a floor buckles, and kinks along a diagonal that exists nowhere in the geometry — the signature of an entire console generation. The fix is three lines, and it follows from the bracket 3.1 already proved: 1/w is affine in screen space, so a/w is too. Interpolate both, divide at the end. Ends by measuring the escape route people took instead: subdivision converges quadratically and never arrives.5 h
3.3Near-Plane Clipping publishedWalk into the floor and it vanishes; delete the guard that makes it vanish and it detonates across the screen. Both are one bug: the perspective divide has a precondition, not a guard, and it must be enforced a stage earlier. Derives the near plane as z_clip = 0 from the projection’s own depth row, shows why w ≥ 0 is a different plane that admits coordinates 691,714 pixels wide, and implements Sutherland–Hodgman for polygons and segments. The camera can now move through the world.5 h
3.4Back-Face Culling publishedOne comparison — the sign of a signed area the rasterizer was already computing and then deliberately throwing away. Derives which sign from the viewport’s y-flip rather than finding it by trial; shows that dot(n, camera_forward) is a different question that misjudges 15% of triangles at our field of view and a third at 120°; and settles Lesson 3.1’s 29-pixel debt exactly as predicted. Two folk facts get measured and corrected on the way.3 h
3.5A Hand-Rolled OBJ Loader publishedParsing is an afternoon; the lesson is the sentence hiding in f 1/1/1. A file gives every face corner three independent indices and a vertex buffer has one, so a cube’s 8 positions become 24 vertices — the first time this course reshapes data to suit the hardware, which is what an asset pipeline is. Then the debts: you cannot assume a loaded mesh’s winding or its watertightness, so both get measured — Euler characteristic (0 for the torus, not 2), boundary and non-manifold edges, and signed volume by the divergence theorem. Round-tripped through our own writer: 0 pixels different.6 h
3.6Normals and Lambert’s Cosine Law publishedWhy cos θ — from a torch tilted at a table, where the beam does not dim but spreads over a footprint 1/cos θ wider. Then the result that catches everyone: a normal is not transformed by the model matrix. Derived from the one property that defines a normal — perpendicular to every tangent — the answer is the inverse transpose, and the naive version tilts a normal by up to 68° and gets 97.5% of a squashed object’s pixels wrong. It is also invisible under rotation and uniform scale, which is exactly why it ships.5 h
3.7Specular and Blinn-Phong publishedThe first shading term that changes when you move your head — and 3.6 had measured that nothing else did. The mirror direction from 1.8’s bounce with the arrow reversed; the halfway vector derived as exactly the normal a microfacet would need, which is the doorway to Module 6. Then the parts everyone repeats without checking: Blinn’s exponent must be ~4× Phong’s (fitted: 4.38× falling to 4.01×), and Phong’s cut-off is not the mirror ray dipping below the surface — it never does. Measured on a plane: at 35° sun elevation Phong highlights 0 of 30,806 lit pixels and Blinn all of them. Ends by showing, not claiming, that per-vertex is the wrong home for a highlight: cube.obj has none at all in 157 of 180 frames of a spin.5 h
3.8Flat, Gouraud and Per-Pixel Shading publishedLesson 3.6 shipped one enum holding two questions, and its own comment said so. They separate here: where a normal comes from is a property of the mesh, where the equation is evaluated is a property of the pipeline, and the 2×3 grid they span has cells that provably coincide — with a face normal all three evaluation points agree to 0 pixels, for exactly as long as the shading is view-independent. Gouraud turns out to be a name for what 2.4 and 3.6 already built. Mach bands turn out to be in your eye rather than the framebuffer. And the folklore about cost is wrong below three pixels per triangle, where per-pixel is measured at 0.91× — cheaper — rising to 2.15× at 4K.5 h
3.9Texture Mapping and Bilinear FilteringFrom UVs to texels, and why nearest-neighbour sparkles. Bilinear as a weighted average you can compute by hand.5 h
3.10Profiling: Measure, Don't GuessOur first profiling pass. Your intuition about the bottleneck will be wrong — mine was too. Capstone: a textured, lit, z-buffered scene, all on the CPU.5 h

Milestone: a textured, lit, depth-buffered 3D scene rendered entirely on the CPU — every stage written by you.

5What "done" means

The course is not finished until all of the following exist and work. This list is a contract, not an aspiration:

  • A documented public C++ API.
  • An SDL_GPU forward PBR renderer with shadow-mapped lights, HDR, tonemapping, and a post-processing stack.
  • Skybox and image-based lighting.
  • An asset pipeline covering images, OBJ, and glTF.
  • A handle-based resource system.
  • A from-scratch ECS runtime with transform hierarchy.
  • Skeletal animation.
  • Rigid-body collision and response.
  • 3D audio, and input mapping.
  • ImGui tooling and an editor — hierarchy, inspector, gizmos.
  • Profiling hooks, serialization and a scene format, hot reload, and a job system.
  • Cross-platform CMake builds, sample demos, and an HTML documentation site.
  • A capstone game built solely on the public API.
