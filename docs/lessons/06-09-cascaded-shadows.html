Module 6 — Advanced Rendering · Lesson 6.9

Cascaded Shadow Maps

Lesson 6.8 fitted one orthographic box around the whole scene and rendered one depth map into it. That box has to contain the far field, so the near field — the part you are actually looking at — gets whatever texels are left over. The fix is a change of question: the scene is not what needs resolving, the camera's frustum is, and a frustum is mostly far away. Split it by distance, fit a box around each slice, give each its own map. What makes this a lesson rather than a loop is that a camera-fitted box moves, and 6.8's never did — so this lesson is mostly about two artefacts that only exist once it does, and about auditing whether 6.8's bias was really a formula or a fudge with a good story.

1The problem: one box, and where its texels go

Lesson 6.8 ended with shadows in both renderers and a bias derived from the sampling geometry rather than tuned by hand. It also ended with a line in shadow.hpp naming what it had not done, and this is the one we are here to fix:

One cascade. A single map stretched over a whole outdoor scene gives the near field far too few texels. Splitting the camera frustum into ranges and giving each its own map is cascaded shadow mapping, and it is an extension of this file rather than a replacement for it.

Take that seriously as arithmetic. fit_directional squares a box around the whole scene, so a 40-metre scene at 1024 texels gives

world_per_texel = 40 m ÷ 1024 ≈ 0.039 m

— and it gives that everywhere. The rock forty metres away that covers nine pixels of your screen gets 39-millimetre texels. So does the crate two metres from your eye that fills half the frame. The crate needs perhaps a hundred times the density and gets exactly the same, because the box was fitted to the thing that does not matter.

You can see this without any new code. gltf_view takes a resolution, so starve it deliberately — §6 has the full command — and watch the shadow edge under a nearby object turn into a staircase while the distant one looks fine. That is not a bug in 6.8's fit. The fit is doing exactly what it was asked: contain the scene. The question was wrong.

What "resolution" means for a shadow, and why it is not the map's size

It is tempting to answer this by making the map bigger. Doubling a 1024 map to 2048 costs four times the memory and four times the depth-pass fill, and buys a factor of two in world_per_texel — which the crate will spend in the first metre it moves closer. The quantity that matters is not texels, it is texels per screen pixel, and that is a ratio between the map's density and the camera's — so it is a fact about where the box is at least as much as about how many texels are in it. Cascades attack the numerator's placement. Raising the resolution attacks the denominator with money.

2Building intuition: resolve what you are looking at

Here is the whole idea in one sentence, and it is worth reading twice because everything difficult in this lesson is a consequence of it rather than part of it:

Fit the light's box around a slice of the camera's frustum instead of around the scene, and use several slices.

A frustum is a wedge. Near the eye it is small — a few metres across — and far away it is enormous. If you cut it into four pieces by distance and fit a box around each piece separately, the first box is small and its map is dense, and the last box is huge and its map is coarse. Coarse is the right answer at forty metres: a texel out there already covers a fraction of a screen pixel, and spending more on it would be spending it where nobody is looking.

Notice what this costs before we go further, because it is not free and the lesson should not pretend otherwise. Four cascades means four depth passes, four times the map memory, and a shader that has to decide which map to read. The demo below reports 3.22 ms for four cascades against 2.49 ms for one, on the same scene — so roughly 30% more time for four times the passes, because the depth pass is dominated by fixed costs rather than by triangles at this scale. Your scene will differ; measure it.

The three things that only exist once the box moves

6.8's box was fitted to the scene. Walk the camera around and the scene does not move, so the box does not move, so the map's texel grid is nailed to the world and stays there. That is why 6.8 could get away without a single line about stability, and it said so: "no texel snapping — the fit below depends on the SCENE's bounds and not the camera's, so the grid does not move when the camera does."

A camera-fitted box moves every frame. Three things follow, and together they are the lesson:

  1. A box that resizes makes every texel resize. Fit a box tightly to the eight corners of a slice and then turn the camera on the spot: the corners rotate within the box, so the box grows and shrinks — measured below at 30.2% across a full turn. Since world_per_texel is the box side over the resolution, every texel in the map changes size while you merely looked around, and every shadow edge in the picture breathes. The cure is to fit a sphere, which has no orientation.
  2. A box that slides makes every edge crawl. Even with a fixed size, the box translates as you walk, so its texel grid slides continuously under stationary geometry and each shadow edge lands on a different texel every frame. The cure is to snap the box to its own grid, so it advances in whole-texel jumps and lands where it landed last frame.
  3. Two cascades meeting is a new artefact with a new name. They have different texel grids and different biases, so they disagree along their shared distance, and that disagreement draws a straight line across your picture. It is called the seam, and the usual cure is to blend a band either side.
The claim this lesson is really testing

Lesson 6.8 spent its longest section deriving a bias from the sampling geometry — the argument being that shadow acne is not a mystery but a sampling error with a computable magnitude, and every term in that derivation was a multiple of world_per_texel.

Cascades change world_per_texel, by a factor of 7.3 across four of them. So this lesson is an audit. If 6.8's derivation was real, cascades need no new bias code at all — only their own light_camera. If it was a fudge with a good story attached, this is where it falls over and starts needing a per-cascade constant somebody tuned. §3.4 reports which of those happened, and the answer is more interesting than either.

3The theory

Four things to derive, in the order the code needs them: where to cut the frustum, what shape to fit around a cut, how to stop that shape sliding, and what any of it does to the bias.

3.1  Where to cut: two schemes that are both wrong

We need n distances between the near plane and wherever we stop caring. There are two obvious answers and neither survives contact with a picture, which is a good sign that the right answer is between them.

Uniform splits put the cuts at equal world distances:

di = near + (far − near) · i/n

With near = 0.1 m, far = 60 m and n = 4 that is 15.08, 30.05, 45.03 and 60 metres. Every slice is the same depth. This sounds fair and is useless: the near slice — the one filling your screen — now covers fifteen metres, which is most of what you can see, so cascade 0 is barely denser than the single map we are trying to beat.

Logarithmic splits come from an actual argument. A surface at distance d covers screen pixels in proportion to 1/d. To hold texels per screen pixel constant — which is the quantity §1 identified as the one that matters — the slice depths must grow in proportion to d. Something whose growth is proportional to its own size is a geometric series:

\[ d_i \;=\; near \cdot \left(\frac{far}{near}\right)^{i/n} \]
d_i = near · (far/near)^(i/n)
You have met this 1/d before

Lesson 4.7 spent its precision section on the fact that a perspective depth buffer spends its codes in proportion to 1/d, so the near field gets a hundred times the depth resolution of the far field. That was the same geometry arriving as a problem. Here it arrives as a prescription: if screen density falls as 1/d, then equal density means slices whose size rises with d. One fact, met twice, from opposite sides.

Now push the numbers through, which is where the logarithmic scheme falls over. With the same near = 0.1 and far = 60:

d0 = 0.1 · (600)1/4 = 0.1 · 4.95 = 0.55 m

Cascade 0 covers from 0.1 m to 0.55 m. That is your own feet. Three quarters of your shadow budget is spent before you reach the far end of a dining table, and the interesting middle distance — where things you can actually interact with are — is crammed into the last cascade. The scheme is theoretically right and practically miserable, and the reason is that the near plane is an arbitrary number chosen for depth precision, not a statement about where you are looking.

The practical scheme (Zhang et al., 2006) is the blend:

\[ d_i \;=\; \lambda \cdot \underbrace{near\left(\tfrac{far}{near}\right)^{i/n}}_{\text{logarithmic}} \;+\; (1-\lambda)\cdot \underbrace{\left(near + (far-near)\tfrac{i}{n}\right)}_{\text{uniform}} \]
d_i = lambda · logarithmic_i + (1 − lambda) · uniform_i

At λ = 0.5 the cuts land at 7.78, 16.25, 28.57 and 60 metres. Cascade 0 now covers the first eight metres — the room you are standing in — and the far cascade absorbs everything beyond thirty. λ is the one number in this lesson chosen by eye rather than derived, and that is worth stating plainly. What makes it defensible is that it interpolates between two schemes that are each explicable: it is not a magic curve, it is a dial between "equal metres" and "equal density", and 0.5 says you believe both arguments about equally.

3.2  The slice: eight corners from three numbers

To fit a box around a slice we need the slice's eight world-space corners. That is an inverse projection — and we do not have one. mat4 has no general inverse(): Lesson 2.9 gave us rigid_inverse, which handles a view matrix because a view matrix is a rotation and a translation, and nothing in this engine inverts a perspective matrix.

We could write it. A perspective matrix's inverse is closed-form and about six lines. But there is a better route, and it is the one most engines take: the frustum's shape is three numbers, so build the corners directly from them. At distance d, the frustum's half-height is

half_height = tan(fovy / 2) · d,    half_width = half_height · aspect

which is not a re-derivation of anything — it is the definition of a vertical field of view, and it is the same tangent perspective() is built from in mat4.hpp. Going through a matrix to recover numbers we already have would be a round trip that can only lose precision.

Worked example. fovy = 60°, aspect = 16/9, and the near plane of a slice at d = 1 m.

tan(30°) = 0.57735
half_height = 0.57735 × 1 = 0.57735 m
half_width = 0.57735 × 1.7778 = 1.02640 m

So in view space the four near corners are (±1.02640, ±0.57735, −1). The harness prints corner 0 as (−1.0264, −0.5774, −1.0000), which is the prediction to five figures.

And the independent check. Agreeing with my own formula proves nothing, so §B pushes a far corner through the actual perspective() matrix and asks where it lands. It comes out at (1.0000, 1.0000, 1.0000) — exactly the corner of the NDC box, which is what a frustum corner means. That is the test that makes §3.2 a check of the corner builder rather than a restatement of it.

−z is forward

A corner at distance d sits at z = −d (conventions §2). Write +d and you have built the frustum behind the camera — and the symptom is not a crash or a blank screen. It is a shadow map that is correct exactly when you turn around, which is one of those bugs that gets diagnosed as "the light direction is wrong" and fixed by negating something unrelated.

3.3  Fitting: a sphere, and what it costs

Now fit the light's orthographic box around those eight corners. The obvious thing is to transform them into light space and take their axis-aligned bounds. Do that and the picture boils.

Here is why, and it is worth being precise because the mechanism is not obvious. Turn the camera on the spot, changing nothing else. The eight corners sweep around, and an axis-aligned box in light space that contains them has to grow to hold the diagonal and shrink again as they come back into alignment. The harness yaws a camera through 360° and measures the box side varying by 30.2% — from 24.6 m to 35.3 m.

Now recall what that side is:

world_per_texel = box_side ÷ resolution

A box side that changes by 30% is a world_per_texel that changes by 30%, which means every texel in the map changes size, which means every shadow edge in the picture moves, while the camera did nothing but turn. That is the shimmer, and no amount of filtering hides it because it is not noise — it is the whole map breathing in and out.

A sphere has no orientation. Take the slice's eight corners, average them for a centre, take the largest distance from that centre for a radius, and make the box the square that contains the sphere. Rotating the camera rotates the corners within their own sphere and the radius does not care. The harness measures the spread across the same 360° yaw as 9.9 × 10−8 — which is float rounding, not geometry.

Be honest about the price

A sphere that contains a box is bigger than the box. The harness measures both on the same slice: the corner-fitted box would give 0.01508 m per texel and the sphere gives 0.01945 m — so stability costs 29% of the resolution.

That is a real trade and it is worth knowing which side you are on. If your camera is on rails and never rotates, fit the box and keep the 29%. Almost nobody is, so almost everybody pays it — but pay it deliberately, and if a reader works the frustum's dimensions out by hand and expects a bigger win than §1's numbers show, this is where the difference went.

3.4  Snapping: the grid that jumps

The sphere fixed the box's size. Its position still slides continuously as you walk, and a texel grid sliding under stationary geometry is exactly as bad: each shadow edge is quantised to whichever texel it happens to land in, so it jitters back and forth by a texel every frame. Edges crawl.

The cure is to make the grid land in the same place every frame: round the box's centre, in light space, down to a whole number of texels.

\[ centre'_{x} = \left\lfloor \frac{centre_x}{world\_per\_texel} \right\rfloor \cdot world\_per\_texel \]
centre'_x = floor(centre_x / world_per_texel) · world_per_texel

The box now advances in texel-sized jumps rather than sliding, so its grid lands on the same world positions it landed on last frame. A shadow edge either stays exactly where it was or moves exactly one texel — and the eye reads a one-texel step as stability where it reads a continuous slide as crawling.

The harness walks a camera forward in sub-texel steps and watches where a fixed world point falls on the grid. Snapped, it stays within 7.6 × 10−6 texels of the same position; unsnapped it wanders 0.499 texels, which is the worst it can possibly be — half a texel is the furthest anything can get from the nearest grid line.

Two ways to get this exactly wrong, and both compile

Snapping in the wrong space. The grid we are rounding to is the map's, and the map's axes are the light's. Round in world space and you round along axes that have nothing to do with the texel grid, which achieves precisely nothing while looking correct.

Anchoring the basis at the thing you are rounding. This one bit me while writing the lesson, and the test caught it. If you build the light's basis with look_at(centre, centre + fwd, up), then basis · centre is (0,0,0) by construction — the centre is the eye — so there is nothing left to round and snapping silently does nothing. The grid has to be nailed to the world, so the basis must be anchored at the world origin. The symptom was §E reporting an identical 0.499 texels with snapping both on and off, which is a much better error message than a picture that merely looks a bit crawly.

floor, not a cast

A cast to int truncates toward zero, so it rounds negative coordinates the opposite way from positive ones. Light space straddles the origin by construction, so half the world would snap one way and half the other, with a discontinuity running through the middle — which is roughly where the camera usually is.

3.5  The bias: the audit, and a surprise

Now the question this lesson exists to ask. Lesson 6.8 derived

\[ bias \;=\; \frac{reach \cdot world\_per\_texel \cdot \tan\theta}{depth\_range} \]
bias = reach · world_per_texel · tan(theta) / depth_range

and argued that every term was there for a reason. Cascades change world_per_texel by a factor of 7.3 and depth_range along with it. If that derivation was real, nothing needs to change. Here is what the harness reports:

No line of 6.8's bias code was touched. Each cascade owns a light_camera, light_camera is where world_per_texel and depth_range live, and shadow_map::visibility already read them from there. A cascade is a fit, not a feature. That is the audit passing.

But look at the fourth column, because it says something nobody set out to arrange. In world units the biases differ enormously — 0.0413 m for cascade 0 against 0.3005 m for cascade 3, a factor of 7.3, exactly as world_per_texel does. In device depth — the units the buffer actually stores and the comparison is actually performed in — cascades 1, 2 and 3 all want the same number, 2.031 × 10−3.

That is not a coincidence, and it falls out of the sphere fit in three lines:

\[ bias = \frac{reach \cdot wpt \cdot \tan\theta}{range}, \quad wpt = \frac{2r}{res}, \quad range \approx 2r \;\Longrightarrow\; bias \approx \frac{reach \cdot \tan\theta}{res} \]
bias = reach·wpt·tanθ / range,  wpt = 2r/res,  range ≈ 2r  ⟹  bias ≈ reach·tanθ / res

The radius cancels. Both the texel size and the depth range are set by the same sphere, so a sphere-fitted cascade's device-space bias depends on the resolution and the slope and on nothing else — not on which cascade it is, not on how far away it is. Putting the numbers in: reach = 2.1213 texels for a 3×3 kernel, tan θ = 1 for a 45° surface, resolution = 1024, giving 2.0716 × 10−3 against a measured 2.031 × 10−3 — agreement to 2%, and the 2% is the half-texel padding the fit adds.

The exception that confirms the mechanism

Cascade 0 does not follow the rule: it needs only 64% of cascade 1's bias. And the reason is exactly the one the derivation predicts. Its depth range is 31.9 m where its own sphere is only about 19.9 m across — because the range was stretched to reach the casters, not the slice (§3.6 below). The moment something other than the sphere sets the range, the cancellation is spoiled and the bias goes its own way.

An exception you can predict from the mechanism is worth more than a rule with no exceptions, because it is the difference between having a formula and having a coincidence.

3.6  The depth range belongs to the casters

One subtlety that is easy to miss and produces a memorable bug. The sphere tells us how wide the box is. It must not tell us how deep it is.

An occluder standing between the light and a slice is outside that slice — it is above it, or beside it — but it still casts into it. Fit the near plane to the slice and that occluder is clipped out of the depth pass, so it stops casting the moment it leaves the camera's view. The symptom is shadows blinking out at the edge of the screen, which reads as a culling bug and is not one.

So the near and far planes come from the casters' extent along the light's axis. The harness checks both halves of that: a 50-metre caster above a small slice stretches the depth range from 26.1 m to 62.3 m — and leaves world_per_texel unchanged at 0.024999, because a tall caster is a fact about depth, not about texel density.

3.7  The seam

Two cascades meet at a split distance. On one side the texels are 0.0384 m and the bias is one number; on the other they are 0.0670 m and it is another. Both are correct answers to slightly different questions, and they disagree — along a line, at a fixed distance from the camera, which is precisely the shape the eye is best at finding.

The usual cure is to fade. Pick a band at the end of each cascade, look up both cascades inside it, and interpolate. Since both answers are defensible, anything between them is too, and a ramp is invisible where a step is not. It costs a second lookup — but only for the fragments inside the band, which is a small fraction of the screen.

cascade_settings::blend_fraction is that band, as a fraction of each cascade's own depth. It defaults to 0, which shows the seam — Lesson 3.5's rule again: the failure has to be reachable, or the fix is folklore.

Selecting on the wrong depth bends the seam

The splits are distances along the view axis. A fragment shader knows a world position, and the tempting conversion is length(eye − world) — which is the radial distance, not the axial one. They differ by 1/cos(angle off axis), and at the corner of a 60° frame that is about 22%.

Select on radial distance and the corners of the screen land in the wrong cascade, so the seam — which should be a straight line at a fixed distance — bows into a curve that follows the edge of the frame. The fix is to carry the camera's forward axis and use dot(world − eye, forward): sixteen bytes to keep selection and fitting talking about the same quantity.

4Implementation

Six steps. The first four are the new file; the last two are what the rest of the engine had to give up to accommodate it, which is the more interesting half.

4.1  shadow_map learns to be handed a camera

6.8's render fits the box itself, from the same meshes it is about to draw. That was the right call then and it is exactly what a cascade cannot use, since a cascade's box comes from the camera. So split it: the old signature keeps its behaviour and delegates.

Everything else in that function moves into the new overload unchanged. This is worth doing as a delegation rather than a copy: the two must not drift, and the depth pass — Module 2's collect_triangles and Module 3's draw_triangles, still unmodified — is the part 6.8 made a point of not rewriting.

4.2  The split scheme, guarded

The early return for the last cut is not tidiness. Cascade n−1's far distance must be exactly far_d, because the next cascade's near distance is this one's far distance: leave a gap and there is a ring of world that no cascade covers and no shadow appears in; leave an overlap and two maps resolve the same metres. Computing it from the blend would land within a float ULP of far_d, which is not the same as being it.

4.3  The fit

The sphere, then the snap, then the depth range — in that order, because each depends on the one before. The whole function is in §5; here are the three pieces that carry the argument.

Eight points, one average, one max. That is the entire anti-shimmer mechanism from §3.3, and it is worth noticing how little code an idea can be once the idea is right.

z is deliberately not snapped. It is the depth axis, and its grid is the depth buffer's rather than the texel grid's — snapping it would quantise depth for no reason and interact with the bias in a way nobody wants to reason about.

4.4  A depth array, and why the single map became one too

On the GPU, four cascades want four maps. SDL_GPU has a natural home for that — a 2D array texture — and the field that makes one is layer_count_or_depth, a single field carrying two meanings (layers for an array, depth for a 3D texture) because SDL is being honest about a union it did not make one.

Then a decision that is really a decision about how many code paths we are willing to keep in step. HLSL's Texture2D and Texture2DArray are different binding types, so supporting both means two shaders, or one shader with a branch and two bindings. Instead, the shadow map is always an array, even at one layer — which makes 6.8's single map the degenerate case of 6.9's cascade set rather than a second thing to maintain.

A render pass writes one layer. SDL_GPUDepthStencilTargetInfo has a layer field — a Uint8, sitting beside mip_level, both answering "which slice of this texture is the target" — and no way to say "all of them". So four cascades are four passes. That is not a limitation to route around: each cascade has its own camera and its own culling, so they were always going to be separate passes.

⚠ VERIFY, discharged

The plan for this lesson carried a ⚠ VERIFY against the layer field's name, because guessing an SDL3 struct member is exactly the kind of confident fabrication a student would then type in. Checked against SDL3/SDL_gpu.h in the configured build tree: the field is layer, it is a Uint8, and it is declared immediately after mip_level at the end of the struct. SDL_GPU_TEXTURETYPE_2D_ARRAY is the second enumerator of SDL_GPUTextureType. Both confirmed, neither assumed.

4.5  The uniform block, and one field that earns its sixteen bytes

The shader needs, per cascade, a matrix, a split distance, a texel size and a depth range. That is a new per-frame block at fragment slot 2 — separate from the light block for the reason 6.7 gave when it refused to grow the per-draw block: pushes are billed by how often they happen, and keeping the three blocks separate is what lets the biggest one be pushed least often.

Note the shape of the scalar fields. Four floats that are logically an array are declared as one vec4, because HLSL packs float[4] into four separate 16-byte registers — sixteen bytes to carry four, forty-eight of them padding. Lesson 4.6 met that rule from the other side; here it is the difference between 336 bytes and 704.

And then view_forward, which exists entirely because of §3.7's trap:

4.6  The block that must be pushed even when it is not used

This one is a trap worth the paragraph. A cbuffer the shader declares and nobody fills does not read as zero. It reads as whatever was last in that slot — which is a garbage matrix and a cascade_count that could be anything, including a number that sends the shader off the end of its array.

The fallback is a deliberate identity: one cascade, splits past any reachable depth, and 6.8's single matrix copied into every slot. A scene with no shadows at all is still correct, because shadow_strength in the light block is what actually disables the lookup — the same nullable-pointer bargain the rest of the engine makes, with the difference that a GPU uniform slot has no null.

4.7  The shader

shadow_visibility gains a cascade index and reads its two per-cascade numbers from the new block. That is the entire change to the body of 6.8's function — the bias arithmetic below it is untouched, which is §3.5's audit stated as a diff.

Selection and blending go in a wrapper above it, so the sampling function stays a function of "which cascade" and the policy lives in one place:

cascade_pick is a four-way ternary rather than an array index, because indexing a float4 by a runtime value is not something HLSL will do without either a scalar array — which costs the 4× padding above — or a swizzle it cannot resolve at compile time. Four comparisons is the cheaper trade.

7Common pitfalls & debugging

The shadows are correct, but only when you face one particular way

Symptom. Shadows appear and are shaped correctly when the camera looks one direction, and vanish entirely when you turn around.

Cause. The slice corners were built at z = +d instead of −d, so the frustum was constructed behind the camera. −z is forward (conventions §2).

Fix. One sign in slice_corners. The tell is that it is exactly the opposite hemisphere rather than a partial failure.

Snapping is on and the edges still crawl

Symptom. snap = true changes nothing at all — not less crawling, none.

Cause. Almost certainly the basis anchoring described in §3.4: if the light's basis is built at the slice centre, the centre's light-space coordinate is (0,0,0) and there is nothing to round.

Fix. Anchor the basis at the world origin. And test it numerically rather than by eye — take a fixed world point, express it in texels, walk the camera in sub-texel steps and print the fractional part. Snapped it should be ~10−6; if it is 0.499 the snap is not happening.

Objects stop casting when they leave the screen

Symptom. A shadow disappears as its caster slides out of view, often popping back when you turn slightly.

Cause. The depth range was fitted to the slice instead of to the casters (§3.6). An occluder between the light and the slice is outside the slice.

Fix. Extend near and far to the casters' extent along the light axis. This reads like a culling bug and is not one, which is why it is worth recognising by its signature: the shadow vanishes when the caster leaves the frame, not when the receiver does.

The seam is a curve, not a line

Symptom. The cascade boundary is visible and bows outward toward the edges of the screen.

Cause. Selecting on radial distance (length(eye − world)) rather than axial depth (dot(world − eye, forward)). They differ by about 22% at the corner of a 60° frame (§3.7).

Fix. Carry the camera's forward axis into the shader and project onto it. A straight seam is the confirmation.

The shadow map is black on the GPU and fine on the CPU

Symptom. Everything is shadowed, or nothing is, on the GPU path only.

Cause. A Texture2D declared in HLSL against a texture created as SDL_GPU_TEXTURETYPE_2D_ARRAY. The validation layer catches it; without the layer it can silently sample nothing.

Fix. Texture2DArray<float>, and sample with a float3 whose z is the layer index. Turn the validation layer on before assuming it is a maths problem — 4.9's RenderDoc lesson is the other half of this.

Turning shadows off produces garbage instead of no shadows

Symptom. A scene with no shadow map renders with random dark blotches, and what they look like changes depending on what was drawn before.

Cause. The cascade cbuffer was not pushed, so the shader read whatever was last in that slot (§4.6).

Fix. Push it unconditionally, with an identity fallback. A uniform slot has no null.

8Exercises

8.1 — Find the crossover guided

§6 shows cascades losing on a 2.4-metre scene and §C shows them winning 2.8× on a 40-metre one. Somewhere between is a scene size where they break even. Find it: vary the scene extent in §C's setup, plot cascade 0's world_per_texel against the single map's, and report the crossover.

Hint

The single map's texel size grows linearly with scene extent; cascade 0's does not depend on the scene at all (except through the depth range). So one curve is a straight line and the other is flat — the crossover is where the line crosses it, and it should land near the point where the scene stops fitting inside cascade 0.

8.2 — Make λ visible guided

Run the demo at --csm-lambda 0, 0.5 and 1 and record the four split distances and four texel sizes each time. Which λ gives the best cascade 0, and what does it cost the others? Write down the trade in one sentence.

Solution sketch

λ = 1 gives the finest cascade 0 by far and an unusable cascade 3, because the far cascade absorbs everything the near ones did not. λ = 0 gives an almost useless cascade 0. The sentence is roughly: λ trades the near field's density against the far field's, and the right value depends on how much of your gameplay happens within arm's reach.

8.3 — Cache the far cascades engine challenge

Cascade 3 covers thirty metres and changes slowly; re-rendering it every frame is the single biggest waste in this implementation. Re-render cascade i only every 2i frames, and measure the saving. Then find the artefact you introduced.

Hint

Two traps. A cached cascade's matrix must be cached with it, or the shader will sample last frame's texels with this frame's transform. And a moving caster in a stale cascade drags its shadow behind it — which is a reason to invalidate on motion rather than purely on a frame counter.

8.4 — Visualise the cascade split guided

Add a debug mode that tints each cascade a different colour — cascade 0 red, 1 green, 2 blue, 3 white. It is four lines in the fragment shader and it turns every question in this lesson into something you can see, including whether your seam is straight.

8.5 — Break the cancellation open

§3.5 shows the device-space bias is ≈ reach·tanθ/resolution because the sphere radius cancels between world_per_texel and depth_range. Construct a scene where that cancellation fails for every cascade rather than just cascade 0, predict the resulting biases before you measure, then measure.

Hint

The cancellation needs depth_range ≈ 2r. Anything that makes the caster extent along the light axis dominate the sphere breaks it — a very tall scene, or a light close to horizontal so its axis runs the length of the world. Predict with reach·wpt·tanθ/range using the printed ranges.

9Recap & next

What the engine gained.

  • Cascaded shadow maps in both renderers — a practical split scheme, a sphere fit, texel snapping, per-cascade bias and an optional blend band.
  • A depth array texture, and with it the observation that 6.8's single map is the one-layer case rather than a separate path.
  • A stability argument that is measured rather than asserted: 30.2% box variation cured to 9.9 × 10−8; 0.499 texels of grid slide cured to 7.6 × 10−6.
  • An audit of Lesson 6.8 that it passed. Cascades change world_per_texel by 7.3× and not one line of the bias derivation had to move. The surprise was that the device-space bias barely moves either, because the sphere's radius cancels — with cascade 0 the exception that confirms the mechanism.
  • An honest measurement of when not to use this, which is the demo scene this course has been carrying since Module 6 opened.

What it still does not do. Every cascade is the same resolution; nothing is cached between frames; the light is still directional and still singular. Those are 8.3's territory and Module 9's.

Next — 6.10, Mipmaps, LOD, and Anisotropic Filtering. Lesson 3.9 named the shimmering floor and deferred the fix; 4.7 created every texture with num_levels = 1 and wrote "Module 6" in the comment. That debt comes due, and it has to come due before image-based lighting, because a prefiltered environment map is a mip chain indexed by roughness.

10Further reading

  • Zhang, Sun, Xu & Lu, "Parallel-Split Shadow Maps for Large-scale Virtual Environments" (2006) — the practical split scheme, and the 1/d argument for the logarithmic term stated properly.
  • Real-Time Rendering, 4th ed., §7.4 "Cascaded Shadow Maps" — the standard treatment, including the variants this lesson does not implement.
  • Microsoft, "Common Techniques to Improve Shadow Depth Maps" — still the clearest single account of cascade fitting, texel snapping and the bounding-sphere trick, with pictures of each artefact.
  • Eric Lengyel, Foundations of Game Engine Development, vol. 2 — the frustum-corner construction, done as geometry rather than as a matrix inverse.
  • SDL3, SDL_GPUTextureCreateInfo and SDL_GPUDepthStencilTargetInfo — layer_count_or_depth and layer, which are the two fields this lesson checked rather than guessed.
