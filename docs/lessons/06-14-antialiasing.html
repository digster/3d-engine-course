Module 6 — Advanced Rendering · Lesson 6.14

Antialiasing: Geometric and Shading

Two different problems share this name, and the cure everyone reaches for fixes exactly one of them. Geometric aliasing is the staircase on a silhouette — coverage, a step function with infinite bandwidth, answered yes or no. Shading aliasing is the sparkle on a polished curve, where what is undersampled is the lighting: a specular lobe narrower than the pixel looking for it. We can say exactly where the second begins, because the GGX lobe has a closed-form width — sin t = α·√((√2−1)/(1−α²)), which is 0.6436 α and not the α everyone quotes — and on a sphere 40 px in radius that crosses the normal's per-pixel variation at roughness 0.2468. The demo's 0.49 sits safely above it; every polished metal Lesson 6.12 introduced sits below. Then the measurement that reframes the whole subject: filtering the NDF improves per-pixel accuracy by 2.4× and at some roughnesses makes it worse, while improving stability by 2,034×. Antialiasing was never about getting a more correct pixel.

1The problem: one name, two failures

Render the demo with a polished material and two things look wrong. They look wrong in different places, they respond to different settings, and almost every treatment of antialiasing runs them together.

The first is the one everybody names: the silhouette is a staircase. A triangle's edge cuts across the pixel grid at some angle, each pixel is either inside or outside, and the boundary comes out as a flight of steps.

The second only appears once the camera moves: the highlight sparkles. Isolated pixels on a curved shiny surface flash on and off as the view pans, and they do it at a spatial frequency that has nothing to do with the geometry. Hold the camera still and the frame looks fine. That is the tell.

Why they are not the same problem

Both are aliasing — high-frequency content masquerading as low-frequency content, because the sample grid cannot tell them apart. But what is undersampled differs, and that decides which cures are even available:

geometricshading
what is undersampledcoverage — is this pixel inside the triangle? the lighting — how bright is this surface here?
where it appearssilhouettes and intersections anywhere the surface curves and is shiny
visible when still?yesbarely — it is a motion artefact
fixed by MSAAyesno, by construction

That last row is the reason this lesson separates them before building anything. MSAA is the default answer to "how do I antialias this", and it is a coverage technique: it evaluates coverage and depth at several positions inside each pixel while running the fragment shader once per primitive per pixel. That asymmetry is not a shortcut — it is what MSAA is, and it is why 4× MSAA costs about 1.3× rather than 4×.

You cannot buy shading samples with a coverage feature.

And we can say exactly where the second one starts

This is where the module's own arc pays off. Lesson 6.12 put a value of 55,917 in the frame buffer by polishing a metal; Lesson 6.13 measured what a display could do with it. The same polishing is what makes a surface sparkle, and by §3.4 we will have the crossover as a number rather than a feeling: on a sphere 40 pixels in radius, shading aliasing begins at roughness 0.2468.

The demo's own roughness is 0.49 — above it. Every polished material 6.12 introduced (0.20, 0.10, 0.05) is below it. The module has been walking toward this number for three lessons.

2Building intuition: a pixel is an area

Almost every bug in this lesson comes from one habit of thought, so it is worth attacking directly.

A pixel is not a point. It is a little square region of the image plane, and the value it should hold is the average of the image over that region. The rasterizer we built in Module 2 decides coverage by testing whether the pixel's centre is inside the triangle — one point sample standing in for an integral over an area.

That substitution is exact only when the thing being sampled does not vary over the pixel. When it does — and at a silhouette it varies from "entirely covered" to "entirely uncovered" — the point sample is not an approximation of the average. It is a different number that happens to share a location.

Why more samples is not the whole answer

The obvious fix is more sample points. It helps, and it is what supersampling does, but it is worth understanding why it cannot solve the problem before relying on it.

The sampling theorem says a signal can be reconstructed exactly from point samples only if it contains no frequencies above half the sample rate. Coverage is a step function — it jumps from 0 to 1 at the triangle's edge — and a step's Fourier transform has energy at every frequency, falling off only as 1/f. There is no sample rate high enough.

So no amount of supersampling makes a silhouette correct. What it does is push the error down and, crucially, make the error smooth: instead of a pixel that is fully black or fully white depending on which side of the edge its centre happens to fall, you get one that changes gradually as the edge moves across it.

Worked example. A checkerboard of period 0.37 pixels — far above the Nyquist limit. Its true local mean is exactly 0.5 whatever the phase, which is what makes it a ground truth rather than another rendering.

factor samples/px per-pixel RMS deviation worst pixel 1 1 0.5000 0.5000 2 4 0.1719 0.5000 4 16 0.0794 0.1240 8 64 0.0231 0.0333

At 1× every pixel is black or white — the deviation is a full 0.5, which is as wrong as it is possible to be. At 8× it is 0.0231. But the checker is never recovered, at any factor, because it is not representable on this grid. What changes is that the image stops claiming to be something it is not.

A measurement that measured nothing. The first version of that table reported the mean over the whole image, and found it already correct at 1×. Of course it did: across 4,096 pixels whose phases differ, errors of opposite sign cancel. The mean was blind to the thing it was pointed at. Aliasing is a per-pixel phenomenon, so the statistic has to be a per-pixel one — and a check that passes while measuring nothing is worse than no check at all.

Antialiasing does not recover detail the grid cannot hold. It removes the lie.

Hold on to that sentence — §4 turns it into a measurement, and the measurement is the most surprising result in this module.

3The theory

3.1 Supersampling, and what it costs

The direct implementation of "a pixel is an area" is to sample that area more densely: render at N times the linear resolution and average each N×N block down.

Note what changes and what does not. The camera does not move, the projection matrix is identical, the triangles are the same triangles. Only the viewport scales. That is what makes supersampling the honest baseline — it is the same rendering, sampled more densely — and it is also why it costs N²: every fragment is shaded.

3.2 The resolve, and a mistake making its fourth appearance

Averaging the samples is one line, and it is the line this course has now got wrong in four different contexts. framebuffer holds sRGB-encoded bytes. Averaging bytes averages codes, and codes are not quantities of light — the transfer function exists precisely because they are not.

Worked example. A pixel exactly half covered: two white sub-samples, two black.

the correct answer: half the light is 0.5 linear sRGB(0.5) = 1.055 × 0.5^(1/2.4) − 0.055 = 0.7354 × 255 = code 188 averaging bytes: (255 + 255 + 0 + 0) / 4 = code 127 which is a linear 0.2122

So the wrong resolve delivers 21.2% of the light instead of 50%. The symptom is specific and usually misdiagnosed: antialiased edges come out too dark, every silhouette grows a thin dark outline, and the usual guess is that edges are being composited with the background twice.

This is Lesson 6.1's rule arriving after 6.10's mip chains and 6.11's compositing, and it will keep arriving — because every new way of averaging is a new opportunity to average the wrong quantity.

3.3 How wide is a specular lobe?

Now the shading half, which needs one number nobody usually computes: the angular width of a GGX highlight.

The GGX normal distribution, from Lesson 6.3, is

\[ D(\theta) = \frac{\alpha^{2}}{\pi\left(\cos^{2}\theta\,(\alpha^{2}-1) + 1\right)^{2}} \]
D(t) = α² / (π (cos²t (α² − 1) + 1)²)

At t = 0 the bracket is α², so D(0) = 1/(πα²). Ask where it falls to half of that. Writing d for the bracket:

\[ \frac{D(\theta)}{D(0)} = \frac{\alpha^{4}}{d^{2}} = \frac{1}{2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad d = \sqrt{2}\,\alpha^{2} \]
α⁴ / d² = 1/2 → d = √2 α²

Substitute the definition of d and solve for the angle:

\[ \cos^{2}\theta = \frac{1 - \sqrt{2}\,\alpha^{2}}{1 - \alpha^{2}} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \sin\theta = \alpha\sqrt{\frac{\sqrt{2}-1}{1-\alpha^{2}}} \]
sin t = α √((√2 − 1) / (1 − α²))

Three lines, no fitting, and exact at every roughness rather than to leading order. For small α it reduces to

\[ \theta \approx \alpha\sqrt{\sqrt{2}-1} = 0.6436\,\alpha \]
t ≈ 0.6436 α

Which corrects a piece of folklore by 55%. "The lobe is about α wide" is the standard hand-wave. The coefficient is √(√2−1) = 0.6436, so the folklore overstates the width by a factor of 1.55. verify_614 §A checks the closed form against a bisection of the real NDF — two independent routes to one number — and they agree to 1.6 × 10⁻⁸ across roughness 0.02 to 0.90.

One more thing before we use it: this is the half-vector lobe. The lobe you see — the image of the light in the surface — is twice as wide, because rotating the half-vector by t rotates the mirror direction by 2t. Which of the two you want depends on whether you are asking about the surface or about its reflection.

3.4 Where the sparkle begins

A pixel does not see one normal. It sees every normal on the patch of surface it covers. So the question "is this highlight adequately sampled?" becomes:

Is the lobe wider than the range of normals inside one pixel?

Both sides of that are now computable. The left is §3.3. For the right, take a closed surface: a sphere's normal sweeps a full quarter turn from the centre of its silhouette to the rim, so if it is R pixels in radius, the average turn per pixel is (π/2)/R.

Worked example. A sphere 40 px in radius: the normal turns 0.039270 rad per pixel.

Bisecting for the crossover gives roughness 0.2468. At roughness 0.05 the lobe is twenty-four times narrower than the pixel's worth of normal variation — which is why the highlight does not merely shimmer, it appears and vanishes.

3.5 Why MSAA cannot reach it

Now the two halves meet. MSAA evaluates coverage and depth at several positions inside each pixel, and runs the fragment shader once per primitive per pixel, replicating that one result to every covered sample.

On a pixel that straddles a silhouette, that is exactly right and very cheap: the samples differ in whether they are covered, which is what MSAA measures. On a pixel in the interior of a surface, every sample belongs to the same primitive — so MSAA's answer is, identically, the single-sample answer.

Worked example. An interior pixel on a curved metal, shaded once (MSAA) and four times (SSAA), from verify_614 §F.

roughness MSAA (1 shade) SSAA (4 shades) ratio 0.50 5.99 5.97 1.003x 0.20 191.66 181.98 1.053x 0.10 406.28 1288.18 0.315x 0.05 52.02 15587.65 0.003x

At roughness 0.50 they agree to three parts in a thousand. At 0.05 they differ by a factor of 300. That gap is the shading aliasing, precisely, and no sample-count setting on an MSAA target touches it.

And a resolve does not rescue a firefly either. Lesson 6.13's 55,917, caught by one sample of four, resolves to 13,979 — still 2,192× above ACES's lid of 6.3774. Averaging in linear light divides by at most 4, and the lid is 8,769× below. That is 6.13's clamp argument arriving from a completely different direction, and it is why the two lessons need each other.

3.6 Filtering the distribution instead

If the problem is that the lobe is too narrow for the pixel, the fix is not more samples. It is to widen the lobe until it is no narrower than the normal variation the pixel has to integrate over.

That is the same move as a mip chain: prefilter the thing being undersampled, at the frequency the grid can actually represent. Here is the derivable core.

A pixel sees a distribution of surface normals, spread over its footprint with some variance σ². The reflectance it should report is therefore the surface's own NDF convolved with that spread. And convolution adds variances:

\[ \alpha'^{2} = \alpha^{2} + 2\sigma^{2} \]
α'² = α² + 2σ²

with σ² estimated from the normal's screen-space derivatives. On the GPU those are free — fragments are shaded in 2×2 quads (Lesson 4.1) precisely so a neighbour is always available to subtract, so ddx(n) and ddy(n) are one instruction each.

What is derived here and what is not. The convolution argument is real: a pixel integrates over a footprint, and convolving distributions adds their variances. The factor of 2, the screen-space width σ² = 1/2π, and the clamp κ = 0.18 come from Tokuyoshi and Kaplanyan's 2019 paper and are fits.

And one step deserves to be flagged rather than glossed: "α behaves as a standard deviation" is an approximation doing real work, because GGX's variance is in fact infinite — its tails are heavy enough that the second moment diverges. That is the same heavy tail Lesson 6.13 exploited to make bloom look like real glare. It is a good approximation here and it is not a theorem, so we measure whether it helps rather than assuming it.

The clamp is load-bearing rather than defensive. At a silhouette the normal sweeps most of a hemisphere inside one pixel, so without a ceiling a chrome bumper would be filtered to fully rough exactly at its own edge — the technique would produce a new artefact in the act of curing an old one.

4What antialiasing actually optimises

Here is the measurement this lesson exists for, and it did not come out the way I expected.

Take a curved metal, sweep the sample point across one pixel's worth of normal variation, and at each phase compare three numbers: the naive single sample, the same sample with a filtered NDF, and brute-force ground truth — the BRDF integrated over that pixel's own footprint with 256 samples.

roughnessfiltered tonaive RMS errorfiltered RMS error naive swingfiltered swing
0.300.30872.8%8.1%1.1×1.1×
0.200.225410.5%28.5%1.6×1.3×
0.100.181481.3%77.5%24.0×1.9×
0.050.1773230.1%94.7%3996.5×2.0×

Read the two right-hand columns against the two middle ones, because they disagree.

On accuracy, the filter is nearly useless. At roughness 0.05 it improves the per-pixel RMS error by 2.4× — and it still leaves 94.7%. At roughness 0.30 and 0.20 it makes the error three times worse.

On stability, it is transformative. The swing — the ratio between the brightest and dimmest value a pixel takes as the camera pans by less than one pixel — falls from 3,996.5× to 2.0×. That is a factor of 2,034.

Antialiasing does not make a pixel correct. It makes it stable.

And once stated, it is obvious in hindsight. The artefact was never "this pixel has the wrong value" — a still frame with a slightly wrong specular pixel looks fine, and nobody files a bug. The artefact is "this pixel changes violently when nothing in the scene did". Aliasing is not an error in magnitude; it is an error that is discontinuous in the parameters, so it converts smooth camera motion into flashing.

Which means the question to ask of any antialiasing technique is not "how accurate is it". It is:

What frequencies does it remove, and can the grid carry what is left?

Both halves of this lesson answer that question the same way. Supersampling removes the high-frequency content of coverage by integrating over the pixel's area. NDF filtering removes the high-frequency content of the lighting by widening the lobe. Both discard real information. Both are correct.

And this is 6.13's trade in a different currency. That lesson had more range than the display could carry, and spent area to encode it. This one has more detail than the sample grid can carry, and spends sharpness to buy stability. In both, the pixel grid is the thing that cannot be enlarged, and in both the fix is to convert the surplus into a currency the grid does have.

5Implementation

5.1 The lobe, where it belongs

ggx_lobe_half_angle goes in microfacet.hpp, not in the new antialiasing header, because it is a property of the distribution rather than of any technique that consults it. Lesson 6.15's prefiltered environment maps will want the same number for the same reason.

0.41421356 is √2 − 1, written out because HLSL has no <numbers> and the two implementations have to agree digit for digit — the same argument k_inv_pi made in 6.2. The clamp on a2 keeps 1 − α² away from zero at roughness 1.

5.2 The resolve

Decode every sample to light, average the light, encode once. That last word is the half of 6.1's rule people forget — and the wrong version is kept selectable behind encoded_average, for the same reason tonemap::clamp was kept in 6.12: a failure you can switch on is worth more than one you have to describe.

5.3 The filter

dot(d, d) and not length(d). The quantity that adds under convolution is the variance, not the standard deviation — which is the entire content of §3.6 and the easiest thing to lose by reaching for a length out of habit.

And filtered_roughness does a double round trip on purpose: roughness → alpha, filter, alpha → roughness. The filtering is a statement about the NDF's actual width; roughness is a remapping chosen in 6.3 to make a slider feel linear. Filtering the slider would be filtering the wrong quantity, and the error would grow with how nonlinear the remap is.

5.4 The same three lines, on the GPU

This is the one place in the course where the GPU has it easier than the CPU, and the reason is Lesson 4.1: fragments are shaded in 2×2 quads specifically so that a neighbouring fragment is always available to subtract, which is what makes ddx a single instruction. A scanline rasterizer has no neighbour, so a correct CPU version has to derive the gradients from the triangle — exactly the position Lesson 6.10 was in when it needed uv_gradients instead of ddx(uv).

The uniform cost zero bytes: specular_aa is the slot that was alpha_pad0, added by 6.11 to fill a register. That is the second time this block has absorbed a new flag for free, after 6.7 took the padding 6.4 left.

Why it is not a field of material. uniforms_of takes it as a separate argument rather than deriving it from the material, and that is a real distinction: whether to filter the NDF depends on how the surface is being viewed. The same sphere at 400 pixels across and at 4 pixels across has the same roughness and needs very different filtering. It is a render setting that happens to travel in the material's block.

6MSAA in SDL_GPU

6.1 Ask the device; do not assume

SDL_GPUTextureSupportsSampleCount exists because MSAA support is per format. On the machine this lesson was written on:

8× is simply unavailable here. A lesson that hard-coded it would be teaching a bug, and a program that refused to start without it would be worse behaviour than one that quietly runs at 4× and says so in the log — which is what create_colour_target does.

6.2 A multisample texture cannot be sampled

This is the fact that shapes everything else. You render into the multisample target and read from the single-sample target it resolves into, so an MSAA frame needs two colour targets where a plain one needs one — and gpu_post_stack grows a second accessor to keep them straight:

scene_target() — what the scene pass renders into (multisample, if MSAA is on) resolved_target() — what everything downstream reads (always single-sample)

Getting those backwards is a validation error on a debug device and undefined behaviour on a release one, which is why create_colour_target silently drops the SAMPLER usage when the sample count is above 1 rather than passing on a request the driver cannot honour.

6.3 The resolve, and two fields that must both be right

RESOLVE rather than RESOLVE_AND_STORE, and SDL3's own header says why: the first lets the driver discard the multisample memory afterwards and is "the most performant method", while the second additionally writes the per-sample contents out and is "not recommended". Nothing in this engine reads per-sample data, so keeping it would be paying bandwidth for nothing.

scene_target_info() exists so a caller cannot assemble that struct and forget the resolve. Omit either field and the frame renders perfectly into a texture nobody reads — which looks like the scene pass failing rather than the resolve missing, and is a genuinely miserable hour.

6.4 What it costs, counted

That pipeline number is worth pausing on. 6.11 made it nine (three surface styles × three blend styles), 6.12 added the resolve, 6.13 added three for the bloom, and MSAA does not add one — it doubles the scene set, because sample count is another axis of baked-in state. Lesson 6.13 said the enumerate-versus-hash argument would stop being a curiosity; this is where it stops.

And 6.13's ownership rule survived its first real test. The lesson before this one claimed that gpu_post_stack should own "what crosses between stages" and gpu_bloom should own its own intermediates. MSAA adds a target with a genuinely new lifetime — written by the scene pass, read by nothing — and the rule placed it without amendment: the resolved target crosses between stages and the stack owns it; the multisample target is an intermediate of the scene pass. Only the allocation changed.

Which is a useful thing to know about a design: it was tested by a case it was not written for, and it held.

9Common pitfalls & debugging

Every silhouette has a thin dark outline

Symptom. Antialiasing is clearly working — the staircase is gone — but edges look dirty, and the image is subtly darker overall.

Cause. The resolve averaged sRGB bytes rather than linear light. A half-covered edge lands on code 127 instead of 188 and delivers 21.2% of the light.

Fix. Decode, average, encode once. Check it with the arithmetic rather than the picture: a half-white half-black 2×2 block must resolve to 188.

MSAA does nothing for the sparkle

Symptom. Silhouettes are clean at 8×, and the highlight still flashes.

Cause. Not a bug. MSAA multisamples coverage and shades once per primitive per pixel, so on an interior pixel its answer is the single-sample answer.

Fix. Filter the NDF (§3.6), or supersample, which shades per sample and costs N². Reaching for a higher MSAA level is the one thing guaranteed not to help.

The render pass will not begin after enabling MSAA

Symptom. SDL_BeginGPURenderPass returns null, often several frames after the setting changed.

Cause. A mismatched sample count. Every attachment in a pass shares its sample positions, so the colour target, the depth target and the pipeline must agree. The "several frames later" part is the giveaway: a cached depth buffer was reused because its size still matched.

Fix. Put the sample count in the depth target's identity test, not just its dimensions — which is what ensure_depth now does.

The MSAA frame renders correctly and displays nothing

Symptom. A RenderDoc capture shows the geometry drawn perfectly into the scene target, and the screen is the clear colour.

Cause. The resolve was not requested — store_op left at STORE, or resolve_texture null — so the downstream passes read a texture nothing ever wrote.

Fix. SDL_GPU_STOREOP_RESOLVE plus a resolve_texture. Build the struct in one place so it cannot be assembled wrong twice.

A chrome surface goes matte at its own silhouette

Symptom. Specular AA works, and mirror-like objects develop a soft dull rim.

Cause. The clamp on the added roughness is missing or too high. At a silhouette the normal sweeps most of a hemisphere within one pixel, so the unclamped filter asks for fully rough.

Fix. min(2σ², κ) with κ = 0.18. It is a fit, and it is the sort of number an artist should be allowed to change.

Your antialiasing measurement says the error is already zero

Symptom. A test compares the mean of an antialiased image against ground truth and reports agreement at every sample count, including 1×.

Cause. The mean is blind to aliasing. Across many pixels whose phases differ, errors of opposite sign cancel exactly. The first draft of §D passed this way while measuring nothing.

Fix. Aliasing is a per-pixel phenomenon, so use a per-pixel statistic — RMS deviation, or the worst pixel. More generally: before believing a null result, check that the measurement is capable of a non-null one.

10Exercises

10.1 — Find your own crossover guided

§3.4 computes the crossover for a sphere 40 px in radius. Recompute it for the demo's torus at the distance the demo actually uses, then set --polish just above and just below your answer and confirm the sparkle appears where predicted.

Hint

A torus is the harder case and that is the point: its normal turns fastest around the minor radius, so the relevant R is the minor radius in pixels, not the major. Expect the crossover to land at a higher roughness than the sphere's.

10.2 — Per-pixel gradients on the CPU engine challenge

The CPU specular AA in the demo estimates one normal variation for the whole scene. Do it properly: derive dn/dx and dn/dy from the triangle, exactly as Lesson 6.10's uv_gradients derives the uv gradients, and apply filtered_roughness per fragment.

Then compare against the GPU on the same scene. They should agree closely and not exactly — and working out why not is the real exercise. (Start with what ddx actually computes across a 2×2 quad at a triangle edge.)

10.3 — A post-process AA, and a test of §6's claim engine challenge

Implement FXAA as a stage in gpu_post_stack: detect edges from luminance differences in the resolved image and blur along them. It is perhaps eighty lines of HLSL.

The interesting part is not the filter. Lesson 6.13 claimed that gpu_post_stack is the right shape at two stages and stops being right at four or five. You are adding the third. Report honestly whether it was comfortable — and if it was not, you have found 6.17's motivation a lesson early, which is worth more than the effect.

10.4 — Measure MSAA's real cost guided

§6.4 claims 4× MSAA costs about 1.3× rather than 4×. Verify it on your own hardware: time the scene pass at 1×, 2× and 4× with the profiling hooks from Lesson 3.10, and plot cost against sample count.

Hint

The scaling depends on what your frame is limited by. A scene that is fragment-shading bound will scale close to 1×, because the shader count does not change; one that is bandwidth bound will scale much worse, because the target really is four times the size. Say which yours is.

10.5 — Break the approximation open

§3.6 flags that "α behaves as a standard deviation" is an approximation, because GGX's variance is infinite. Construct a case where it visibly fails — a surface whose normal distribution within a pixel is strongly bimodal, such as a sharp crease viewed edge-on — and compare the filtered result against brute-force ground truth.

Then consider what a better filter would need to know. (This is an open research area; Kaplanyan et al.'s "Filtering Distributions of Normals" is the place to start.)

11Recap & next

The engine can now antialias both of the things that were aliasing, and — more usefully — it can tell you which one you are looking at.

  • Two problems, not one. Geometric aliasing undersamples coverage; shading aliasing undersamples the lighting. MSAA fixes the first and cannot touch the second, because it multisamples coverage and shades once per primitive per pixel — which is the optimisation that makes it affordable.
  • The lobe has a closed form. sin t = α√((√2−1)/(1−α²)), exact at every roughness, verified against a bisection of the real NDF to 1.6 × 10⁻⁸ — and the familiar "about α wide" overstates it by 55%.
  • The crossover is computable. Compare the lobe's width to the normal's variation across a pixel. On a 40-pixel sphere that is roughness 0.2468, which places every polished material from Lesson 6.12 in the aliasing regime.
  • Prefiltering is the only cure, in both halves. Supersampling prefilters coverage by integrating over the pixel's area; NDF filtering prefilters the lighting by widening the lobe. Both throw information away, on purpose.

And the result that reframes the subject:

Antialiasing does not make a pixel correct. It makes it stable — by 2,034× here, while improving accuracy by 2.4× and sometimes making it worse.

Which is worth carrying beyond this lesson. When you next evaluate a rendering technique, ask what frequencies it removes rather than how accurate it is. A great many things in real-time graphics are best understood as deliberate, well-chosen losses.

Next is Lesson 6.15, skyboxes and image-based lighting — where a surface stops being lit by a handful of directions and starts being lit by everything around it. The connection to this lesson is closer than it looks: prefiltering an environment map by roughness is the same operation as §3.6, performed ahead of time instead of per fragment, and ggx_lobe_half_angle is about to be asked how much of the sky one mip level should cover.
