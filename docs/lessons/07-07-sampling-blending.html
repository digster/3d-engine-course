Module 7 — Motion: Rotation, Animation, Audio · Lesson 7.7

Sampling and Blending Animations

Lesson 7.6 ended with a character that can be posed and a demo that posed it from two sliders. A clip replaces the sliders, and the whole of the replacement is a function from time to pose — which sounds like an afternoon's work and is not, because a clip does not store poses. Storing them costs 3.6 MB for thirty seconds of one character; storing a handful of samples per channel and reconstructing everything between them costs a fiftieth of that, and every interesting decision in this lesson comes from that one substitution. Finding the two keys that bracket a time is a search, and playback is coherent, so it is a search you can usually skip — but the cursor that skips it has a worst case, and that worst case is the loop wrap. Reconstructing the value between two keys means choosing a rule per field: a position lerps, a rotation nlerps (three orders inside Lesson 7.5's threshold, because two adjacent keys of a 30 Hz clip are 24° apart even on a limb turning at 720°/s), and scale is the decision 7.5 left open — settled here with a picture, a conformance argument, and the observation that ln(0) is −∞ and a scale of zero is how an animator hides a thing. Then the two questions that only arise once motion is data. A loop has a seam, and a clip whose last key lands one frame short of its stated duration ticks by 5.82° once per cycle — while a walk cycle's root legitimately ends 1.2 units from where it started, so the instrument that finds the first must not report the second. And a cross-fade is not the weighted sum Lesson 7.6 built: blend the poses and every bone keeps its length exactly; blend the composed matrices instead — the same inputs, one step later in the pipeline — and the limb shortens by Σ cos(kδ/2), which is 7.6's candy wrapper with the accumulated whole-chain angle in place of the per-joint one, worst at hands and feet.

1. The problem: the pose has to come from somewhere

Here is the state of the engine at the end of Lesson 7.6, stated as a pipeline: 

    pose  ──▶  compose_pose  ──▶  build_palette  ──▶  skin_into  ──▶  pixels

Four stages, all of them written, all of them measured. The arrow on the far left is the one with nothing behind it. compose_pose takes a std::span<const transform> and asks no questions about where those transforms came from; in 7.6's demo they came from two ImGui sliders, and the doc comment on the function that built them said so in as many words: 

/// **This is the only place in the program that knows what "a pose" means**,
/// and it is deliberately tiny: Lesson 7.7 replaces it with a clip sampler
/// and nothing else in this file changes, because everything downstream takes
/// `std::span<const transform>` and does not care where the transforms came
/// from.

That is a prediction with a date on it, and this is the lesson that collects. What replaces the sliders is a clip: a recorded piece of motion, authored in a tool by somebody who is not you, that answers one question —

\[ \mathrm{pose} \;=\; \mathrm{clip}(t) \]

…a clip is a function from time to pose.

It really is that simple, and the simplicity is worth pausing on, because it is what makes the rest of this lesson possible to reason about. A clip has no state. It does not know what frame it is on, it does not know how fast it is playing, it does not know how many characters are playing it. Ask it for t = 0.37 and it answers; ask it again and it gives the same answer. Everything in an animation system that does have state — the playhead, the blend weights, the state machine that decides what to play next — sits on top of that function and could be thrown away and rewritten without touching it.

So where is the difficulty? In the word function. A clip does not store the pose for every t, because there is no such thing as every t and because even the ones you might ask for are ruinously expensive to keep. Count it on the rig the harness uses — a plausible humanoid with a spine, two arms and two legs, 23 joints:

\[ 100 \;\text{joints} \times 40 \;\text{bytes} \times 30 \;\text{Hz} \times 30 \;\text{s} \;=\; 3{,}600{,}000 \;\text{bytes} \]

…a hundred-joint character, sampled thirty times a second for thirty seconds, is 3.6 MB of pose.

That number comes out of Lesson 7.6's own arithmetic: transform is a vec3, a quat and a vec3, which is 40 bytes, so a hundred-joint pose is 4 KB and thirty seconds of them at 30 Hz is 3.6 MB. For one clip. A game has hundreds. A fighting game has thousands.

And the memory is only the visible half of the problem. The invisible half is that a pose array is a film strip: it records everything at the same rate, whether or not anything is happening, and it has no way to say this joint does not move. On a real rig that is most of them.

2. Building intuition: a bundle of curves, not a film strip

Open any animation tool and look at what an animator actually sees. It is not a strip of poses. It is a curve editor: one horizontal track per thing that moves, with dots on it where the animator set a value, and a curve drawn through the dots. The left thigh's rotation is one track. The root's forward translation is another. The left hand's scale is a track too, and it is empty, and the animator has never once thought about it.

That picture is not a user-interface convenience laid over a pose array. It is the storage format, in every engine and every interchange format, and the reason is exactly the two problems above. Independent tracks let each channel be sampled as densely as it needs and no denser; and a channel with nothing in it costs nothing.

Before writing any of it down, it is worth being precise about what a "channel" is, because the word does a lot of work below. A channel is one field of one joint, over time. Joint 15's rotation is a channel. Joint 15's position is a different channel, with its own key times, which may be — and usually is — empty. A rig with n joints has 3n channels, and the whole storage argument is about how few of them are occupied.

How few? The harness bakes a walk-shaped motion into every channel of all 23 joints at 30 Hz — 2,139 keys, deliberately naively — and then runs the reduction. Here is the map of what survived: 

   what survived, joint by joint (P/R/S, . = gone):
     root         P . .    26 keys
     hips         . R .     6 keys
     spine1       . R .     4 keys
     …
     head         . . .     0 keys
     clavicle.L   . . .     0 keys
     upperarm.L   . R .    14 keys
     …
     thigh.L      . R .    19 keys
     shin.L       . R .    16 keys
     foot.L       . R .    11 keys
     toe.L        . . .     0 keys

   channels carrying data: 16 of 69  (23.2%)
     channels elided entirely: 53

One position channel in the entire rig, on the root, because that is the joint that carries the character across the ground. No scale channels at all. Fifteen rotation channels out of twenty-three, because the head, the clavicles, the hands and the toes do not move in a walk. That is 23.2% of the channels holding 100% of the motion, and it is not a property of this fixture — it is a property of how limbs work. A limb does not slide along its parent; it pivots. The whole of animation is rotation, with translation on the root and almost nowhere else.

The elision is exact, not approximate

A channel with no keys is not "close enough to the bind pose". The sampler falls back to sk.joints[j].local_bind for an empty channel, so deleting a channel whose only value is that bind value reproduces the same pose bit for bit — the harness's §I.7 control samples an entirely empty clip and compares it against rest_pose, and the difference is 0.000e+00 on every joint. The zero-key case is a representation, not a compression artefact.

3. The clip, in memory

Three nested things, and the nesting is the whole design: 

    clip
     +- tracks[j]                       joint j
          +- position  [ (t, v) ... ]   its own times
          +- rotation  [ (t, q) ... ]   its own times
          +- scale     [ (t, v) ... ]   its own times

A keyframe is a time and a value. A joint_track is three vectors of them. A clip is a name, a duration, a loop flag and one track per joint. That is the entire format, and it is glTF's, and Unity's, and Unreal's, with different spellings.

Two things in that picture are worth stopping on, because they are the parts a first implementation tends to get wrong in the other direction.

Each channel keeps its own times. Not one time array shared by all three channels of a joint, and certainly not one shared by the whole clip. It costs four bytes per key to repeat the time, and it buys the ability to reduce each channel independently — which is where the saving comes from, and §9 measures it. A shared time array means the busiest channel sets the key rate for every other one, which is the film-strip problem in miniature.

The time is absolute, not a delta from the previous key. Deltas would save nothing (the field is the same four bytes either way) and would make a random seek impossible without a running sum. A clip has to be seekable — a scrub bar, a blend that starts mid-clip, a network rewind — and absolute times make a seek a comparison.

3.1 What is not in the clip, and why it matters more than it sounds

There is no float time in the struct above. There is no cursor, no playback rate, no "current frame". That absence is a deliberate architectural line and it is the same one Lesson 7.6 drew between skeleton and pose, one level up.

A clip is an asset. One copy, loaded once, shared by every character playing it, immutable from the moment it arrives. Put a playhead in it and two guards could not walk out of step. Put the sampler's search cursor in it and they could not walk at all without fighting over a std::uint32_t. So the mutable half lives in the caller: 

struct track_cursor
{
    std::uint32_t position = 0;
    std::uint32_t rotation = 0;
    std::uint32_t scale = 0;
};

Twelve bytes a joint — 1.2 KB for a hundred-joint character — against a clip that may be megabytes and is shared by everyone. That ratio is what makes the split obviously right, and it is the same ratio that made 7.6's palette the thing to upload rather than the deformed mesh.

The engine's convention for caller-owned scratch is already established: skinning_palette(sk, pose, model_from_joint, palette) takes two vectors it neither owns nor allocates. sample follows it exactly: 

void sample(const clip& c, const skeleton& sk, float time,
            std::vector<track_cursor>& cursors, std::vector<transform>& out);

Read that signature as a sentence and it says what the lesson is: this clip, on this skeleton, at this time, remembering this — produces a pose. The pose that comes out is the span every function in anim/skeleton.hpp already takes, and that is the seam holding. Nothing in Lesson 7.6 changes by a character.

4. Sampling: the bracket, and the cursor

Everything a sampler does reduces to one question asked once per channel per frame: which two keys is this time between? Once you have the pair, the rest is a normalised parameter and an interpolation, and both are three lines.

Say it precisely, because the precision is where the off-by-ones live. Given keys with strictly increasing times t₀ < t₁ < … < tₙ₋₁, find the largest i with keyᵢ.time ≤ t, clamped into [0, n−2] so that keyᵢ₊₁ always exists. Then

\[ u \;=\; \frac{t - t_i}{t_{i+1} - t_i} \]

…u is how far into the interval the time falls, from 0 at the left key to 1 at the right one.

Push real numbers through it. A 30 Hz track has keys at multiples of 1/30, so the third and fourth keys are at 0.1333 and 0.2000. Ask for t = 0.1800:

\[ i = 2, \qquad u = \frac{0.1800 - 0.1333}{0.2000 - 0.1333} = \frac{0.0467}{0.0667} = 0.7000 \]

Seven tenths of the way from key 2 to key 3. That is the whole of sampling, and the figure below is that calculation drawn.

4.1 The search you can usually skip

Finding i is a search over a sorted array, and the textbook answer is a binary search: log₂(n) comparisons, each of them a branch the predictor cannot help with, per channel per joint per frame. On a 301-key track that is 8.2 unpredictable branches, multiplied by sixty-nine channels, multiplied by however many characters are on screen.

But look at what is actually being asked. Playback is not a random access pattern. The time this frame is a few milliseconds after the time last frame, which means the answer is nearly always the same answer as last frame, or the next one. A search that starts from scratch every frame is throwing away a near-perfect prediction.

So keep the answer. That is what track_cursor is, and the lookup becomes: 

template <typename Key>
std::uint32_t walk(const std::vector<Key>& keys, float t, std::uint32_t cursor)
{
    const auto last = static_cast<std::uint32_t>(keys.size() - 2u);
    if (cursor > last) { cursor = last; }

    // Backwards: a seek, or the moment the clip looped. Not a walk.
    if (keys[cursor].time > t) { return search(keys, t); }

    for (std::uint32_t step = 0; step < k_walk_limit; ++step)
    {
        if (cursor >= last || keys[cursor + 1u].time > t) { return cursor; }
        ++cursor;
    }

    // Still not there: this is a forward seek wearing a walk's clothes.
    return (cursor < last && keys[cursor + 1u].time <= t) ? search(keys, t) : cursor;
}

In the ordinary case that is one comparison that fails. The interesting part is the three ways out of the ordinary case, and the middle one was not in the first draft.

Backwards is a search, not a walk. A time that moved backwards means a seek, or a scrub, or — once per cycle, on every looping clip in the game — the wrap. The first version of this function walked backwards too, with a second while, on the argument that the wrap happens once per cycle and is therefore amortised away. That argument is very nearly right, and the measurement is worth having anyway. Here are three strategies timed on one key array, with the driver advancing exactly one key interval per lookup so that the only difference between the two rows is the track length: 

   one lookup per key interval, best of three:
     keys   two walks   walk+search   binary
       31     1.548 ns     1.324 ns    3.626 ns
      301     1.729 ns     0.985 ns   10.183 ns
   the cursor beats the search by 2.74x and 10.34x

So the search fallback is worth 14% at 31 keys and 43% at 301 — a modest win, stated as one. What it really buys is the shape of the worst case: a wrap costs n steps in the retired version and log₂(n) in this one, so the cost of a seek stops growing with the clip. And look at the column the cursor exists for at all: against a pure binary search it is 2.74× on a short track and 10.34× on a long one, and the ratio grows because log₂(n) grows and 1 does not.

The first version of that measurement was measuring two things

It varied the wrap period by changing the driver's step size — so each lookup on the "more wraps" row also crossed ten intervals instead of one, and the forward walk gave up and fell into the search every time. It reported 43% for the wrap alone, confidently and wrongly. A benchmark with two independent variables in it is a benchmark with none.

A long forward run is a seek too. Ordinary playback advances the cursor by one interval every frame or two, so the walk limit can be small; four leaves room for a clip whose keys are denser than the frame rate (120 Hz keys sampled at 60 advances two) without ever paying log₂(n) on a frame that did not jump. A caller sampling a 30 Hz clip at 10 Hz crosses three intervals a frame and still walks; one that seeks across the whole clip searches, which is the right answer.

4.2 A stale cursor costs time, not truth

This is the property that makes the whole optimisation safe to live with, and it is worth stating as a guarantee rather than a hope: both loops are driven by the key times, not by trust. A cursor left over from a different clip, or from a seek the caller forgot to announce, or from an uninitialised vector, changes how many comparisons the lookup does and nothing else.

The harness's control for that is deliberately harsher than "do not warm the cursor". Lesson 7.6 ended with a rule — ask what your control would say if the thing were completely broken — after one of its checks ran at the bind pose, where every palette matrix is the identity and the wrong rule gives the right answer. An unwarmed cursor of 0 is the correct answer on frame 0, so it proves nothing. So §A.3 jams every cursor at index 9999 and demands the poses come back bit-identical to a from-scratch binary search: 

   977 samples over 6.97 s of a 1.00 s clip
   worst position gap between the two routes 0.000e+00
  [PASS] A.1  cursor and binary search agree to the bit
  [PASS] A.2  and after a backward jump, still to the bit
   CONTROL cursors jammed at 9999: same poses yes
  [PASS] A.3  CONTROL: a stale cursor costs time, not truth

That is also why sample_at exists in the public header beside sample. It is the same function with the cursor replaced by a binary search — useful in its own right for one-off lookups where there is no previous frame to remember, and indispensable as the thing sample is checked against. Two implementations that must agree to the bit is a much stronger test than one implementation checked against a description of itself.

5. Three fields, three rules

Now the other half of a sample: given the bracket and u, produce the value. A transform has three fields and they live in three different kinds of space, so this is not one rule applied three times. It is three decisions, and exactly one of them was left open by Lesson 7.5.

5.1 Position: lerp, and there is no case to answer

A position is a point in an affine space, and the only structure a point has is the straight line to another point. A straight line traversed at constant speed is the unique path between two positions that invents no information; anything curved would be a claim about a trajectory that the two endpoints simply do not contain. glTF says the same thing in symbols — its LINEAR mode is vₜ = (1−t)·vₖ + t·vₖ₊₁, componentwise — and there is no interesting alternative to consider.

5.2 Rotation: nlerp, with a number behind it

Lesson 7.5 spent half a lesson measuring this exact tradeoff and arrived at a threshold: nlerp walks the same great circle as slerp (excess turning 0.00%) on a schedule that is wrong, and the error stays under half a degree up to an arc of 73.50°, for 3.42× less work. The only question left is whether the arcs a clip contains are inside that.

They are, by three orders of magnitude, and the way to see it is to stop thinking about quaternions and think about limbs. A key interval at 30 Hz is 1/30 s. A limb turning at r degrees per second therefore moves r/30 degrees between two adjacent keys: 

   rate deg/s   at 30 Hz   nlerp err   slerp
           90      3.00    0.000033   exact
          180      6.00    0.000264   exact
          360     12.00    0.002112   exact
          720     24.00    0.016921   exact
   worst over every rate above             0.016921 deg

720°/s is two full turns a second — a punch, a whip crack, the fastest thing a limb does. It moves 24° between keys, and nlerp lags slerp by 0.0169°, which is a thousandth of the threshold. Inside a clip, nlerp is not a compromise; it is free and exact to anything anyone could see.

The specification agrees, and says so in a way worth quoting because it is unusually candid. glTF's LINEAR mode, targeting a rotation, says spherical linear interpolation SHOULD be used; then Appendix C, having given the slerp formula, adds that implementations may approximate these equations to reach application-specific accuracy and/or performance targets, and notes that when the arc is close to zero, spherical linear interpolation turns into regular linear interpolation. That is the same observation from the other end, written by people who expected exactly this decision to be made.

The factor of two charges rent in the documentation too

Writing this lesson turned up a defect in a doc comment this course shipped in Lesson 7.5. quat_nlerp's comment quoted a table — 0.13° at 30°, 4.07° at 90°, 26.34° at 150° — and then argued from it that "under about 30° of arc … is where a 30 Hz animation clip's adjacent keyframes live". Both halves are true and they are in different units. The table is in sphere arcs and sphere degrees; the sentence is thinking in rotation angles, which are double. Read as sphere arcs, that sentence claims a clip's keys are 60° of rotation apart, which is 1,800°/s and nothing a limb does. Both tables are now written out in the header, labelled. A quantity with a factor of two in it needs its unit said every single time, and a comment is not exempt.

5.3 Scale: the decision Lesson 7.5 left open

Lesson 7.5's twelfth section asked the reader to write transform_slerp, and named the open question inside it: position lerps, rotation slerps, and scale could go either way. It is time to settle it, and the honest way to settle it is with a picture rather than a preference.

The two candidates are linear — equal differences in equal times — and geometric — equal ratios:

\[ s_{\text{lin}}(t) = (1-t)\,a + t\,b, \qquad s_{\text{geo}}(t) = a\left(\frac{b}{a}\right)^{t} = e^{(1-t)\ln a + t \ln b} \]

…the first adds the same amount each step; the second multiplies by the same factor each step.

On a growth from 1 to 8 they disagree loudly, and the disagreement is exactly the gap between the arithmetic and geometric means:

\[ \frac{a+b}{2} - \sqrt{ab} \;=\; \frac{1+8}{2} - \sqrt{8} \;=\; 4.500 - 2.828 \]

And the geometric one is the better-looking curve. Scale composes by multiplication all the way down a hierarchy, and perceived size is closer to logarithmic than to linear, so "equal ratios per second" is what steady growth looks like to an eye. If the argument stopped there, geometric would win.

Three things settle it for the lerp anyway, in increasing order of how hard they are to argue with.

First, the gap is invisible over the range content uses. Squash and stretch lives between about 0.8 and 1.25, and the harness measures the worst midpoint gap over that band at 0.62%. A full doubling is 6.07%. The 59.10% of the 1-to-8 example needs a ratio no rig animates — and if a rig ever does, see the last paragraph of this section.

Second, a scale of zero is legal, common, and has no logarithm. Scaling an object to 0 is how animators make things vanish, and mirrored rigs carry a negative scale down one side. Geometric interpolation does not shrink toward zero; it teleports: 

   geometric 1 -> 0, by t:
       t    a*(b/a)^t     lerp
    0.00     1.000000    1.0000
    0.01     0.000000    0.9900
    0.25     0.000000    0.7500
    0.50     0.000000    0.5000
    1.00     0.000000    0.0000
   CONTROL ln(-1) = NaN, ln(0) = -inf

The object disappears on the first frame after t = 0 rather than over the second the animator asked for. A rule that needs a special case for the most common extreme value of its own input is not the rule.

Third, and decisively, the file format says linear. glTF defines LINEAR as componentwise lerp for every animated property that is not a rotation. An importer that log-lerps scale plays the file differently from every other viewer on earth, which is a correctness bug wearing a mathematician's coat. The interpolation rule is not ours to choose once we accept content from a tool.

The answer to somebody who wants geometric growth is a keyframe

Not a runtime rule. The gap between the two schedules is the arithmetic-minus-geometric mean of the per-segment ratio, and splitting the span cuts that ratio by an n-th root. Measured: one key spans 1→8 with a 59.10% gap, three keys span ratios of 2 with 6.07%, and six keys span ratios of √2 with 1.51%. The animator already has the tool and already knows how to use it. A policy the content pipeline can express does not belong in the sampler.

5.4 The name, and why it is not transform_slerp

Which leaves the function. Lesson 7.5's exercise asked for transform_slerp, and that name is wrong: exactly one of the three fields slerps, and calling the whole thing after the rule that governs a third of it is the kind of name that makes a caller believe the position is travelling on an arc. transform_blend claims nothing: 

[[nodiscard]] inline transform transform_blend(const transform& a, const transform& b, float t)
{
    return {.position = lerp(a.position, b.position, t),
            .rotation = quat_nlerp(a.rotation, b.rotation, t),
            .scale    = lerp(a.scale, b.scale, t)};
}

Three lines, three arguments, all of them above. transform_blend_slerp sits beside it with quat_slerp in the middle, for the caller whose arcs are not small — and §7 is about how to find out which you are, rather than guessing.

One more thing hides in that snippet, and it is the reason §8 is short. quat_nlerp calls nearest internally. So the sampler pays for the double cover without a single line in this file mentioning it, which is what a well-placed primitive looks like from the outside.

6. Looping, the seam, and the negative time

A walk cycle is played for as long as the character walks, which is longer than the clip. So the time has to be mapped onto the clip, and the map is fmod — except that it is not, and the two ways it is not are both worth having met.

6.1 The duration is authored, not derived

The obvious definition of a clip's length is "the largest time on any key". It is wrong, and it is wrong for exactly the clips people care most about.

Bake a two-second walk at 30 Hz. Sixty frames. If the baker writes one key per frame, the last key lands at frame 59, which is 59/30 = 1.9667 s. Loop at that and the cycle is one frame short: it restarts one frame early, every cycle, forever. The exporter knows the intended length; the key times only know where somebody happened to put a key.

So duration is a field, and the baker's job is to make the last key land on it — frames + 1 samples over frames intervals, which is the <= in the demo's loop that is easy to write as <: 

const int frames = static_cast<int>(k_clip_seconds * k_bake_hz);
for (int f = 0; f <= frames; ++f)          // <=, not <
{
    const float t = static_cast<float>(f) / k_bake_hz;
    …
}

And because that is easy to get wrong and invisible when you do, validate measures it. Sample the clip at t = 0 and again at t = duration, compare the two poses, and report the worst difference: 

   keys at 30 Hz over 1.00 s:
     closed (last key AT 1.000)  31 keys
     open   (last key at 0.967)  30 keys
   loop gap, closed     0.0000 deg  0.000e+00 units
   loop gap, open       5.8215 deg  0.000e+00 units

5.82° — one frame of a walk's fastest joint, stepped over once per cycle. It is a tick, not a glitch, and it is genuinely hard to find by looking at the character, because the animation is correct at every instant except one.

Root motion is a loop discontinuity by design, and the first instrument could not tell

The first version of this measurement included every joint, and it reported a 1.200-unit seam on a walk cycle that is perfect. Of course it did: the root carries the character forward, so it is supposed to end the cycle 1.2 units from where it started. A walk with zero there is one that moonwalks on the spot. An instrument that cannot tell root motion from a broken loop reports every correct walk as broken, so clip_report now splits them — root_travel for joints with no parent, loop_gap_position for everything else. The fixture reports 1.2000 units of travel and 0.000e+00 of seam, which is two facts where there was one confused one.

That split also names something the engine does not do yet, and §10 says so plainly: nothing applies that root travel to the character's world transform, so a looping clip snaps the character back to the origin on every wrap. Extracting root motion is its own piece of design and it is not in this lesson.

The control for the seam measurement is worth reading too, because it is a case where the two obvious wrong durations fail differently: 

   CONTROL duration on a 1.0 s clip:
     1.5 -> gap  0.0000 deg, 0 keys out of range
     0.5 -> gap 42.7780 deg, 1035 keys out of range

A duration longer than the keys cannot produce a seam at all — the sampler holds the last key, which for a closed loop is the first one again, so the clip simply pauses before restarting. A duration shorter cuts the cycle off in the middle: 42.78° of seam, plus a thousand keys reported past the end. Same mistake, two completely different symptoms.

6.2 std::fmod is not wrap_time

Here is the whole function, and the two lines that are not fmod: 

float wrap_time(const clip& c, float t)
{
    if (!(c.duration > 0.0f)) { return 0.0f; }
    if (!c.loops) { return std::clamp(t, 0.0f, c.duration); }

    float wrapped = std::fmod(t, c.duration);
    if (wrapped < 0.0f) { wrapped += c.duration; }
    return wrapped;
}

std::fmod(-0.1f, 1.0f) is -0.1f, not 0.9f. C's fmod takes the sign of the dividend, which is the right answer to a question about remainders and the wrong answer to a question about phase. One add fixes it. And a negative playback time is not exotic: it arrives the moment anything scrubs backwards, runs a time dilation below 1.0 with an offset, or starts a cross-fade with a negative lead-in.

What makes this worth a section rather than a footnote is what happens next, because there are two different wrong things and which one you get is decided three functions away, by whether the sampler clamps its normalised parameter. 

   CONTROL the same times with NO clamp on u:
        t       u    extrapolated   frozen
    -0.033   -0.99       0.015 deg   5.763 deg
    -0.100   -3.00       0.800 deg  16.458 deg
    -0.300   -9.00      21.995 deg  26.630 deg
    -0.600  -18.00     100.007 deg  16.458 deg

Read the two columns against each other, because the moral is not the obvious one.

Clamped — which is what this engine does — the pose sticks on the clip's first key for as long as the time stays negative. The character freezes. That error is immediately visible, and it is bounded: it can never exceed the range of motion the clip itself contains, so 16.458° is roughly as bad as it gets.

Unclamped, u goes negative and the pose extrapolates backwards out of the start of the clip. At a thirtieth of a second back that is 0.015° — you could not see it with a protractor. At a tenth of a second, 0.8°. And then it keeps going: 100° at six tenths, with no bound at all.

So the clamp produces the louder error and the unclamped version produces the quieter one, and the quiet one is worse. It is finite, it is smooth, it moves in a plausible direction, and it is wrong — which is Lesson 7.5's rule about finite-and-wrong, arriving from a direction that lesson did not consider. The clamp is the right choice precisely because it fails where you can see it.

Wrap the clock; do not let it accumulate

wrap_time gives the right answer for any float, so it is tempting to keep a playhead that just adds dt forever. Do not, and the reason is a measurement nobody expects: std::fmod is not constant time. Its cost grows with the quotient, so a one-second clip whose clock has reached 6,666 seconds does ten times the reduction a ten-second clip does. Timed directly, with nothing else in the loop: 1.603 ns on a bounded clock against 5.901 ns on a grown one for a ten-second duration, and 3.798 against 7.726 for a one-second one. A program left running overnight pays for every hour of it, on every sample, forever. The rig demo wraps its playhead every fixed step for exactly this reason.

7. Cross-fading, and the one step that ruins it

A character that can play one clip is a character that teleports between animations. What makes motion look continuous is the cross-fade: sample two clips at the same instant and blend the results, sliding the weight from 0 to 1 over a fifth of a second.

Which sounds like it should be the same weighted sum Lesson 7.6 spent a whole lesson on. It is not, and the difference is the most important thing in this section.

7.1 Two weighted sums, on two different things

Lesson 7.6's blend sums matrices:

\[ v' \;=\; \sum_i w_i \,\mathrm{skin}_i\, v \]

and that was forced. A vertex near an elbow is influenced by two joints at once, and there is nowhere else to put the weights: the thing being weighted is the effect of several matrices on one point. The price was exact and unavoidable — the sum of two rotations is not a rotation, so a twisted limb collapses to cos(θ/2) of its radius.

A cross-fade has a choice, because the two things being blended are two poses, and a pose is a list of transforms. Blend those:

\[ \mathrm{out}_j \;=\; \mathrm{transform\_blend}(a_j,\, b_j,\, w) \]

…joint by joint, in local space, before anything is composed.

And now read what came out. transform_blend's rotation field is quat_nlerp, which normalises. So the blended rotation is a unit quaternion, which is a rotation, which means the blended transform is a genuine placement — and a placement's position field is the bone's length, untouched by the rotation blend. Bone lengths are preserved exactly. The collapse that dominated Lesson 7.6 simply does not arise, because nothing here is being averaged that is not closed under averaging.

7.2 The cheap wrong version, and what it costs

The wrong version is not stupid, which is what makes it worth building. It is what you reach for if the thing you have in hand is the composed model matrices — say, because a renderer handed you two model_from_joint arrays and you would like something halfway between them: 

for (std::size_t j = 0; j < posed_.size(); ++j)
{
    posed_[j] = matrix_lerp(posed_a_[j], posed_b_[j], fade_);
}

Sixteen independent lerps. Same inputs, same weight, one step later in the pipeline. And it is linear blend skinning one level up, with all of 7.6's consequences and one new one.

The right-hand tube is shorter and visibly thinner toward its tip. Both are the same cause and the derivation is short enough to do here.

Joint j's position in pose A is pjA and in pose B is pjB. A matrix lerp averages the translation columns, so the blended joint sits at the midpoint of the two. Bone k runs from joint k−1 to joint k, so its blended length is

\[ \left|\frac{(p_k^A - p_{k-1}^A) + (p_k^B - p_{k-1}^B)}{2}\right| \;=\; \left|\frac{d^A + d^B}{2}\right| \;=\; L\,\cos(\Delta_k/2) \]

…the average of two unit vectors an angle apart is shorter than either, by the cosine of half that angle.

That is the same isoceles triangle Lesson 7.6 drew for the candy wrapper, and it is here for the same reason. But Δk is different, and the difference is the new part. Bone k's direction is joint k−1's own axis, and joint k−1 has accumulated every rotation above it in the chain. Two poses that differ by δ at every joint differ by (k−1)·δ at bone k, so the whole chain becomes

\[ \text{chain} \;=\; \sum_{k=1}^{n-1} \cos\!\left(\frac{(k-1)\,\delta}{2}\right) \]

A prediction with no free parameters. Measure it on a six-joint chain of unit bones, blended half and half, sweeping δ: 

   per-joint   pose blend   matrix blend   cos(d/2)
      gap deg   chain len      chain len    predicts
          0.0     5.00000        5.00000     5.00000
          5.0     5.00000        4.97150     4.97150
         10.0     5.00000        4.88662     4.88662
         20.0     5.00000        4.55657     4.55657
         30.0     5.00000        4.03906     4.03906
         45.0     5.00000        3.01367     3.01367
   worst bone-length error, pose route  2.980e-07
   worst gap from sum cos(k d / 2)      4.768e-07

At twenty degrees per joint — which is a perfectly ordinary difference between a walk pose and a turn pose — the limb loses 0.4434 units of five. The pose route holds at 5.00000 to six figures, with the worst individual bone out by 2.980e−07, which is float noise.

And the error grows down the chain, which is where it bites. Bone 1 is untouched (Δ = 0), bone 5 carries 4δ. So a matrix cross-fade barely moves the hips and ruins the hands and the feet — which is where the sword is attached, where the foot plants, and where a player looks.

The control that makes the disagreement mean something

At w = 0 and w = 1 there is nothing to blend, so the two routes must agree exactly — if they did not, the gap in between would be a property of the instrument rather than of the blend. Measured: 6.743e−07. That is 7.6's rule applied again: a control has to be one the broken thing would fail.

7.3 Knowing which regime you are in

§5.2 argued that nlerp is free inside a clip because adjacent keyframes are a few degrees apart. A cross-fade has no such guarantee: it blends two poses chosen by gameplay, which can be as far apart as the content allows. So blend_poses returns a small report, and the field that matters is worst_arc: 

struct pose_blend_report
{
    std::size_t joints = 0;
    float worst_arc = 0.0f;     ///< radians, the whole rotation angle
    std::size_t flips = 0;
};

Read it against Lesson 7.5's threshold: a few degrees and nlerp is exact; over 73.50° and you want transform_blend_slerp, or a shorter fade, or a better-chosen pair of clips.

Computing it honestly took one decision worth copying. The metric that is well conditioned near zero is angle_between, which costs twelve multiplies and an atan2 — far too much to run per joint per frame to answer a question about a single joint. But |scalar| is monotonically decreasing in the rotation angle, and the sampler computes it anyway for nearest. So the loop tracks the argmin with one dot product each, and the expensive metric runs exactly once, on the winner.

One thing to be careful about when reading it: worst_arc is a local arc. 

   blend report: 6 joints, worst arc 20.000 deg, 0 flips
     (the LOCAL arc: every joint differs by 20. The TIP has
      accumulated 100, and that is not this number.)

The chain above differs by 20° at every joint and 100° at the tip, and the report says 20 — correctly, because the thing being interpolated is the per-joint transform and the per-joint arc is what sets nlerp's error. The accumulated angle is a property of the composition, and §7.2 is about what it does. Substituting one for the other would over-report the interpolation error by the depth of the skeleton.

8. The double cover, inside one track

Lesson 7.4 established that q and −q are the same orientation, and Lesson 7.5 paid for it with one comparison inside nearest. Here is where the bill actually arrives, because a clip is the first thing in this course whose quaternions were written by somebody else.

An exporter has no obligation to keep signs consistent along a track. It computes each keyframe's orientation independently, and half the time the four floats it produces are the antipode of the ones before them. Both are correct. Both name the same pose. And they are not the same point, which is all that an interpolation cares about.

The harness builds exactly that clip — the walk with every other rotation key negated — and then measures three things, in an order that matters.

What validate sees. 690 adjacent pairs on opposite halves of the sphere, against zero for the clean clip. It is counted and it is deliberately not part of ok(), for the same reason skeleton_report::nonuniform_binds is not: the data is legal. This is a measurement, not a verdict.

What this engine does about it: nothing, and it does not need to. 

   sign flips reported: clean 0, flipped 690
  [PASS] F.1  validate sees an inconsistent export
   this engine's samples differ by  0.000e+00 deg
  [PASS] F.2  nearest inside nlerp already pays for it

Sampling the flipped clip and the clean clip at four hundred times produces bit-identical poses, because quat_nlerp calls nearest and nearest is the whole fix. That is the honest half of this section and it is worth saying out loud: a correctly built sampler is immune, and if you have one, the sign inconsistency in your content is not hurting you.

What happens to anything that is not this engine. Written out rather than described — five lines in the harness that lerp and normalise, with no comparison: 

   CONTROL a lerp without nearest, worst error
     on thigh.L   180.000 deg
     across one flipped interval (t, error):
       0.0333     0.000 deg
       0.0400    61.148 deg
       0.0500   120.000 deg
       0.0600   155.279 deg
       0.0666   180.000 deg

A hundred and eighty degrees, inside one keyframe interval — a thirtieth of a second. The character's thigh takes the long way round between two poses a degree apart, arrives correctly at the next key, and does it again two keys later. What that looks like is not a sign error; it looks like the animation file is corrupt, and that is what makes it expensive. This is the single most common quaternion bug in shipped animation code, and it is one dot product.

8.1 Paying once, at load

canonicalise_rotations walks every rotation channel and puts each key on the same half of the sphere as its predecessor: 

   canonicalise negated 345 keys; flips now 0
  [PASS] F.4  one load-time pass, and the data is consistent
  [PASS] F.5  and running it again changes nothing

345 negations to fix 690 reported flips, which is arithmetic rather than a coincidence: negating every other key repairs the pair before it and the pair after it at once.

After it has run, nearest inside the sampler becomes a test that is never true. So is it worth doing? The honest answer is that the cycles are not the point, and the doc comment says so:

  • It makes the count available, which is a fact about your content pipeline that the runtime cannot tell you. A track that needed a lot of flips came from a tool worth asking about.
  • It fixes the data for every consumer that is not this sampler — a GPU vertex shader doing the blend, a tool that re-exports the clip, a network packer that quantises components, a colleague's reimplementation.
  • It is one dot product per key in the file, once, against one per rotation sampled per joint per frame per character, forever.

And the sampler keeps calling nearest anyway. A clip can arrive from anywhere — a procedural generator, a network stream, a tool that edited keys after the pass ran — and the cost of being wrong is enormously out of proportion to the cost of a comparison that predicts perfectly.

9. What a clip weighs, and what reading it costs

§1 opened with 3.6 MB. Time to find out what the layout actually bought, and the first number is not the one the argument predicted.

9.1 The per-channel layout loses, until something is elided

   23 joints, 31 frames at 30 Hz
   layout                      bytes    of raw
   pose array (40 B/joint)      28520     76.9%
   per-channel, every key       37076   100.0%
   per-channel, reduced          3716     10.0%

Read the first two rows again. A naive per-channel bake is 30% bigger than the pose array it was supposed to beat. And the reason is obvious once you count it: a pose array spends 40 bytes per joint-frame, and three channels spend 16 + 20 + 16 = 52, because every key carries its own four-byte time.

The layout is not the saving. The layout is what makes the saving possible. Independent times are what let a channel be reduced without dragging its neighbours along, and an independent channel is what can be deleted outright. Bake everything and the format is a liability; reduce, and it is 3,716 bytes against 28,520 — 7.7× smaller — with 53 of 69 channels gone entirely.

Scaled to production density: a hundred joints, thirty seconds, the same proportion of channels moving, and the pose array's 3.60 MB becomes 0.48 MB.

9.2 Keyframe reduction

Elision handles channels that do nothing. Reduction handles channels that do something smoothly, which is all of them: a baker at 30 Hz writes a key every frame whether or not the curve needed one, and a straight line through three collinear keys reproduces the middle one exactly.

The algorithm is a greedy fit-and-split. Keep the first key. Extend a candidate span as far as it will go while every key strictly inside it is within tolerance of the value the sampler would reconstruct from the span's two endpoints; the moment one is not, close the span at the previous key and start again from there. The last key is always kept, because a clip that ends somewhere other than where it was authored to end is a clip whose loop seam has moved. 

   tol deg    keys   bytes   err deg   hdr/keys
      0.00    2139   37076    0.1534      0.04x
      0.05     386    7616    0.1534      0.22x
      0.25     287    5636    0.2553      0.29x
      0.50     191    3716    0.5102      0.45x
      2.00     104    1976    3.0000      0.84x
      8.00      62    1136    7.8921      1.46x

Three things in that table repay attention.

The first row is the floor, and it is not zero. The error column is measured against the closed form the keys were baked from, so row one is what sampling a continuous motion at 30 Hz costs before anything is thrown away at all: 0.1534°. Asking the reduction for 0.05° cannot get below it, which is why rows one and two have the same error and wildly different key counts. Measured against the unreduced clip instead, the half-degree reduction comes in at 0.4918° and keeps its promise.

The tolerance is in the animator's units. Not a percentage — "0.1%" means something different on a joint two metres from the root than on one two centimetres from it, and the thing a human can actually judge is "half a degree". Half a degree is about the smallest rotation of a limb a viewer can see on a moving character at gameplay distance, which is why it is the default.

The last column is a warning. hdr/keys is the ratio of std::vector headers — three per joint, 72 bytes on a 64-bit toolchain — to the key data they point at. It starts at 0.04× and climbs to 1.46×, because the headers are a fixed cost per joint and the keys are not. The better the reduction works, the more of the clip is container. That is not an argument against reduction; it is an argument for a flat arena with offsets instead of 69 separate heap blocks, which is Module 9's custom-allocator lesson arriving early and uninvited. Named here, not fixed here.

9.3 Fitting against a curve you will not play

One detail in reduce is easy to get wrong in a way that is invisible until it is expensive, and it is the reason the rotation fit calls quat_nlerp: 

removed += reduce_channel(track.rotation,
    [&](const quat_key& k0, const quat_key& k1, const quat_key& mid) {
        const float u = segment_t(k0.time, k1.time, mid.time);
        return near_rotation(quat_nlerp(k0.value, k1.value, u), mid.value);
    });

Reduction manufactures long arcs on purpose. Its entire job is to replace many small steps with one large one — which is exactly the regime where nlerp and slerp disagree. Fit against a curve the sampler will not walk and the playback error can exceed the tolerance you asked for, by more the better the reduction worked.

The harness makes that concrete with the worst case for it: one joint turning at a constant rate about a fixed axis, which slerp reproduces exactly, so a slerp-fitted reduction keeps two keys and claims zero error. 

   a 120 deg constant-rate turn, tolerance 0.50:
     fitted with nlerp   3 keys, plays  0.4651 deg out
     fitted with slerp   2 keys, plays  2.2340 deg out
     the slerp fit overshoots its budget   4.5x

One key saved, four and a half times the error budget spent, and nothing anywhere in the pipeline reports it — the reducer thinks it succeeded and the sampler thinks it is doing its job. The rule generalises past quaternions: a lossy transformation must be fitted with the exact reconstruction its consumer will perform, not with the one the textbook describes.

Is greedy reduction idempotent? Measure, do not assume

In principle a second pass fits against the first pass's output rather than against the original curve, so the tolerances could add — and a pipeline that reduces on export and again on import would be spending the budget twice, quietly. Run to convergence and it does not: pass two removes 4 more keys of 191, pass three removes none, and the playback error stays at 0.4918° to four decimal places. The greedy anchors move; the curve they are approximating does not. Worth knowing rather than worth fearing — but that is a measurement on this content, not a proof, so check it on yours before chaining two reducers.

9.4 What a frame costs

   sampling 31 keys/track (unreduced):
   (best of three)
   sample, cursor        0.159 us  ( 6.89 ns/joint)
   sample, binary        0.341 us  (14.84 ns/joint)
   the cursor is          2.15x
   compose + palette     0.240 us
   the same clip 10x longer (301 keys/track):
     cursor   0.172 us   binary   0.966 us    5.63x

Sampling 23 joints costs 0.159 µs, which is two thirds of what composing the pose and building the palette cost together and — as Lesson 7.6 measured — a rounding error beside the 44.72 µs of skinning 4,840 vertices. Sampling is not where a frame goes. It is also not free, and a game with forty characters on screen is doing it forty times, plus once more per layer of every blend.

The cursor's margin over the binary search is 2.15× at 31 keys and 5.63× at 301, which is the log₂(n) against 1 showing up exactly where it should.

Two confounds in one measurement, and the second one survived the first fix

This timing block was wrong twice. First, both rows drove the sampler with a clock that climbed to 666 seconds, and std::fmod's quotient-dependence made the short clip slower — so the 301-key row came out faster than the 31-key one, which is backwards. Wrapping the driver's clock fixed that and the inversion stayed. The second cause was the benchmark itself: §A's table is the first timing in the process, and on a laptop the first hundred milliseconds run at a lower clock than everything after them, by about 30% — best_of_three cannot help when all three attempts are inside the ramp. The harness now spins for 150 ms before it measures anything. Two independent reasons for one wrong number is the normal case, not the unlucky one.

10. What this does not do

Every lesson simplifies; this one's simplifications are large enough to name individually, because several of them are things a reader will reasonably expect to find here.

Nothing loads a clip from a file. The demo bakes its clips from procedural functions, and the harness bakes its walk from a closed form. That is a deliberate scoping decision, not an oversight: glTF animation import is a real piece of work — the importer still flattens the node tree and ignores skins entirely, as gltf_primitive::world_from_local says in its own doc comment — and doing it properly means reordering the skeleton topologically and reordering the tracks in the same pass, or every joint in the character is animated by its neighbour. It is Exercise 5, with the shape of the work written out.

Root motion is measured and not applied. §6 split root_travel out of the loop seam so that the instrument tells the truth, and that is as far as it goes. Nothing subtracts the root's travel from the pose and adds it to the character's world transform, so a looping clip snaps the character back to its origin on every wrap. Doing it properly means deciding what happens when the blend weights change mid-stride, which is a design question and not an arithmetic one.

There is no STEP and no CUBICSPLINE. glTF defines three interpolation modes and this sampler implements one. STEP is four lines and is genuinely needed — it is how you animate a discrete thing, a visibility flag or a texture index, where interpolating between two values produces a value that means nothing. CUBICSPLINE stores an in-tangent and an out-tangent beside each value and costs three times the storage; it is what a curve editor's smooth handles export as.

There is no additive blending. A cross-fade replaces one pose with another; an additive layer applies the difference between an animation and a reference on top of whatever is already playing, which is how aim offsets and reaction flinches are built. The machinery is already in the engine — Exercise 4 derives it, and the whole of it is quat_pow_unit, which Lesson 7.5 wrote for slerp and which means precisely "a fraction of this rotation".

There is no blend tree and no state machine. Deciding what to play, and when to start fading, is gameplay logic that sits on top of this API and would not change a line of it. That is the right boundary, and it is the same one Lesson 5.10 drew between an input device and an action.

Component quantisation is named and not implemented. Three of a quaternion's four components at 16 bits each, plus two bits saying which one was dropped and reconstructed from the norm, takes a rotation key from 20 bytes to 12 — and shipped engines go further. The reduction in §9.2 is the algorithmic half of compression; quantisation is the arithmetic half, and it interacts with §8 in a way worth thinking about before implementing, since a quantised −q and a quantised q do not round to negatives of each other.

13. Common pitfalls & debugging

SymptomCauseFix
The character does a full-body spin for exactly one keyframe interval, in the middle of a clip that is otherwise fine. An interpolation without nearest, across a pair of keys the exporter wrote as q and −q′. The long way round is 359°. One four-component dot product; negate the second if it is negative. quat_nlerp and quat_slerp both do it already, so suspect any blend you wrote yourself — especially one in a shader. §8.
A visible tick once per loop, at the same point every cycle, on an animation that looks correct at every instant. The last key is one frame short of the stated duration — frames samples instead of frames + 1. validate and read loop_gap_radians: the fixture reports 5.8215° for a one-frame gap and 0.0000° when closed. §6.1.
The character snaps back to the origin every time a looping clip wraps. Root motion in the clip, applied to the pose rather than to the world transform. Not fixed in this lesson, and named in §10. clip_report::root_travel is how you confirm the diagnosis — 1.2000 units on the fixture.
The animation freezes for a fraction of a second when something scrubs or slows down, then resumes perfectly. A negative playback time reaching the sampler, clamped to the first key. wrap_time, not std::fmod — the sign of the dividend is the whole bug. §6.2.
A cross-fade looks almost right, but the hands and feet swim and the character seems slightly small during the transition. The blend happened on composed matrices rather than on local poses; the error is cos(Δ/2) with the accumulated angle, so it is worst at the extremities. Blend the poses, then compose. If you only have matrices, that is the bug — get the poses. §7.2.
An upper-body clip plays and the legs collapse to the origin. The pose vector was not filled for the joints the clip has no tracks for. sample writes every joint and falls back to local_bind, so this is a symptom of a hand-rolled sampler. §2.
Reduction says it met a half-degree tolerance and playback is visibly worse than that. The fit used a different interpolation rule than the sampler — the classic case is slerp-fitted, nlerp-played. Fit with the sampler's own function. The harness measures a 4.5× budget overrun on a constant-rate turn. §9.3.
Every joint is animated by its neighbour after an import. The skeleton was reordered to satisfy joints[i].parent < i and the tracks were not reordered in the same pass. One permutation, applied to both arrays. clip_report::beyond_skeleton catches the case where the clip is longer than the skeleton; nothing catches a permutation, which is why it has to be one pass. §10.
Sampling gets measurably slower the longer the program has been running. A playback clock that accumulates without bound; std::fmod's cost grows with the quotient. Wrap the playhead every step. Measured at up to 3.7× in §9.4's box.
A clip's memory does not go down as much as the key count does. Three std::vector headers per joint are a fixed 72 bytes, whether the channels hold ten keys or none. Expected, and measured: hdr/keys climbs from 0.04× to 1.46× as the tolerance loosens. The real fix is a flat arena, which is Module 9. §9.2.

14. Exercises

  1. Add STEP interpolation. A discrete channel — a visibility flag, a texture index, a hand-pose enum — must hold its value until the next key rather than slide toward it. Add an interpolation field to joint_track and honour it in eval.

    Hint: the change inside eval is one line — return keys[cursor].value. The interesting part is reduce: what does fit-and-split mean when the reconstruction is a staircase? Sketch: a step channel's interior key fits only if its value equals the span's left endpoint, so the greedy pass collapses runs of identical values and nothing else — which is exactly right, and is run-length encoding arriving from a different direction.

  2. Find the arc where nlerp stops being free, for your own budget. §5.2 shows 0.0169° at 24° of arc and Lesson 7.5 reports 73.50° for a half-degree budget. Write a loop that, for a given budget, finds the arc at which nlerp's worst lag reaches it, and tabulate budgets of 0.1°, 0.5° and 2°.

    Hint: nlerp's angle from a at parameter t on a sphere arc Ω is atan2(t sin Ω, (1−t) + t cos Ω); the lag peaks near t ≈ 0.23 and 0.77. What to notice: the relationship is not linear and not quite a square root — and every number you produce is in sphere degrees unless you double it. Say which in your own comment; §5.2's box is what happens when you do not.

  3. Make the loop seam a warning the demo shows. Bake the rig demo's clips with f < frames instead of f <= frames, and make the panel print loop seam in a different colour when it exceeds a degree.

    Hint: ImGui::TextColored. What to notice: watch the tube while the clip loops with the broken bake. The tick is genuinely hard to see and trivially easy to measure, which is the argument for measuring it.

  4. Additive blending (engine challenge). An additive layer applies the difference between an animation and a reference pose on top of whatever is playing — an aim offset, a flinch, a limp. Implement blend_additive(base, reference, additive, w, out).

    Sketch: the rotation delta in the joint's own frame is D = conj(R) · A, and a fraction of it is quat_pow_unit(nearest(identity, D), w) — the function Lesson 7.5 wrote for slerp, meaning exactly "a fraction of this rotation". Then out = B · D^w. Position is B + w·(A − R). The test to write first: when base equals reference and w = 1, the result must equal additive exactly — R · conj(R) · A = A. That identity is free and it catches every side you could have multiplied on. What to think about: scale has no good additive rule here — a componentwise difference makes "grow 10%" mean +0.1 rather than ×1.1 — and the ratio that fixes it runs into §5.3's zero. Decide, and write down why.

  5. Load a real clip from glTF (engine challenge, a lesson's worth of work). §10 scoped this out; here is the shape of it. glTF stores animations as channels, each with a sampler (an input accessor of times and an output accessor of values) and a target (a node index and one of translation, rotation, scale).

    The four things that will bite, in order: (1) the importer must build the skeleton from skins.joints and topologically sort it to satisfy joints[i].parent < i, then apply the same permutation to the tracks — do both in one pass or every joint is animated by its neighbour; (2) a channel targets a node, and nodes that are not joints are legal and must be dropped; (3) the duration is not in the file — it is the maximum input time across all samplers, and §6.1 is about why that is not quite the right answer; (4) rotations arrive in xyzw order and this engine stores wxyz, which a blind memcpy turns into a 180° error about a diagonal axis. The test: validate the imported clip, and expect sign_flips to be non-zero — real exporters produce them, and finding that it is not zero is evidence your importer read the file rather than your own bake.

15. Recap & next

The engine can play recorded motion. Concretely, it gained:

  • A clip format — independent per-channel tracks with their own absolute key times, where an empty channel means the bind value exactly and costs nothing.
  • A sampler that finds the bracketing interval with a remembered cursor, falls back to a binary search on any jump, and is immune to a corrupt cursor.
  • Three interpolation rules, one per field, each with a measurement, a failure mode or a specification behind it — including the scale decision Lesson 7.5 left open.
  • Correct looping, with the duration authored rather than derived, the seam measured, and root motion separated from it.
  • Cross-fading in pose space, which preserves every bone length exactly, and a report that tells you whether nlerp is the right rule for the blend you are doing.
  • Load-time canonicalisation and keyframe reduction, which together take a one-second walk from 37,076 bytes to 3,716.

And three things worth carrying forward that are not code.

The seam held where it was drawn, and did not pretend to cover more. Lesson 7.6's prediction — that a clip sampler would replace build_pose and nothing else in the demo would change — was exactly right for the sampler, because everything downstream takes a span of transform. It was not right for M, the matrix-blend path, and it could not have been: that is a different operation on a different type, and a span cannot hide a change of what is being averaged. An abstraction that holds is one that is honest about its edges.

A benchmark with two variables in it has none. §4.1's first table varied the wrap period by changing the step size and confidently reported 43% for the wrong cause. §9.4's first timing had two confounds, and fixing the first one left the number still backwards. Both were found by noticing a result that contradicted a simpler fact — a longer track cannot be faster to search — rather than by suspecting the instrument.

A lossy transformation must be fitted with its consumer's exact reconstruction. §9.3 is about quaternions, and the rule is not. Whatever you throw data away against, the thing that reads it back is the thing you owe the tolerance to.

Next: Lesson 7.8 — SDL3 Audio: Streams, Mixing, and 3D Sound. Module 7 closes with the sense this engine has never addressed. SDL3's audio model is a stream you push into and a callback that pulls, with resampling and format conversion handled at the boundary; on top of it goes a small mixer, and on top of that, spatialization — attenuation with distance and panning with direction, which is the first time this course will use a listener's transform for something other than drawing. Two of this lesson's ideas come along unchanged: a sound is an asset and its playhead is not, and an attenuation curve is another lossy reconstruction that has to agree with what plays it back.
