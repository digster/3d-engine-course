Module 6 — Advanced Rendering · Lesson 6.18

Text and 2D Overlay Rendering

This engine can light a surface with a microfacet BRDF, sample an irradiance cube, cascade a shadow map, bloom its highlights, tonemap to a display and deduce its own frame order. It cannot put a single character on the screen. Every number the last seventeen lessons measured went to a terminal, which means the picture and the measurement have never once been on the same surface. Fixing that turns out to be three problems and only one of them is about drawing. The first is arithmetic: a pen, a baseline, a bearing, an advance, and the question of what to round — where we find that snapping a glyph's position has an error bounded at half a pixel forever while rounding its advance drifts 4.32 px by the end of one line, from the same rounding applied to a different quantity. The second is packing: ninety-five glyphs into one texture, with the padding argument from Lesson 6.10 arriving at magnification instead of minification — and an occupancy figure that looks like a verdict on the packer and is really a fact about the font. The third is the one this course has been building toward for eighteen lessons. Compositing a glyph is over, over is a lerp, and a lerp must happen in linear light. Get it wrong and light text on dark looks thin while dark text on light looks fat — 62.2% and 137.8% of the correct ink, measured — and both are blamed on the font. Then a second, entirely separate bug (uploading a coverage atlas as _SRGB) turns out to produce the identical number on a dark background, because it is the same function applied in a different file. The two are told apart by one test, which this lesson derives. And the overlay is declared as a frame-graph pass — the first external use of Lesson 6.17's API — with an honest report of what that did and did not catch.

1. The problem: an engine with nothing to say

Count what this engine can do. It imports glTF and OBJ. It evaluates a Cook–Torrance BRDF with a GGX distribution, a Smith geometry term and a Schlick Fresnel. It renders four shadow cascades with percentage-closer filtering. It prefilters an environment map into a mip chain and splits the specular integral in two. It runs a six-level bloom pyramid, an ACES tonemapper, 4× MSAA, a frustum culler, an instanced submission path and — as of last lesson — a frame graph that deduces its own pass order.

Now count what it can tell you. Nothing. Every measurement in this module went to stdout: the cull rate in 6.16, the compile time in 6.17, the convergence of the split-sum approximation in 6.15. You have been reading numbers in one window and looking at a picture in another, and correlating them by hand. That is not a missing feature, it is a missing sense.

There is a second absence, and it is older. Module 1 owned the 2D case completely — a framebuffer, put_pixel, fill_rect, a blit — and every one of those was written by hand and understood to the byte. Then Module 4 moved the renderer to the GPU and the 2D path did not come along, because nothing needed it. Every demo since has been a camera looking at a mesh. The engine has been three-dimensional-only for fourteen lessons, and a three-dimensional-only engine cannot draw a health bar, a crosshair, a menu, a subtitle or a frame-time graph.

What about Dear ImGui?

Lesson 5.11 integrated Dear ImGui and that integration stands. But read what it is for: ImGui is tooling — an inspector, a profiler window, a set of sliders for a developer. Its own documentation is emphatic that it is not a game UI library, and the reasons are structural rather than stylistic: immediate-mode widgets own their layout, their font and their style, and a shipped game's HUD needs to be laid out by an artist, localised, and rendered in the game's own colour pipeline. An engine that shipped ImGui as its UI would be shipping a debug tool to players.

There is also a narrower reason that matters for this lesson specifically. ImGui bakes its own atlas and composites in its own way, and both of those decisions are exactly the ones we are about to examine. Using it here would mean reading somebody else's answers instead of deriving them.

2. What we build, and what we borrow

This course applies one test to every third-party library, stated once in image.hpp at Lesson 4.7 and applied every time since: is the hard part the subject? The OBJ parser was written by hand because the hard part of OBJ is the mismatch between how a file describes a vertex and how hardware fetches one, and that is the subject. The PNG decoder was not, because the hard part of PNG is DEFLATE and five scanline filter modes, and there is nothing about an engine in the fifth filter mode.

Fonts fall on the second side, and it is worth being specific, because "fonts are hard" is not an argument. Turning an outline into coverage is a weekend's work: quadratic B-splines, flatten to line segments, scanline fill under the non-zero winding rule, antialias by exact area. That part is graphics, and if it were the whole job we would write it. It is not. The rest of the job is file format:

  • cmap subtable formats 0, 4, 6, 12 and 13 — five different encodings of "which glyph is this character", any of which a font in the wild may carry, sometimes several at once under different platform ids.
  • Composite glyphs: glyf entries that are other glyphs under a 2×2 affine transform with a point-matching rule, nested.
  • Hinting: a stack-based bytecode virtual machine, executed per glyph, whose job is to move control points onto the pixel grid at small sizes.
  • GPOS: a lookup-table language for pair positioning with class-based coverage, which is where every font shipped in the last fifteen years puts its kerning.

So stb_truetype does the decode and the rasterization, in exactly one translation unit, exactly as stb_image and cgltf do — and font.hpp names no third-party type, which is the receipt that the isolation worked.

Three things we do write, because all three are the subject:

  1. The atlas packer. Where glyphs land in one texture, and why the padding between them is not optional — Lesson 6.10's bleed argument arriving in a place where it bites at magnification rather than minification.
  2. The layout. Pen, baseline, bearing, advance, kerning, and the question of what to round. This is where text is won or lost.
  3. The compositing. Which is a blend equation and a colour space, and this course has spent six lessons earning opinions about both.

Figure 1 shows the shape the three files take, and the shape is load-bearing rather than tidy. font.hpp produces a grid of coverage bytes and some metrics. overlay.hpp turns strings and rectangles into quads in pixel space. Neither of them mentions SDL_GPU. Only the third file does — and that is why hello_cube, a two-hundred-line demo with no GPU device at all, gets text by adding twenty lines, and why §11 can render the same batch two ways and compare every channel.

3. The anatomy of a glyph

Start with what a font actually stores, because almost every early text bug is a confusion between two rectangles that happen to have the same size.

A glyph, rasterized at a size, is a small bitmap of coverage: one number per texel saying what fraction of that texel is inside the outline. Zero is outside, 255 is fully inside, and everything between is an edge. That bitmap has a position in the atlas — a rectangle in texels — and it has a position on the line, which is an entirely separate rectangle described relative to a moving point called the pen.

The pen sits on the baseline, the invisible line letters stand on. Three numbers carry the glyph from the pen to the page:

  • offset_x, the left side bearing: how far right of the pen the ink starts. Usually small and positive; negative for glyphs that lean out of their own box.
  • offset_y: how far below the pen the ink starts, with +y pointing down. Almost always negative, because a letter's ink is above the baseline. This is the single most surprising number in the file.
  • advance: how far the pen moves afterwards. Not the width of the ink.

Here are four real glyphs from the font this engine ships — Karla, at a requested 16 px — printed by the harness rather than transcribed from a drawing:

glyphink wink hoffset_x offset_yadvance
H79+1.0−9.09.1565
g8120.0−8.07.7331
j514−2.0−10.0 4.2977
.330.0−2.02.4500

Read the row for j. Its advance is 4.2977 px and its ink is 5 px wide, starting 2 px left of the pen. A j therefore draws outside its own advance box in both directions. Any code that sizes a background panel from a string's advance width and then clips to it will clip the descender off the j in "adjust", and the bug will look like a font problem.

That is why text_metrics reports two widths. width is where the pen ended up; ink_x0 and ink_x1 are where the pixels actually went. For the string "AVIi" the harness measures an advance width of 23.1172 px and an ink extent of 22.0248 px, and those are different numbers for a reason, not by rounding.

3.1 The size you asked for is not the size you got

Ask stb_truetype for a "16 px" font and it scales the outlines so that ascent minus descent is 16 px. That is what stbtt_ScaleForPixelHeight means, and it is what a UI toolkit means by a 16 px font. It is not the em square.

Karla's hhea table says ascent 917 and descent −252 in a 1000-unit em. So ascent − descent is 1169 units, which is 1.169 em, and:

em square = 16 px × 1000 / 1169 = 13.6869 px

A font whose ascent and descent happened to sum to exactly one em would give 16 px for the same request, and its glyphs would look noticeably larger beside Karla's at the same nominal size. This is the whole explanation for "why does 14 pt look different in every application", and it is not a bug in any of them. The harness asserts both numbers.

While we are in the vertical metrics: Karla's suggested line_gap is zero . A great many fonts report zero here, so a renderer that computes line spacing as line_gap alone produces lines that touch. The correct expression is ascent − descent + line_gap, and the subtraction is not a typo — descent is negative.

3.2 Kerning, and where the data actually lives

Some pairs of letters look wrong at their nominal advances. AV is the canonical example: the diagonal of the A and the diagonal of the V leave a visible gap that neither letter is responsible for. A font ships a table of corrections, and ours moves AV together by 0.3832 px at 16 px.

The interesting part is where that table lives, because there are two mechanisms and one of them is dead. The legacy kern table is what every tutorial written before about 2010 reads. GPOS — the OpenType layout table — is where every font shipped since puts it. The font this engine ships has no kern table at all, which we verified by reading its table directory. A naive reader of kern, or a version of stb_truetype before 1.19, returns zero for every pair in it, and the symptom is "kerning does nothing" with no error anywhere to chase.

The table is sparse, and the sparsity was measured rather than assumed. Over printable ASCII there are 95 × 95 = 9,025 ordered pairs; 186 of them are non-zero. That is 2.06%, so a dense table would be 97.9% zeros — 36,100 bytes against 2,232 for a sorted list of pairs, and the dense form grows as the square of the character set the moment anybody wants Cyrillic.

3.3 Digits are not tabular, and a HUD is the case where that matters

Here is a thing about proportional fonts that almost no text-rendering tutorial mentions and that bites immediately when the first thing you draw is a frame-time readout. Karla's digit advances at 16 px:

01234567 89
8.1304.5307.9388.0897.651 7.7337.8436.4338.418 7.952

The spread from 1 to 8 is 3.887 px — on a 16 px font, nearly a quarter of the line height. So "11.1 ms" measures 39.2951 px and "88.8 ms" measures 50.9564 px, and a counter that moves between them changes width by 11.66 px, sixty times a second. The effect is that your frame-time display appears to breathe, and if it is right-aligned, every character in it jitters.

The fix is one option flag. text_layout_options::tabular_digits lays digits out on a fixed pitch equal to the widest digit's advance, and centres each digit inside its cell so a narrow 1 does not hug the left of its column. With it on, the two strings measure the same to the last bit — the harness asserts a difference of exactly 0.0000. Most text has no business being laid out this way; a numeric readout is precisely the case that does.

Why not just use a monospace font?

You can, and for a debug overlay it is a reasonable call — the engine's own font_bake_options is happy to bake one. But notice that "monospace" solves this problem by making every glyph the same width, which is a much larger hammer than the problem needs and costs you the readability of proportional text for the labels beside the numbers. Tabular figures give you both, and most professionally-drawn fonts ship a tabular variant of the digits for exactly this reason, selectable through an OpenType feature that we do not read and stb_truetype does not expose. Our version computes the pitch from the widest digit, which is the same idea implemented at the layout instead of in the font.

4. What to round, and when

A glyph's bitmap is a grid of texels. The screen is a grid of pixels. If those two grids line up — one texel per pixel, both on integer boundaries — the blit copies coverage values directly and the text is exactly as crisp as the rasterizer made it. If they do not line up, the sampler interpolates, and interpolation is a low-pass filter applied to an image whose entire value is its high frequencies. The text goes soft.

So glyph quads should land on integer pixel boundaries. The question is how you achieve that, and there are two ways that look equivalent and are not.

Snap the position: let the pen accumulate in floating point, and round each glyph's top-left corner as you emit its quad.
Round the advance: round each glyph's advance to a whole number of pixels, so the pen is always integral.

The second is tempting because it is simpler — if the pen is always an integer, nothing else needs rounding. It is also wrong, and the reason is that the two roundings apply to different quantities and therefore compose differently. Snapping the position discards the fraction for display only; the pen keeps it, so the error at glyph n is independent of the error at glyph n−1 and is bounded by half a pixel forever. Rounding the advance discards the fraction from the accumulator, so each error is added to the next one. It is a random walk.

Figure 3 is that prediction, measured over a real line — 45 glyphs of "the quick brown fox jumps over the lazy dog, and again." at 16 px. The green series never leaves the shaded half-pixel band, worst case 0.4765 px. The red series wanders down past four pixels by glyph 25 and is still 4.3216 px out at the end of the line — which reads as a word that has drifted away from the column you carefully lined it up with, and which gets worse the longer the line.

The implementation is three lines, and they are worth reading for what is not in them:

x is the pen and it is never touched. qx is a local derived from it and thrown away. That is the entire difference between the two curves in Figure 3.

The half-texel offset that fixes the wrong thing

There is a famous "fix" for blurry text, usually written as adding 0.5 to the texture coordinates or subtracting 0.5 from the vertex positions. It comes from Direct3D 9's pixel-centre convention and it survives in tutorials thirty years later. It is correct for a renderer whose pixel centres are at integers — and wrong for every modern API, where they are at half-integers, including SDL_GPU.

Our quads carry no offset at all: a glyph spans exactly width() pixels and exactly width() texels, and the half-open texel range divided by the atlas size puts the sampler's pixel centres exactly on texel centres. If your text is blurry, the question to ask is not "am I missing a half-texel" but "is my quad on the pixel grid" — and if the answer is no, adding half a texel moves the blur around rather than removing it.

5. The atlas, and measuring a packer honestly

Ninety-five glyphs is ninety-five little bitmaps, and ninety-five textures would be ninety-five binds and ninety-five draw calls for one line of text. So they go in one texture, and the question is where.

We use shelf packing: sort the rectangles by descending height, then lay them left to right in rows whose height is set by the first rectangle placed on them. It is a simple algorithm with a known weakness — the waste is the vertical slack under short rectangles sitting on a tall shelf — and it is worth saying why that weakness barely applies here. Shelf packing is bad when heights vary wildly and good when they cluster, and glyph heights at one size cluster hard: every printable ASCII glyph in Karla at 16 px is between 3 and 14 texels tall, and sorting puts the 14s together and the 3s together.

Figure 4 is the result: six shelves, descending in height from 14 px to 7 px, holding 17, 14, 17, 15, 16 and 15 glyphs respectively. Sixty-seven of the atlas's 128 rows are used and the rest are untouched.

5.1 The measurement that measures the wrong thing

The obvious way to judge a packer is to ask what fraction of the atlas is glyph. Ours reports:

7,145 texels of glyph ÷ (128 × 128) = 43.6%

That number sounds like a verdict and is not one. Look at the denominator: the atlas is a power of two, so its size was decided before the packer ran — by the observation that 7,145 texels will not fit in 64 × 64 = 4,096. The numerator is the total area of the glyphs plus their padding, which is a property of the font at this size. Neither half of that fraction depends on anything the packer did. A perfect packer scores 43.6% and a hopeless one that put every glyph on its own shelf would score 43.6% too.

The honest question is: of the space the packer reached for, how much did it use? That is the glyph area over the rows it actually touched:

7,145 ÷ (128 × 67) = 83.3%

Now both halves move when the algorithm changes. Put every glyph on its own shelf and the denominator explodes; sort ascending instead of descending and the slack grows. 83.3% says shelf packing wastes about a sixth of the rows it uses, which for an algorithm that is fifteen lines is a good trade, and it says so in a way that could have come out badly.

A check that passed on infinity

This section shipped its first draft against a stale library, where used_height was still zero. The denominator was therefore zero, efficiency was inf, and the assertion efficiency > 80.0 is true for infinity. The harness printed shelf efficiency = inf% and reported a PASS.

That is Lesson 6.16's golden_615 finding — a comparison that printed identical=YES when both of its file reads failed — arriving in a division instead of a comparison. A check whose degenerate case is a pass is not a check. The version in the listings asserts that the footprint is plausible before it divides by it, and asserts std::isfinite on the result.

5.2 Padding, and 6.10's argument in a new place

Glyphs get one texel of empty space around them. The reason is the same one Lesson 6.10 gave for atlas padding under minification, arriving at the other end of the scale: a bilinear tap at the edge of a rectangle reads half of the texel outside it. Pack the next glyph flush against this one and that texel belongs to a neighbour, so a fraction of the wrong letter bleeds into this one's edge.

The harness bakes a second atlas with padding = 0 and counts, for every glyph, how many of the texels immediately right of its rectangle carry somebody else's ink:

paddingedge-adjacent texels carrying a neighbour's ink
10 of 842
0398 of 842 — 47.3%

Nearly half the edge texels. And here is the honest qualifier, which matters more than the number: at a 1:1 blit with nearest filtering, none of this happens. A nearest fetch at a pixel centre reads exactly one texel, so an unpadded atlas draws perfectly today. The padding is insurance against the first person who scales the overlay, rotates it, or turns on mip generation — and it costs 1,559 texels in an atlas that had 9,239 spare rows' worth.

Which sets the rule for a mipped atlas, because it is the same argument scaled: a level-N texel spans 2N of level 0's, so a chain L levels deep needs 2L texels of padding. One texel buys you level 0 and nothing else, and font_atlas::padding is reported precisely so a consumer can work out how deep a chain it is allowed to build.

5.3 One texel that is entirely covered

A HUD is not only text. There is a panel behind the readout, a divider under the heading, a bar that fills up. Those are untextured rectangles, and drawing them looks like it needs a second pipeline with no texture bound — a state change between the panel and the words on top of it.

It does not, because a solid quad is a textured quad whose coverage happens to be 1 everywhere. The bake reserves a 2 × 2 block of fully-covered texels at the top-left of the first shelf and reports its centre's texture coordinates, and overlay_batch::rect emits a quad with all four corners at that coordinate. The panel and the string are then in one vertex buffer, in one draw call, with nothing between them.

Two by two rather than one by one, deliberately: a bilinear tap at the exact centre of a 1 × 1 block is correct, and one bit of drift in the rasterizer's interpolation reads the transparent padding beside it, which gives the panel a faint dark edge. Two texels leave half a texel of slack in every direction. Placing it at the start of the first shelf — rather than in its own reserved strip — means that on any atlas whose tallest glyph is at least two texels high, it costs no area at all.

6. Decoding, and three rules that are security bugs

A string arrives as UTF-8 bytes and the atlas is indexed by codepoint, so something has to decode. This is twenty lines and it is worth every one of them, because the failure mode of a sloppy decoder is not a rendering bug.

The arithmetic is the easy part. A leading byte's high bits say how many continuation bytes follow — 0xxxxxxx is one byte, 110xxxxx is two, 1110xxxx three, 11110xxx four — and each continuation contributes six bits. Three rules that most hand-rolled decoders omit are the part that matters:

  1. Over-long forms. The two-byte sequence 0xC0 0xAF decodes, by the arithmetic above, to U+002F — the character /. A naive decoder returns a slash. A path check performed on the raw bytes before decoding therefore sees nothing to reject, and the slash appears after the check. This is the classic directory-traversal vector, it has shipped in web servers and in Java's own decoder, and the rule that stops it is one line: a codepoint must use the shortest sequence that can represent it.
  2. Surrogates. U+D800 through U+DFFF are UTF-16 machinery — the mechanism by which UTF-16 represents codepoints above the basic plane — and are not characters. Encoded in UTF-8 they are ill-formed; the practice of doing it anyway has a name, CESU-8, and it is a compatibility hack rather than a format.
  3. Beyond U+10FFFF. The four-byte form can express up to U+1FFFFF. Unicode stops at U+10FFFF, and the extra range is not reserved for later, it is invalid.

And a fourth that is not about validity at all: a malformed byte must still advance the pointer. A decoder that returns U+FFFD without consuming anything is an infinite loop, which is the other way this function can become a security bug. The harness feeds it five consecutive malformed bytes and asserts that five calls consume all five.

7. Stem weight: the colour space of a glyph

Now the part this course has been building toward since Lesson 1.6.

Compositing a glyph over a background is Porter and Duff's over operator:

result = source × a + destination × (1 − a)

where a is the glyph's coverage at that pixel. Lesson 6.11 established the key fact about that expression: it is a lerp, and a lerp between two light values must happen in linear light. Lerping sRGB codes does not lerp the light they encode. Lesson 6.11 measured the single-pixel case — half-coverage white over black gives 0.5 of the light correctly and 0.2159 incorrectly, 43% — and this lesson is where that number becomes an artefact with a name.

7.1 Why text is the worst case

A 16 px glyph is mostly edge. Its stems are one to two pixels wide, so a large fraction of its pixels are partially covered — and partially covered pixels are exactly where the two blend spaces disagree. Worse, the disagreement is not uniform: it is largest where the coverage is lowest.

Figure 5's left half is one pixel of white over black at every tenth of coverage. Read the ratios:

coveragecorrect lightlight from lerping codesratio
0.100.09990.010310.3%
0.200.20160.033116.4%
0.500.50290.215942.9%
0.800.79910.603875.6%
1.001.00001.0000100.0%

A tenth-covered pixel emits a tenth of the light it should. The faintest parts of a glyph — the tapers, the joins, the antialiased flanks of every stem — very nearly disappear. That is why this bug presents as weight rather than as a colour shift: the interior of a stem is fully covered and comes out right, and everything around it is wrong.

7.2 The measurement: ink mass

To put a number on a whole string we need a measure of how much ink is on the page. The harness uses ink mass: the total light the text adds, summed over every pixel, in linear light.

mass = Σpixels ( luminance(pixel) − luminance(background) )

Linear light rather than code values, deliberately — the question is "does this text emit the right amount of light", and summing sRGB codes would beg exactly the question under test. Rendering "Handgloves 0123" at 16 px, white on black:

blend spaceink massshare of correct
linear (correct)253.04100.0%
sRGB codes157.2862.2%

Thirty-eight percent of the light, gone. And now the same string, black on white — where ink mass is negative, because the text removes light from the page, so we take the magnitude:

blend spaceink massshare of correct
linear (correct)252.73100.0%
sRGB codes348.37137.8%

The same error, in opposite directions. Light text on dark gets thinner; dark text on light gets fatter. That asymmetry is the reason this bug is so durable: half the people looking at it see text that is too spindly and half see text that is too heavy, both conclude the font is wrong, and neither suspects the compositor.

7.3 Two bugs that are the same function

There is a second, entirely independent way to get this wrong, and it lives in a different file. Upload the coverage atlas as an _SRGB texture and the sampler will "decode" every texel on the way out — applying an inverse gamma to an area fraction, which is a category error, since coverage was never encoded through anything. The measured result:

bugink mass, white on blackshare
lerping sRGB codes157.276362.2%
_SRGB coverage atlas157.233862.1%

That is not a coincidence, and noticing it is the most useful thing in this section. Work both out for white over black:

lerping codes: code = 255a, so light = srgb_to_linear(a)
_SRGB atlas: a′ = srgb_to_linear(a), and the linear blend emits a′

They are the same function, applied at different points in the pipeline. One is a colour-space error in the blend equation; the other is a format error on a texture; they are in different files, written by different people, on different days — and on a dark background they are numerically indistinguishable. The remaining 0.04 of difference is the 8-bit quantisation of the modified atlas.

So measuring light-on-dark text cannot tell you which one you have. Figure 5's right-hand table is the test that can:

bugwhite on blackblack on white
lerping sRGB codes62.2%137.8%
_SRGB coverage atlas62.1%62.1%

Render your text both ways round. A blend-space bug fattens dark-on-light, because it corrupts the lerp and the lerp is directional. An atlas-format bug thins both, because it corrupts the coverage before the lerp ever sees it and coverage does not know which way the contrast runs. That is a diagnostic you can run in five minutes on any text renderer, including one you did not write.

7.4 The part that is usually left out: text is tuned for the wrong arithmetic

Everything above says linear compositing is correct, and it is. Here is the complication, and leaving it out is how you end up in an argument with a type designer.

Outlines are drawn, and hinted, and their coverage tuned, against what the renderer of the day actually did — and for thirty years the renderer of the day blended in sRGB space. So switching to correct blending makes light-on-dark text measurably lighter than its designer intended it to look. The correct answer is not to go back to the wrong arithmetic. It is stem darkening: a deliberate adjustment to coverage, applied before compositing, that every serious text stack has. FreeType calls it stem darkening, Skia folds it into gamma and contrast, and macOS's historical "font smoothing" was a version of it.

Ours is the simplest honest form — a power curve, a′ = a^(1−k), which pushes partially-covered pixels up and leaves 0 and 1 exactly where they are. At k = 0.2 a half-covered pixel becomes 0.5743, a 15% gain in coverage where it matters and none at the ends. Measured over the whole string:

casek = 0k = 0.2change
white on black253.04281.59+11.3%
black on white252.73281.34+11.3%

And there is the reason it is a parameter and not the default. The correction is identical in both directions, because it is a function of coverage and coverage does not know which way the contrast runs. It helps light-on-dark, where correct blending thinned the glyph — and it hurts dark-on-light by exactly the same 11.3%, where correct blending already gives the right weight. One global constant cannot be right for both. A real implementation varies k with the contrast direction and the point size; ours exposes the knob, defaults it to zero, and says what it would take to set it properly.

7.5 Where the blend actually happens, and why the format decides

On the GPU, none of this arithmetic is in our shader. Hardware blending happens after the fragment shader, in the raster operations stage, on whatever is in the target — so what decides the blend space is the colour target's format:

  • an _SRGB target: the ROP decodes the destination, lerps in linear light, and re-encodes. Correct, and free.
  • a plain UNORM target with a shader that encodes its own output: the ROP lerps sRGB codes. That is blend_over_encoded, in silicon.

Lesson 6.1 predicted this in a comment — "hardware blending happens after the shader, and if we encode there, the blender adds codes" — and Lesson 6.11 put the 43% on it. This lesson can finally measure it on real hardware, because this engine still supports both paths: gpu_device asks for an _SRGB swapchain and falls back to UNORM plus a shader-side encode when SDR_LINEAR composition is unavailable.

So §11 draws the same batch twice — once at an _SRGB target, once at a UNORM one with encode = 1 — and compares each against the two CPU implementations:

the UNORM target's own blender, compared against…pixels differing
blend_over_encoded (the wrong one)0
blend_over (the right one)933

Zero. Not "close to" — the hardware fallback path reproduces our deliberately-wrong CPU function exactly, pixel for pixel, on a Metal backend. A prediction written in a comment seventeen lessons ago, discharged with an equality.

8. Before the curve or after it

The engine's frame now ends: scene → HDR float target → bloom → tonemap → swapchain. An overlay could composite at two points in that chain, and this is not a matter of taste.

Composite into the HDR target, before the tonemap, and the text goes through the curve with everything else. Pure white at linear 1.0 comes out of the ACES fit at:

ACES(1.0) = 0.803797 → sRGB code 232

Your white UI is grey. Not dramatically — 232 rather than 255 — which is worse than dramatically, because it looks like a design choice. And it is grey by an amount that changes with the exposure:

exposure0.25×0.5×1×2×4×
white becomes code165206232245252

A HUD that dims when the player walks into a dark room is not a stylistic decision, it is that table. It would also bloom, because the bloom pass runs between the two — which some games want deliberately (a holographic visor, a diegetic UI drawn on glass) and which no debug overlay wants ever.

Composite into the swapchain, after the tonemap, and white is 255 and stays 255. That is the right answer, and the reason is the line Lesson 6.12 drew through the whole module: a UI colour is display-referred. It is a code somebody picked in a colour picker, not a quantity of light arriving from a surface. Scene-referred values belong before the tonemap; display-referred values belong after it. Text is the first thing in this course that is display-referred from birth.

The exception, stated so you know it is a choice

Diegetic UI — an interface that exists inside the world, on a screen the character is looking at — should composite before the tonemap, because it is light in the scene. It should bloom, it should be exposed, and it should dim when the room is bright, because a real monitor does. The rule is not "always after"; it is "decide whether this is light or a code", and then put it on the matching side of the curve. Our overlay is a code.

9. Implementation: quads, a pipeline, one draw call

Everything above is arithmetic. What reaches the GPU is twenty bytes per vertex and one draw call, and three decisions in that are worth reading.

9.1 The colour is four bytes, not a word

Everywhere else in this engine a colour is a packed Uint32 in ARGB order — pack_argb, since Lesson 1.6. A vertex attribute is not a word. SDL_GPU_VERTEXELEMENTFORMAT_UBYTE4_NORM hands the shader the four bytes in memory order, so a packed ARGB word arrives on a little-endian machine as (B, G, R, A) and the text comes out blue.

This is not hypothetical. Lesson 6.15 shipped exactly this bug in a BRDF lookup table, and it survived review because green occupies bits 8–15 in both layouts, so half the data read correctly and the result was merely dim. So overlay_vertex names its four colour bytes:

Naming the bytes makes the endianness question disappear rather than making it catchable, which is a strictly better outcome than a test. set_colour is the one place the ARGB convention is unpacked, and there is nothing left to get wrong.

9.2 The atlas is one channel, and never _SRGB

gpu_texture gained a new entry point rather than a flag on the old one:

Every argument create_sampled makes is about colour: four channels because a picture has three plus alpha, and an srgb flag because a colour is stored through a transfer function. A glyph atlas is neither. It is one byte of coverage per texel, linear by construction, and there is no flag combination on the four-channel path that expresses that — create_sampled(..., srgb=false) would store the right numbers in four times the memory and leave the next reader unable to tell whether the choice was considered.

The 4× is real (16,384 bytes against 65,536 for our atlas) but it is the smaller half of the argument. The larger half is that a distinct entry point with "coverage" in its name is the documentation, and §7.3 is what happens when it is missing.

9.3 One pipeline, blending on, depth off

The pipeline is blend(false) — straight alpha — and nothing else. Straight rather than premultiplied because there is nothing in a coverage atlas to have been multiplied: the shader produces (tint, coverage × vertex_alpha) and the ROP does the rest.

No depth state at all, and it is worth stating the absence rather than leaving it as one. The overlay is drawn last, into a target with no depth attachment, in declaration order — and declaration order is the sort. That is the correct answer for a HUD and it is why overlay_batch has no sort key: Lesson 6.11's order_draws sorts transparent geometry back to front because depth decides what is in front of what; here the programmer decides, and a sort would be second-guessing them.

Culling stays at the course default (counter-clockwise front, back faces culled) rather than being disabled, which turns the winding in push_quad into something the hardware checks: get it backwards and the overlay is invisible on the GPU while remaining perfect on the CPU, which is precisely a divergence §11's comparison exists to catch.

9.4 The flip lives in one line

Pixel space has +y down; SDL_GPU's clip space has +y up. That conversion happens exactly once, in the vertex shader:

No projection matrix. An orthographic matrix would do the same job in four dot products and would invite somebody to put a camera in it; an overlay has no camera, it has a viewport. And inv_half_viewport is 2/width and 2/height, computed on the CPU once per frame rather than divided per vertex — which is the entire reason this is a shader instead of arithmetic baked into the vertices, since a window resize then pushes eight bytes rather than rewriting every quad.

10. The overlay as a frame-graph pass

Lesson 6.17 built a frame graph and ended by predicting that this lesson would be its API's first user from outside. It named the thing that had never been exercised: keep against an imported resource. Here is that prediction cashed.

The overlay reads nothing and writes the swapchain, which the resolve has just written. In the graph's vocabulary that is one line:

keep means "my output depends on what was already in this target", which for an alpha blend is simply true — the destination is an operand of over. That is a statement about arithmetic, and a pass author can answer it without knowing what a load op is.

From that one word the graph derives four things, and the harness reads them back rather than trusting them:

passresourceload opstore op
resolve (stand-in)backbufferCLEARSTORE
overlaybackbufferLOADSTORE

Note which row the interesting deduction is on. The overlay said keep, and the consequence landed on the resolve — a different pass, in a different file, which must now STORE because somebody downstream reads the version it produced. That is exactly the class of fact humans get wrong across a file boundary, and it is the strongest argument for the whole apparatus.

10.1 What had never been run

6.17's own header says that version 0 of an import is a real value, not undefined — "whatever is in the texture when the frame begins" — so keep on it should be legal where keep on version 0 of a transient is an error the compiler catches. That branch had never executed. The harness exercises it directly, with an overlay and no resolve in front of it:

It works, and it derives LOAD, and the pass survives culling because writing an import is what makes a pass a root. Four checks, all green, on code that had been written and never run. That is the value of a lesson using the previous lesson's API in earnest rather than in its own test.

10.2 And the honest half: what it does not catch

Declare the overlay with discard_write instead — "I write every texel and read none", which for a blend is a lie — and the graph compiles it happily and derives DONT_CARE, which would erase the entire frame under the text. The harness asserts that too, as a pass:

check(wrong.compile(r.gpu), "discard_write also compiles — this is NOT caught");

So the graph does not verify dataflow claims; it relocates them. A load op buried in a struct three files away becomes a sentence about arithmetic at the call site, which a pass author can answer correctly without understanding render passes. It cannot check that the answer is true. Saying so is the point — a tool oversold is a tool that gets trusted in the one case it does not cover.

11. The instrument, and proving it can fail

The characterization shot is a null instrument for this lesson, and we know that structurally rather than hopefully: write_reference_shot renders through soft_renderer.cpp and raster.cpp, neither of which includes font.hpp, overlay.hpp or gpu_overlay.hpp, and demo_scene.cpp draws no text. It is run anyway — byte-identical for the twenty-seventh lesson, hash E917C06C — and what that proves is that this lesson broke nothing else, which is a different and smaller claim.

The real test had to be built, and the shape to copy is 6.17's: render the same thing two ways, and include a control that proves the comparison can report a difference. Here the two ways are the two renderers, which is available only because overlay.hpp knows nothing about SDL_GPU.

One batch — a panel and two lines of text, 28 quads — composited by composite_overlay into a framebuffer, and recorded by gpu_overlay into an _SRGB render target, then read back:

Note what is not claimed. Lesson 6.17 demanded bit-equality and got it, because both sides of that comparison were the same shader. Here they are not: the CPU decodes bytes to float, lerps, and re-encodes with engine::linear_to_srgb; the GPU's ROP does it in fixed-function hardware whose intermediate precision is not specified anywhere. One code is the honest claim, and the instrument reports the distribution rather than a yes/no — because a comparison that can only say "same" or "different" cannot tell a rounding difference from a bug.

And the control. The same comparison, against a CPU render composited in the deliberately wrong blend space, reports 934 differing pixels, worst difference 53 codes. The instrument can fail, so the 68 means something. That control exists because one lesson ago we found a golden that printed identical=YES when both of its file reads failed, and because this very lesson shipped a draft with a check that passed on infinity (§5.1). 12. The ninety-percent picture

What this lesson built is a Latin debug-text system, and the distance between that and "text" is enormous. Naming the gap:

  • No shaping. One codepoint becomes one glyph, in logical order. That is wrong for Arabic, where a letter's shape depends on its position in the word; wrong for Devanagari, which reorders and forms conjuncts; wrong for any font with ligatures; and wrong for combining marks. The library is HarfBuzz, it is larger than this engine's renderer, and it is the right answer the moment you need it.
  • No bidi. Mixed right-to-left and left-to-right runs need the Unicode bidirectional algorithm (UAX #9) applied before layout can begin.
  • No line breaking beyond an explicit \n. Real wrapping needs UAX #14 break opportunities; breaking at spaces is wrong in most of the world.
  • No subpixel antialiasing. We produce one coverage value per texel; ClearType-style rendering produces three, one per LCD subpixel, and is dependent on the display's stripe orientation.
  • No signed distance fields. An atlas baked at one size is crisp at that size and soft elsewhere. SDF text (Green, 2007) stores distance rather than coverage, so a bilinear tap plus a threshold reconstructs a sharp edge at any scale — and it is the right answer for text that scales, rotates, or needs an outline or a drop shadow. It is not free: corners round off, which multi-channel SDF fixes at 3× the memory, and the threshold has to move with the scale to keep the weight constant.
  • One font, one size, one style per atlas. No fallback chain, so a codepoint this atlas does not carry is counted as missing rather than silently skipped.

Two smaller ones worth knowing. stb_truetype reads a useful subset of GPOS pair positioning — enough for the 186 pairs in our font, not enough to be called an OpenType layout engine. And our glyphs are rasterized unhinted: hinting is a bytecode VM that moves outline points onto the pixel grid at small sizes, and skipping it costs real crispness below about 12 px. Both are reasonable places to stop for an engine whose text is a HUD; neither is a place to stop for a word processor.

15. Common pitfalls & debugging

The text is a solid block of colour

Symptom: every glyph is a filled rectangle in the right place and the right size. Cause: the shader is reading the wrong channel of the atlas, or the atlas is bound but the sampler is not, and the fetch returns 1. Fix: an R8_UNORM texture read through Texture2D<float4> gives you (r, 0, 0, 1) — the alpha is one, not the coverage. Declare it as Texture2D<float> and use .r, as the listing does.

The text is upside down

Symptom: correct on the software renderer, mirrored vertically on the GPU, or vice versa. Cause: the pixel-space-to-clip-space flip has been applied twice or zero times. Fix: there is exactly one 1.0f - in the vertex shader and no flip anywhere else. If you added one in the layout so that a quad's y0 is below its y1, remove it: the layout works in pixel space, +y down, and says so.

Every glyph has a faint border of the letter beside it

Symptom: ghosting, worse on tall letters. Cause: insufficient atlas padding for the filtering in use. Fix: §5.2. One texel covers bilinear at level 0; a chain L levels deep needs 2L.

The text is blurry and moving the quad by half a pixel does not fix it

Symptom: soft, slightly doubled glyphs. Cause: the quad is not on the pixel grid, or the viewport the batch was laid out for is not the viewport it was drawn into. Fix: check snap_to_pixel first, then check that overlay_batch::begin was given the same size as the render target. gpu_overlay::record reads the viewport from the batch rather than from the device precisely so that a mismatch is impossible — but a batch built for last frame's size and drawn this frame will still stretch.

Light text looks thin, or dark text looks fat

Symptom: the weight is wrong and the font is not. Cause: §7. Fix: run the diagnostic. Render the same string white-on-black and black-on-white and compare the ink. If one is thin and the other fat, the blend space is wrong — look at the colour target's format. If both are thin, the atlas is being decoded — look at the texture's format.

The frame-time readout jitters horizontally

Symptom: a right-aligned number whose characters shuffle every frame. Cause: proportional digit advances. Fix: text_layout_options::tabular_digits, §3.3.

A string with a j or an f is clipped on the left

Symptom: one letter has a sliver missing. Cause: a background or a scissor rectangle sized from text_metrics::width, which is the advance width. Fix: use ink_x0/ink_x1 when you need the extent of the pixels — they differ, and §3 says by how much.

Kerning does nothing

Symptom: AV looks loose, and font_atlas::kerning is empty. Cause: the font has no legacy kern table and your reader only knows about that one. Fix: §3.2 — use a stb_truetype at 1.19 or later, which reads GPOS. Check font_atlas::kerning.size() after a bake; zero on a modern font is the signal.

The overlay erases the frame under it

Symptom: the 3D scene disappears and only the HUD is left, on a black field. Cause: the pass declared discard_write (or set SDL_GPU_LOADOP_DONT_CARE by hand) on a target it actually blends into. Fix: §10.2 — an alpha blend reads its destination, so the verb is keep.

16. Exercises

  1. Bake at a second size and look at the atlas. Change font_bake_options::pixel_height to 32 and print width, height, used_height and the shelf efficiency. Predict the atlas side before you run it.

    Hint: glyph area scales with the square of the size, so 7,145 texels becomes roughly 28,600 — which needs a 256-square atlas, since 128 × 128 is 16,384. Shelf efficiency should barely move, because the heights still cluster. If it moves a lot, look at which glyph is setting the first shelf's height.

  2. Make the wrong blend space visible. Change hello_cube's composite_overlay call to overlay_blend::encoded and take two shots — one with light text on the dark background, one with the colours swapped. Look at them side by side at 4× zoom.

    Hint: the effect is much easier to see on the light background, where the text gets heavier, because the eye is better at noticing ink appear than disappear. That is itself worth knowing: the version of this bug that ships is usually the light-on-dark one.

  3. Add a scissor rectangle. Give overlay_batch a clip rectangle that quads are cropped against, in pixel space, with the texture coordinates adjusted to match. Make it work on both renderers and extend §J's comparison to cover it.

    Hint: cropping a quad means moving a vertex and its uv by the same fraction. The arithmetic is a lerp of the uv by (new_x - x0) / (x1 - x0). The GPU alternative is SDL_SetGPUScissor, which is free but is per-draw — so using it splits the one draw call into one per clip region, and that trade is the whole reason ImGui does it in the vertex data instead. Measure both.

  4. Word wrapping. Add a max_width to text_layout_options and break lines at spaces when the pen would exceed it. Then find a string your implementation breaks wrongly.

    Hint: you will find one quickly — "don't" is fine but "e-mail" and "1,000" and any CJK text are not, because break opportunities are a property of Unicode (UAX #14) rather than of the space character. The exercise is worth doing precisely to arrive at that sentence honestly.

  5. Engine challenge: signed distance fields. Bake a second atlas holding distance rather than coverage — for each texel, the signed distance to the nearest outline, normalised into a byte — and add a shader path that thresholds it with smoothstep. Draw the same string at 1×, 4× and 16× and compare against the coverage atlas.

    Hint: stbtt_GetCodepointSDF does the bake. Three things will surprise you. The threshold must move with the scale or the weight changes; corners round off, which is the known limitation multi-channel SDF exists to fix; and the padding requirement is now set by the distance range rather than by the filter, because a texel outside the glyph still carries meaningful data. That last one is §5.2's argument arriving with a completely different constant.

17. Recap & next

The engine can say something. It bakes a real outline font into one channel of coverage, packs ninety-five glyphs onto six shelves deterministically, lays out UTF-8 with kerning and tabular figures, and composites the result in linear light — on the CPU into a framebuffer, and on the GPU in one draw call, with the two agreeing to within one sRGB code on every pixel.

What is worth carrying out of this lesson is mostly not about text.

  • The same rounding applied to two different quantities has completely different consequences. Snap a position and the error is bounded forever; round an advance and it is a random walk. Before rounding anything, ask whether the value you are rounding feeds back into itself.
  • A measurement whose denominator does not depend on the thing under test measures something else. "What fraction of the atlas is glyphs" is a fact about the font. Shelf efficiency is a fact about the packer. This is the sixth member of a family running since 6.14 — can this measurement produce a non-null result; has it converged; can this axis show the effect; can this comparison report a difference; is the reason I am building this true here; and now: does my denominator move when the code does?
  • A check whose degenerate case is a pass is not a check. inf > 80.0 is true. This is the second time in three lessons that a degenerate input has produced a green tick, and both times only an implausible printed number gave it away.
  • Two bugs in two different files can be the same function. An _SRGB coverage atlas and an sRGB-space blend agree to four significant figures on a dark background. When two independent explanations predict the same measurement, the useful move is to find the measurement that separates them — here, simply inverting the contrast.
  • A new type's job can be to make a bug impossible rather than catchable. Naming four colour bytes r, g, b, a removes the endianness question that cost 6.15 a debugging session, and there is no test to write because there is nothing left to get wrong.

Next is Module 7, and the ground shifts. Module 6 has been about light for eighteen lessons; Module 7 is about motion. It opens with rotations done properly — Euler angles and their pathologies, axis-angle, and then quaternions built up from a full understanding of complex-number rotation in 2D rather than dropped in as four mysterious numbers — then skeletal animation, and SDL3's audio-stream model with 3D spatialization. The engine gets its first moving parts that are not a spinning cube.
