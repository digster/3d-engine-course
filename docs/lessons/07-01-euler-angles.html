Euler Angles and Their Pathologies

Every rotation in this engine so far has been composed, never typed. There are twenty-six calls to rotation_x, rotation_y and rotation_z across the demos, four of which multiply two of them together — and each of those four invented its own private convention on the spot, including two on adjacent lines of demo_scene.cpp that disagree deliberately, to show that the order matters. Nobody has ever handed this engine three numbers and said "that is the orientation". A designer does. A file written by somebody else's tool does. A debug panel with three sliders does. So: what are the three numbers, and what do they mean? The answer is that there are twenty-four self-consistent ways to answer that, they are all in use, and reading the same triple (30°, 40°, 50°) under the wrong one puts the nose 83° away from where it belongs — measured, not estimated. We fix one convention, write it into the Conventions page, and derive the matrix it names. Then we meet the hole. Three numbers cannot parameterise rotation without a singularity — that is a theorem, not a bug, and no choice of order escapes it. What a choice of order decides is where the hole sits, and ours puts it at pitch ±90°, nose straight up. Most treatments show you the gimbals and stop. We are going to print the number: the matrix that turns knob rates into angular velocity has determinant −cos(pitch), its weakest gain is |cos p| / √(1+|sin p|), and at a hundredth of a degree from lock you would need 464,000 degrees per second of knob to turn the aircraft at one. Along the way this lesson trips over the same numerical trap three separate times — in the conditioning formula, in the extraction, and in the textbook expression for "the angle between two rotations", which returns exactly zero for a turn of 0.004° and is therefore incapable of measuring the thing every claim here is measured with.

1. The problem: a rotation nobody can type

Open demos/common/demo_scene.cpp and look at two adjacent lines that have been sitting there since Module 2: 

case spin::tumble_xy: return engine::rotation_x(t) * engine::rotation_y(1.1f);
case spin::tumble_yx: return engine::rotation_y(1.1f) * engine::rotation_x(t);

The comment above them says what they are for: "the two tumble modes share both ingredients and differ ONLY in which rotation is applied first". They exist to demonstrate that matrix multiplication does not commute, and they do it well. But notice what they also are. Each of them is a two-angle Euler convention, invented at the call site, used once, and never named. There are four such sites in this repository. Lesson 5.12's rover is another: 

const engine::mat3 heading = engine::rotation_y(r.yaw);
t.rotation = heading * engine::rotation_z(r.bank);

That is a yaw followed by a bank about the yawed forward axis — a perfectly reasonable convention, chosen by whoever wrote the line, documented nowhere, and impossible to hand to anything else. If a designer asked "what numbers do I type to point the rover north-east and lean it 15° into the turn?", there would be no answer in the engine to give them. There would be a line of C++ in a demo.

This is the gap. The engine stores a rotation as a mat3 and has done since Lesson 2.6, and that is the right thing to store — nine floats that the renderer can multiply by directly, with no conversion at the point of use. What it has no answer for is the interface: the small number of human-sized quantities that a person, or a file, or a slider, uses to say an orientation.

A rotation in three dimensions has exactly three degrees of freedom. You can see that without any algebra: to specify an orientation you have to say where the object's forward axis points (two numbers — a direction on a sphere) and then how far it is twisted about that axis (one more). Three. So the minimum possible interface is three numbers, and three numbers is precisely what every tool a human touches offers: three boxes in a transform panel, three sliders, three columns in a spreadsheet of camera keyframes.

Three numbers for three degrees of freedom sounds like it should be the end of the story. It is the beginning of two separate ones, and this lesson is both of them.

The first is that three numbers are not enough information. "Yaw 30, pitch 40, roll 50" is not an orientation. It becomes an orientation only when you also fix which axes the three turns are about, in which order they are applied, and whether each later turn happens about a world axis or about the axis the earlier turns have just moved. There are twenty-four self-consistent ways to answer that, all of them in use somewhere, and §3 measures what it costs to guess wrong. The answer is not "a bit of drift". It is up to 83°.

The second is that three numbers cannot do the job at all, anywhere. Not "our three numbers"; any three. There is no way to cover all rotations smoothly with three coordinates without at least one place where the coordinate system tears. That is gimbal lock, it is a theorem rather than an engineering mistake, and §5 both proves it locally and measures it — because a fact you can print a number for is a fact you can act on, and a picture of three brass rings is not.

What this lesson does not do

It does not change how the engine stores rotation. transform::rotation is still a mat3 when this lesson ends, and Lesson 7.4 replaces it with a quaternion. Euler angles are an interface, and §7 argues that conclusion with numbers rather than assuming it. If you finish this lesson thinking "so I should store yaw, pitch and roll in my entities", the lesson has failed and §7 is where to re-read.

2. Three knobs, and which axis each one turns about

2.1 The chain

Picture an aircraft on a stand, and three knobs on a console.

The first knob is yaw. Turning it swings the aircraft left and right about the vertical — about world +Y, which is our up (Conventions §2). Nothing about the aircraft's current attitude changes which axis this knob uses: the vertical is the vertical.

The second knob is pitch, and here is where it gets interesting. Pitch tips the nose up and down — about the axis that runs through the wingtips. But the wings have already been moved by the yaw. If the aircraft is pointing north-east, the wingtip axis runs north-west to south-east, not east to west. So the pitch knob turns about the +X axis that the yaw carried, not about world +X.

The third knob is roll, which banks the aircraft about the axis running from tail to nose. Both the yaw and the pitch have moved that axis, so roll turns about the +Z axis that both of them carried.

That progression — each knob acting about an axis the previous knobs have already moved — is what the word intrinsic means, and Figure 1 is a picture of it. Watch the "was here" stubs: the first panel has none, because yaw's axis is the world's own and has never been carried by anything.

Written as a product, the whole convention is one line:

\[ R(\psi, \theta, \phi) \;=\; R_y(\psi)\,R_x(\theta)\,R_z(\phi) \] R(yaw, pitch, roll) = rotation_y(yaw) * rotation_x(pitch) * rotation_z(roll)

and it is worth stopping on the fact that this single expression carries both readings. Read it left to right and you get the story above: yaw first, then pitch about the yawed X, then roll about the twice-carried Z. Read it right to left — the direction every matrix product in this engine is read (Conventions §6) — and you get a different but equally true story: a vertex is first rolled in the aircraft's own frame, then pitched, then yawed, each about a fixed world axis. Those are the same matrix. §4.3 proves it rather than asserting it, because "it works out to the same thing" is exactly the kind of sentence that turns out to be false in the one case you needed it.

2.2 Why yaw-pitch-roll, and not x-y-z

You might be wondering why we picked this order at all, given that §3 is about to tell you there are twenty-three others. Hold that thought for two paragraphs, because the reason is the most useful single idea in this lesson.

Every three-angle parameterisation of rotation has a singularity. This is not a defect of any particular choice; it is a consequence of the shape of the space of rotations, and no clever ordering escapes it. So the choice of order is not a choice of whether to have a hole. It is a choice of where to put it.

For the six orders that use three different axes — the family yaw-pitch-roll belongs to — the singularity always lands on the middle angle, at ±90°. (The repeated-axis families put it at a middle angle of 0° instead, which is worse for a reason §5.6 gives.) §5 derives it. So:

  • Order x-y-z puts the hole at the middle angle, which is the rotation about Y. For a camera, Y is the one you swing all day long. The hole would be somewhere you pass through every few seconds.
  • Order y-x-z — ours — puts the hole at pitch ±90°, which is nose straight up or straight down. That is a configuration that every first-person camera in every game you have ever played already forbids, by clamping the pitch, for reasons that have nothing to do with mathematics and everything to do with it being disorienting.

So we are not avoiding the problem. We are putting it where the product already forbids the user to go. That is the kind of decision worth recognising when you meet it: you cannot remove the constraint, so you move it somewhere it costs nothing.

2.3 What the failure looks like

Before any algebra, look at it. Figure 2 is two renders from this lesson's demo, which you will build in §9. The left-hand panel is pitch 0: three hoops at right angles, each pivoting about its own axle, drawn in this course's axis colours — green for the vertical (yaw), red for the wingtip axis (pitch), blue for the nose axis (roll).

Now the right-hand panel, at pitch 90°. The blue axle — the one the roll knob turns about — has swung round until it lies exactly along the green one. Two knobs, one axis. Turning the yaw knob and turning the roll knob now do the same thing, and there is a whole direction of rotation that no combination of the three knobs can produce at all.

Notice also what is missing from the right-hand panel. In the left one there is a magenta arrow: the demo draws, at its true length, the rotation produced by the weakest combination of knob rates. At pitch 90° that arrow has zero length, so it is not there. The picture is not a diagram of the situation; it is the measurement, drawn.

The thing almost every explanation gets wrong

Gimbal lock does not mean the aircraft cannot reach some orientation. It can reach every orientation there is; nothing has been lost from the space of rotations. What has been lost is a direction of motion in the parameterisation. Sitting at pitch 90°, you can still get to any attitude you like — but to move infinitesimally in one particular direction, you would have to make an enormous, finite excursion through the angles first. That distinction is why §5 talks about the derivative of a map and not about brass rings, and it is the whole reason the failure shows up as stuttering and lurching in real systems rather than as something simply refusing to move.

3. A convention is not a detail: there are twenty-four

3.1 Counting them

A Euler convention is two choices, and the count falls out of them.

The axis sequence. You need three turns, and each consecutive pair must be about different axes — turning about x and then about x again is just one turn about x, so it buys no degree of freedom. That leaves 3 choices for the first axis, 2 for the second (anything but the first), and 2 for the third (anything but the second). Three times two times two is twelve. Six of them use all three axes — xyz, xzy, yxz, yzx, zxy, zyx — and are called Tait–Bryan angles, which is the family yaw-pitch-roll belongs to. The other six repeat the first axis as the third — xyx, xzx, yxy, yzy, zxz, zyz — and are the proper Euler angles, which is what the classical mechanics literature usually means by the term. They are not exotic; zxz is what orbital elements use.

The frame. Each turn is either about an axis the previous turns moved (intrinsic) or about a fixed world axis (extrinsic). That is a second binary choice, and twelve times two is twenty-four.

This is not our arithmetic; it is the standard enumeration, and Ken Shoemake's Euler Angle Conversion in Graphics Gems IV encodes exactly these twenty-four in four bits. Figure 3 measures what each one does to the same three numbers.

One of the twenty-four bars is zero, and it is ours. The worst is yzy intrinsic at 83.48°. The most dangerous, though, is not the worst — it is yzx extrinsic at 16.03°, because 16° looks like a tuning problem. You will spend an afternoon adjusting a value that is not wrong.

3.2 The identity that makes it twenty-four and not forty-eight

You might reasonably object that "intrinsic" and "extrinsic" ought to be genuinely different machinery, and that the twelve sequences ought therefore to appear twice over as twenty-four distinct products. They do not, and the reason is a small identity worth having.

Take an intrinsic sequence A-B-C with angles (α, β, γ). §2.1's picture says the composite is RA(α) RB(β) RC(γ). Now take the extrinsic sequence C-B-A with the angles in the reversed order (γ, β, α): turn about fixed C by γ, then about fixed B by β, then about fixed A by α. Extrinsic turns compose by multiplying each new one on the left, because each acts in the world frame that is already there — so the composite is RA(α) RB(β) RC(γ). The same product.

\[ \text{intrinsic } A\text{-}B\text{-}C(\alpha,\beta,\gamma) \;=\; \text{extrinsic } C\text{-}B\text{-}A(\gamma,\beta,\alpha) \] intrinsic ABC(a, b, c) is the same rotation as extrinsic CBA(c, b, a)

Ours is intrinsic y-x-z, so it is also extrinsic z-x-y read (roll, pitch, yaw). verify_71 §B checks that, and gets a worst element difference of exactly 0.000e+00 — not "small", zero, because it is literally the same sequence of floating-point operations.

So the twenty-four are twenty-four distinct interpretations of a triple, not twenty-four distinct families of matrix. Both statements are true and they answer different questions. If you are asking "how many products are there", twelve. If you are asking "how many ways can somebody hand me three numbers and be right", twenty-four, and that is the question that costs you an afternoon.

3.3 Our convention, stated once

Fixed here and on the Conventions page (§8b)

Intrinsic Y-X-Z, active, right-handed, radians. rotation_from_euler({yaw, pitch, roll}) is rotation_y(yaw) * rotation_x(pitch) * rotation_z(roll). Yaw turns about world +Y; pitch about the +X the yaw carried; roll about the +Z both carried. Positive angles follow the right-hand rule about their axis, which is the same rule Conventions §9b already fixed for torque and angular velocity.

"Active" is the fourth bit, and it is the one nobody mentions. A rotation matrix can mean "turn the object" (active) or "turn the coordinate frame and leave the object alone" (passive), and the two are inverses of each other. This course settled it in Module 2 without using the word: Conventions §6 says a matrix acts on column vectors and parent_from_local carries an object's vertices into its parent's space, which is the active reading. Nothing new is being decided here. It is being written down.

Two sanity checks you can do by hand, which verify_71 §A does by machine. Our forward is −Z and our right is +X. A positive yaw is counter-clockwise seen from above (right-hand rule about +Y), so the nose should swing from −Z toward −X — to the left:

yaw +90 sends forward (0,0,-1) to (-1.000, +0.000, +0.000)  ← left, as promised
pitch +90 sends forward (0,0,-1) to (+0.000, +1.000, +0.000)  ← nose up, as promised

What glTF did about all this

Our importer (Lesson 6.6) reads node rotations from glTF files and has never had to worry about any of the above, for one reason: glTF 2.0 stores node rotations as unit quaternions, four numbers with no convention attached beyond the component order. The format's designers looked at the twenty-four-way ambiguity and declined to participate. It is the single strongest argument for Lesson 7.4 that exists, and it is worth noticing that the argument is not about elegance or about speed — it is that a quaternion in a file means the same thing to every reader, and three angles in a file do not.

4. The matrix, derived

4.1 The three elementary rotations

Lesson 2.6 built these and the reasoning is worth one sentence of recall: each is Lesson 2.5's two-dimensional rotation acting in the plane of the other two axes, with the axis's own column left alone. In written notation — rows across, which is not how mat3 stores them (2.6 §3.3):

    | 1   0   0 |      | c   0   s |      | c  −s   0 |
Rx = | 0   c  −s |  Ry = | 0   1   0 |  Rz = | s   c   0 |
    | 0   s   c |      | −s  0   c |      | 0   0   1 |

Ry is the one where the minus sign lands in the row you do not expect, and 2.6's comment explains why rather than asking you to memorise it: going round the axes in the cyclic order x → y → z → x, the plane y's rotation acts in is the z-x plane in that order, so the "first" axis is z and the "second" is x.

4.2 Multiplying them out

Write cy, sy for cos and sin of the yaw and likewise cp, sp, cr, sr. Do the inner product first, because it is the one with structure worth seeing:

            | cr     −sr      0   |
Rx·Rz = | cp·sr   cp·cr   −sp |
            | sp·sr   sp·cr    cp   |

Already something useful has appeared. Look at the middle row. Every entry of it carries a factor of cp except the last, which is −sp on its own. Now multiply on the left by Ry, whose middle row is (0, 1, 0) — so the middle row of the product is the middle row of what we just computed, unchanged:

\[ R \;=\; \begin{bmatrix} c_yc_r + s_ys_ps_r & s_ys_pc_r - c_ys_r & s_yc_p \\ c_ps_r & c_pc_r & -s_p \\ c_ys_ps_r - s_yc_r & s_ys_r + c_ys_pc_r & c_yc_p \end{bmatrix} \] Row 0: cy*cr + sy*sp*sr, sy*sp*cr - cy*sr, sy*cp Row 1: cp*sr, cp*cr, -sp Row 2: cy*sp*sr - sy*cr, sy*sr + cy*sp*cr, cy*cp

Nine entries, and eight of them mix at least two of the three angles. Entry (1, 2) — middle row, right-hand column — is −sin(pitch) and nothing else. That is the single most useful fact on this page. It is why §6's extraction starts by taking an arcsine of that one number and not of some other one, and it is not a coincidence: the middle axis of any Euler sequence is the one whose rotation is not conjugated by anything, so it always leaves exactly one clean entry behind. Change the axis order and the clean entry moves; it does not disappear.

4.3 The intrinsic reading, proved rather than asserted

§2.1 claimed that "turn about world Y, then about the X that turn moved, then about the Z both moved" gives the same matrix as the plain left-to-right product. Here is why, in the one piece of machinery that makes it obvious.

Suppose you have a frame F — a rotation carrying world axes onto the object's — and you want to rotate by angle θ about the object's x axis, which in world coordinates is the vector F·x̂. The trick is to do the turn where it is easy. Step into the object's frame with Fᵀ, where its x axis is world x; do the elementary turn Rx(θ) there; step back out with F. That composite is F Rx(θ) Fᵀ — a conjugation, and it is the standard way to say "the same operation, seen from somewhere else".

Now apply it to a frame that is already F:

\[ \big(F R_x(\theta) F^{\mathsf T}\big)\, F \;=\; F R_x(\theta)\, \big(F^{\mathsf T} F\big) \;=\; F R_x(\theta) \] (F * Rx * F^T) * F = F * Rx * (F^T * F) = F * Rx

The step-back-out and the step-into cancel, and what is left is right-multiplication by the elementary rotation. That is the whole content of "intrinsic multiplies on the right", and it is why the product reads in knob order left to right. verify_71 §A.4 builds the sequence the long way, one conjugation at a time, and compares: worst element difference 2.384e-07, which is float noise from doing five extra matrix products.

C++ — why the code does not contain that matrix

The obvious optimisation is to write those nine entries out directly: twelve multiplies and three sin/cos calls, against the fifty-four multiplies two mat3 products cost. rotation_from_euler does not do it, and the reason is not that the saving is small. It is that the product form is the convention. A reader who wants to know what order this engine applies its angles in can read rotation_y(yaw) * rotation_x(pitch) * rotation_z(roll) and know. Nobody can read it off nine transcribed sines — they would have to reverse-engineer it, which is exactly the afternoon §3 was about. If a profile ever says the fifty-four multiplies matter, the closed form is above and the test that proves the two agree is already written, so the swap is fifteen minutes with a safety net. Optimise then, not now.

5. Gimbal lock, as a number

5.1 Ask about rates, not about positions

§2.3 showed you the rig locking and said the loss is "a direction of motion in the parameterisation". To turn that into arithmetic, stop asking where the three knobs put the aircraft and start asking what happens when you turn them.

Suppose you turn the three knobs at rates (ψ̇, θ̇, φ̇) — radians per second of yaw, pitch and roll. The aircraft then spins at some angular velocity ω, which Conventions §9b defines as a single vector whose direction is the axis and whose length is the rate. What is the relationship?

It is simpler than it looks, and you can read it straight off §2.1's picture. Angular velocities about a common point add as vectors. Each knob contributes a spin about its own axis, at its own rate, and those three axes are exactly the three §2.1 identified:

\[ \boldsymbol\omega \;=\; \dot\psi\,\hat{\mathbf y} \;+\; \dot\theta\,\big(R_y(\psi)\hat{\mathbf x}\big) \;+\; \dot\phi\,\big(R_y(\psi)R_x(\theta)\hat{\mathbf z}\big) \] omega = d_yaw * (world +Y) + d_pitch * (the +X the yaw carried) + d_roll * (the +Z both carried)

That is linear in the rates, so it is a matrix times the rate vector — and the matrix's columns are just those three axes, written in world coordinates. This is the Jacobian of the angles-to-rotation map, and euler_rate_jacobian is four lines because that is genuinely all it is:

column 0 = (0, 1, 0)                   yaw's axis, which never moves
column 1 = Ry(ψ)·(1, 0, 0)        pitch's axis, carried by the yaw
column 2 = Ry(ψ)·Rx(θ)·(0, 0, 1)  roll's axis, carried by both

5.2 The determinant, which is the answer

A 3×3 matrix's determinant is the signed volume of the box its columns span (2.6). So det J asks: how much volume do the three knob axes enclose? If they are mutually perpendicular, one unit. If they are coplanar — if one of them lies in the plane of the other two — zero, and the three knobs between them can only reach a two-dimensional sheet of angular velocities out of a three-dimensional space.

Figure 4 is that box, at three pitches.

Compute it. With a, b, c the three columns, the determinant is the triple product a · (b × c). Column a is (0,1,0), so the whole thing collapses to the y component of b × c, which is −(bxcz − bzcx). With b = (cy, 0, −sy) and c = (sycp, −sp, cycp):

\[ \det J \;=\; -\big(c_y \cdot c_yc_p - (-s_y)\cdot s_yc_p\big) \;=\; -c_p\,(c_y^2 + s_y^2) \;=\; -\cos\theta \] det J = -(cy * cy*cp + sy * sy*cp) = -cp * (cy^2 + sy^2) = -cos(pitch)

det J = −cos(pitch). The yaw does not appear — it cancelled against itself, which it had to, because turning the whole aircraft about the vertical cannot change how well its controls work. The minus sign is nothing: it says the ordering (yaw, pitch, roll) is an odd permutation relative to a right-handed frame, and if we had listed the columns as (pitch, yaw, roll) it would be positive. The magnitude is the content.

Column 2 is (0.383022, −0.642788, 0.663414), and its middle entry is −sin 40° — the roll axis has been tipped 40° below the horizontal by the pitch. Every number in that matrix is somewhere you can point at in Figure 1.

And the determinant by hand: b × c has y component −(0.866025 × 0.663414 − (−0.5) × 0.383022) = −(0.574533 + 0.191511) = −0.766044, and dotting with (0,1,0) keeps exactly that. cos 40° = 0.766044.

verify_71 §C.1 checks det J + cos(pitch) = 0 across 3,249 poses: worst error 5.960e-08.

The measurement needed no new API

std::abs(determinant(euler_rate_jacobian(e))) is "how far from gimbal lock am I", on a scale from 1 to 0, in one expression. determinant has been in mat3.hpp since Lesson 2.6, where it was introduced as "the factor by which volumes change". Nothing was added to make this measurable — the pieces were already there and nobody had put them together. That is worth noticing as a habit: when a phenomenon feels un-measurable, the first question is not "what function do I need" but "what is it the determinant, the norm or the trace of".

5.3 What exactly dies

The determinant tells you that a dimension is lost. It does not tell you which knob combination is wasted or which direction of spin is unreachable. For that, look at JᵀJ — the matrix of all the dot products between columns — which is where every question about lengths and angles of the columns lives.

All three columns are unit length. Column 0 is (0,1,0). Column 1 is a rotated x̂, so unit. Column 2 is (sycp, −sp, cycp), whose squared length is cp²(sy² + cy²) + sp² = cp² + sp² = 1. And of the three dot products between different columns, two are zero — yaw's axis is perpendicular to pitch's by construction, and pitch's is perpendicular to roll's because they are two axes of the same orthonormal frame. The only one left is column 0 · column 2 = −sin(pitch). So:

\[ J^{\mathsf T}J \;=\; \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & -s_p \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ -s_p & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \] J^T J = [[1, 0, -sin(pitch)], [0, 1, 0], [-sin(pitch), 0, 1]]

One number, in two corners, and it is the sine of the pitch. That is the entire pathology, written down: the yaw axis and the roll axis are not perpendicular, and how far from perpendicular they are is exactly the pitch. At pitch 0 they are at right angles; at pitch 90 they are the same line.

The eigenvectors of that matrix can be read off by inspection. (0,1,0) is one, with eigenvalue 1 — the pitch knob is uninvolved, as you would expect, since it is perpendicular to both the others. And (1, 0, ±1) are the other two, with eigenvalues 1 ∓ sp. So the singular values of J — the gains it applies to the best and worst input directions — are the square roots:

\[ \sigma \;=\; \Big\{\sqrt{1 + |\sin\theta|},\;\; 1,\;\; \sqrt{1 - |\sin\theta|}\Big\} \] sigma = { sqrt(1 + |sin pitch|), 1, sqrt(1 - |sin pitch|) }

Check it against the determinant, which is the product of the singular values: √(1+|s|) · 1 · √(1−|s|) = √(1−s²) = |cos θ|. It agrees.

And the weakest input direction is the eigenvector (1, 0, +1) when the pitch is positive — turn the yaw knob and the roll knob at the same rate, and almost nothing happens. That is the key you hold down in the demo. Below the horizon the sign flips and it is opposite rates instead, which is a detail the demo has to get right or the effect reverses and looks like a bug in the demo rather than a fact about the map.

5.4 The same thing, seen algebraically

There is a second route to §5.3's conclusion that needs no derivatives at all, and meeting the same fact twice from different directions is how you know you have understood it. Set pitch = +90° in §4.2's matrix, so cp = 0 and sp = 1, and collapse:

\[ R(\psi, 90^\circ, \phi) \;=\; \begin{bmatrix} \cos(\psi-\phi) & \sin(\psi-\phi) & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & -1 \\ -\sin(\psi-\phi) & \cos(\psi-\phi) & 0 \end{bmatrix} \] At pitch +90 the matrix is built entirely from cos(yaw - roll) and sin(yaw - roll).

Entry (0,0) was cycr + syspsr, which with sp = 1 is cycr + sysr — the cosine difference identity, so cos(ψ − φ). Entry (0,1) was syspcr − cysr, which is sin(ψ − φ). The rest follow.

Yaw and roll appear only as their difference. Two knobs, one number. Every (ψ, φ) pair on a line of constant ψ − φ produces the identical matrix, so there is an entire one-parameter family of poses that are the same orientation. Figure 5 is that family.

At pitch = −90° the same collapse happens with the signs arranged so that ψ + φ survives instead — the other diagonal. verify_71 §C.4 checks both: moving yaw and roll each by +47° at pitch +90° produces 0.0000° of actual rotation, and the same joint move at pitch 0 produces 65.51°, which is what two near-orthogonal 47° turns should give (47√2 = 66.47, and the small shortfall is the axes not being exactly orthogonal at that pose). The second number is the control, and it is there because a check whose degenerate case reads as a pass is not a check — a lesson this course has now learned three times.

5.5 How much it costs, in units you can feel

The smallest singular value, σmin, has an operational meaning worth stating plainly: turn the knobs at a combined rate of 1 rad/s and, in the worst direction, the aircraft turns at σmin rad/s. Invert it and you get the price: to turn the aircraft at 1 rad/s in that direction you must turn the knobs at 1/σmin.

Read the numbers off Figure 6 and the shape of the problem becomes concrete. At 10° from lock it costs you a factor of 8; at 1° a factor of 81; at a tenth of a degree, 810. At a hundredth of a degree from vertical you would need 8,100 rad/s of knob — 464,000 degrees per second — for one radian per second of aircraft. That is the real-world failure: a control loop running a yaw-pitch-roll parameterisation does not stop near the singularity, it saturates and lurches, because the correction it computes is physically impossible and it takes whatever fraction of it the actuators can deliver.

verify_71 §C.2 checks the closed form against a brute-force sweep of a million unit rate vectors per pose — apply J to each and keep the smallest output length, which is the definition of the smallest singular value and assumes nothing about §5.3's algebra. Worst disagreement over the range: 1.788e-07.

The bug this harness found in itself

The formula derived in §5.3 is σmin = √(1 − |sin p|), and it is correct. It is also unusable in float exactly where it matters. At pitch 89.99° the true sine is 1 − 1.5e-8, which is closer to 1.0 than a float can represent; the subtraction returns exactly zero, and the conditioning number comes out inf. The first draft of Figure 6's table printed inf at a pose that is nowhere near singular.

The algebra removes it. Multiply above and below by (1 + |sin p|):

\[ 1 - s \;=\; \frac{(1-s)(1+s)}{1+s} \;=\; \frac{1 - s^2}{1+s} \;=\; \frac{c^2}{1+s} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \sigma_{\min} = \frac{|\cos\theta|}{\sqrt{1 + |\sin\theta|}} \] 1 - s = (1-s)(1+s)/(1+s) = (1 - s^2)/(1+s) = c^2/(1+s), so sigma_min = |cos p| / sqrt(1 + |sin p|)

No subtraction of two nearly-equal numbers, accurate all the way in, and it is the red dashed line in Figure 6 that separates from the blue one and then falls off a cliff. Note where the separation starts: at 89.9° the naive form already reads 0.0012449 against a true 0.0012340, which is 0.9% wrong while still looking entirely plausible. The cliff is the part you notice; the drift before it is the part that gets shipped.

5.6 Where the other twelve orders put their hole

§2.2 promised a reason to prefer the three-distinct-axis families, and now the machinery is here to give it. Take a proper-Euler order — z-x-z, say, which is what orbital elements use — and set the middle angle to zero. Then R = Rz(α) Rx(0) Rz(γ) = Rz(α + γ): the first and third turns are about the same axis and only their sum survives. Exactly the same collapse, by exactly the same mechanism.

But look at where. The singularity of a repeated-axis order is at middle angle 0° — which is the identity, the rest pose, the value every field is initialised to and every editor opens at. A Tait–Bryan order puts its hole at ±90°, somewhere you can arrange never to go. The repeated-axis orders put theirs where everything starts.

6. Going backwards, and the branch that admits defeat

6.1 Inverting the map

Sometimes you have a matrix and need the three numbers: a debug panel showing the orientation of a bone, an exporter writing a file, an editor field a designer is about to type over. euler_from_rotation does it, and §4.2's structure hands you the recipe.

Entry (1,2) is −sin(pitch) and nothing else, so the pitch comes out of an arcsine of it. That fixes cos(pitch) too, and then the remaining entries split cleanly:

r(0,2) = sin(yaw)·cos(pitch)    r(2,2) = cos(yaw)·cos(pitch)
r(1,0) = cos(pitch)·sin(roll)   r(1,1) = cos(pitch)·cos(roll)

Each pair is a (sine, cosine) of the angle we want, scaled by a common factor of cos(pitch). atan2 takes a (y, x) pair and returns the angle, and — this is the useful part — a common positive scale factor on both arguments does not change the answer, because atan2 is about the direction of the vector, not its length. So the cos(pitch) divides out and the yaw is atan2(r(0,2), r(2,2)), the roll is atan2(r(1,0), r(1,1)).

That common factor is also the whole of the problem. When cos(pitch) goes to zero, those four entries all go to zero together, and atan2(0, 0) is asking for the direction of a vector with no length.

6.2 What the degenerate branch keeps, and what it gives up

§5.4 says exactly what is recoverable at lock: the difference ψ − φ, and nothing about the two separately. So the honest thing for the extraction to do is to recover that difference, put it somewhere, and say so. We put it in the yaw and set the roll to zero, and return a degenerate flag.

verify_71 §D.2 — feeding in a pose that is exactly at lock:

  in:  yaw  +20.00 pitch  +90.00 roll  -35.00
  out: yaw  +55.00 pitch  +90.00 roll   +0.00  (cos_pitch 4.371e-08, degenerate YES)
  matrix recovered to 6.537e-06°, but yaw moved by 35.00° and roll by 35.00°

Both halves of that matter. The yaw came back as 55°, which is 20 − (−35) — the difference, exactly as §5.4 predicts, and the whole of the recoverable information. The roll came back as zero, which is not what went in. And the matrix is reproduced correctly. Round-trip those three output numbers through rotation_from_euler and you get the input rotation back to six millionths of a degree. Nothing about the orientation was lost; what was lost is the split, and no implementation can do better because there is nothing better to do.

Note also cos_pitch 4.371e-08 rather than 0. That is cos(π/2) in float — π/2 is not representable, so its cosine is not zero. If you ever write if (cos_pitch == 0.0f) you will have written a branch that does not fire at the one pose it exists for.

6.3 Where the threshold goes, and the argument that was wrong

So the extraction needs a threshold, and the header's first draft justified it like this: near lock the yaw comes out of atan2 of two quantities proportional to cos(pitch), so a float's ~1e-7 of error in a matrix entry reaches the recovered angle divided by cos(pitch). Put the floor at 1e-4 and the amplification is bounded at about 0.06°.

Then we measured it, and there was no amplification at all, at any pitch. The argument had missed something: floating-point error is relative, not absolute. The two entries atan2 reads are themselves proportional to cos(pitch), so when cos(pitch) is 1e-4 the entries are around 1e-4 and their absolute error is around 1e-11. The ratio survives, and so does the angle.

That is a property of a matrix that rotation_from_euler has just built, and of nothing else. A rotation that has come down a transform hierarchy (Lesson 5.9), or out of a file, or through a few hundred compositions, carries absolute error of about 1e-7 spread across all nine entries — and then the division is entirely real:

verify_71 §D.3 — 200 trials of ±1e-7 per entry, worst recovered yaw error:

      0.1000°    1.745e-03      0.00000°       0.00384°        no
      0.0200°    3.490e-04      0.00000°       0.02029°        no
      0.0057°    9.950e-05      (in yaw)  (all flagged)       YES
      0.0020°    3.488e-05      (in yaw)  (all flagged)       YES

The perturbed column is 1.2e-7 rad / cos_pitch, converted to degrees — check it: at cos_pitch = 3.49e-4 that is 0.0197°, and the measurement is 0.0203°. At the threshold of 1e-4 it would be 0.07°, which is below what any consumer of these angles will notice, and 1e-4 is where it was put. The threshold corresponds to being within 0.0057° of vertical.

The "(all flagged)" cells are the part of this table it would have been easiest to get wrong. Every perturbed trial below the threshold reports degenerate and is skipped, so the running worst stays at its initial 0.0 — and the table would print the best-looking number in it at the worst pose in it. Lesson 6.16 shipped exactly that shape, printing identical=YES on two failed file reads. Count what you actually measured, and print a dash when the count is zero.

Reaching 0.0057° at all depends on one detail in the implementation. cos_pitch is computed as hypot(r(0,2), r(2,2)) and not as the obvious √(1 − sin²), for exactly the reason §5.5 gave: the obvious form hits zero a full 0.01° early. And the pairing is neat rather than a trick — those two entries are sin(yaw)·cos(pitch) and cos(yaw)·cos(pitch), so their length is |cos(pitch)|. The conditioning of the yaw extraction is the length of the vector whose angle the yaw extraction takes. Same two numbers, one gives you the answer and the other gives you your confidence in it.

6.4 It is not a true inverse, and cannot be

std::asin returns values in [−90°, +90°], so the extraction can never report a pitch outside that strip. Feed it a matrix built with pitch 100° and it comes back as something else entirely:

verify_71 §D.4

  pitch 100° comes back as yaw -170.0 pitch +80.0 roll +180.0
      — same rotation, 1.465e-05° apart

That is not a bug and cannot be fixed. The map from triples to rotations is many-to-one — §5.4 showed an entire line of triples mapping to one rotation, and here is a much more ordinary example of the same thing — so its inverse has to choose. Choosing the shortest pitch is the choice every library makes, and the one thing an implementation owes you is that the triple it returns names the rotation you gave it. This one does, to fifteen millionths of a degree.

The practical consequence is worth stating because it bites: do not round-trip through Euler angles in a loop. Reading a rotation out as angles, adjusting one, and writing it back is a perfectly good thing to do once, in an editor, with a human watching. Doing it every frame means an orientation that silently teleports to a different triple the moment it crosses the pitch strip boundary, and a slider that was at 100 last frame is at 80 this frame with two other fields changed.

7. Interpolation: the real indictment

Everything so far has been about one orientation at a time. The argument that actually settles the question — the one that sends Lesson 7.4 to quaternions rather than leaving us with three tidy floats — is about two.

Blending is what an engine does constantly. An animation system blends between keyframes (Lesson 7.7). A camera eases toward a target. A turret swings to face a thing. All of those are "give me the orientation a fraction t of the way from A to B", and if orientations are stored as three angles then the obvious implementation is to lerp the three angles.

7.1 Measuring a path

To say what is wrong with it we need a number, so first: what does "the right answer" even mean here? It means the geodesic — the shortest route through the space of rotations, which is a steady turn about one fixed axis. Any other route performs more turning than it had to. So:

  • Path length: sample the interpolation finely, measure the angle between each consecutive pair of orientations, and add them up. That is how much turning actually happened.
  • Geodesic distance: the angle between the two endpoints. That is how much turning was strictly required.
  • Excess: the ratio, minus one. Detour, as a percentage.
  • Speed variation: the largest per-step turn divided by the smallest. A geodesic at constant rate has ratio 1; anything else speeds up and slows down, which is what "the animation surges" looks like from inside.

Figure 7's left panel traces where the nose goes, for a pair of orientations chosen with nothing near lock. The amber curve is the Euler lerp; the dashed blue is the geodesic. They start and end in the same place and the amber one bulges away. The numbers: 204.29° of turning performed for a 179.05° journey — 25.24° of detour, 14.1% excess, and the rate varies by a factor of 1.56 along the way. That last one is the part a player sees, because a blend that is 56% faster in the middle than at the ends reads as a lurch.

7.2 A surprise, and why the first version of this measurement said nothing

The first draft of this check swept the yaw alone at pitch 88° — right up against the singularity, where surely the damage would be worst — and reported an excess of exactly 0.00%. That is not an instrument failure. It is a fact worth knowing:

Every single-knob Euler path is already a geodesic

Turning one knob and leaving the other two alone is, by construction, a steady rotation about one fixed axis — which is exactly what a geodesic is. It does not matter which knob, or how close to lock you are. So if you go looking for the interpolation pathology with one angle moving, you will not find it, and you may conclude it is not there. It takes at least two angles changing before the path can leave the geodesic at all.

Figure 7's right panel is the check rewritten to say something. It runs the same three angle deltas four times, sliding the pitch band away from the singularity and changing nothing else:

Run near the singularity, the same move costs three times as much turning as it needs. Away from it, a quarter more — which is still wrong, and is the honest headline: an Euler lerp is never the geodesic unless it only moves one knob. The bottom two rows are not monotone with each other, and that is real rather than noise: the endpoints differ between bands, so the geodesic distance differs too, and the excess is a ratio. The claim the table supports is "near lock is catastrophically worse", not "excess falls smoothly with pitch".

7.3 The other bug, which is not about rotation at all

There is a second failure in angle interpolation that has nothing to do with gimbal lock, geodesics or three dimensions. Blend a yaw from 170° to −170°:

verify_71 §E.4

  yaw 170° -> -170°: raw lerp turns 340.0°,
                     shortest-delta lerp turns 20.0°   (the journey is 20.0°)

Those two headings are 20° apart, across the back. A lerp of the raw floats performs 340° in the other direction — a full spin, backwards, for a twenty-degree turn. Nothing is wrong with the rotation maths; the bug is that an angle is a position on a circle and a float is a position on a line, and code that forgets loses the wrap.

shortest_angle_delta fixes it in one line, and the engine gets it because any code that blends a heading needs it — the character controller in Lesson 8.13 will, and so will every camera that eases toward a target. The implementation is std::remainder rather than std::fmod, and that choice is the function: fmod truncates toward zero and keeps the sign of its left operand, landing in (−2π, 2π); remainder rounds to nearest and lands in [−π, π], which is the interval we want. Getting it from fmod takes a conditional fixup that people write wrong.

And note what the fix does not fix

Wrapping gets you the short way round each angle. It does nothing at all about §7.1 and §7.2 — the corrected lerp still leaves the geodesic by 14% on a generic pair, because the problem there is not the wrap, it is that a straight line in angle-space is a curve in rotation-space. It is worth being clear that these are two separate bugs with two separate fixes, and only one of them has a fix at this level. The other one is Lesson 7.5.

8. The instrument, and the formula that cannot measure

Every number in §7 came out of one function: the angle between two rotations. It is worth a section of its own, because deriving it is pretty and shipping the derivation would have been wrong.

Any rotation is a turn of some angle θ about some axis. Choose a basis whose first vector is that axis, and in that basis the matrix is block-diagonal: a 1 in the corner for the axis, which does not move, and a 2×2 rotation for the plane it turns. Its trace is therefore 1 + 2cos θ. Trace is unchanged by a change of basis, so that holds in every basis — including the one we are actually in, where we have no idea what the axis is. With R = AᵀB (undo A, then do B) the formula falls straight out:

\[ \theta \;=\; \arccos\!\Big(\frac{\operatorname{tr}(A^{\mathsf T}B) - 1}{2}\Big) \] theta = acos((trace(A^T B) - 1) / 2)

It is correct, it is in every reference, and it cannot measure a small angle. Near θ = 0 the trace is 3 − θ², so a turn of 0.004 radians moves the trace by 1.6e-5 — about an eighth of a float's resolution at 3.0. The information is destroyed before acos is called, and acos then amplifies whatever is left, because its slope at 1 is infinite.

Figure 8 measures both forms against turns of known size. The red curve is the textbook one, and at a true angle of 0.004° it has a relative error of 1.00 — it returns exactly zero. That is not a rounding concern. A path measurement takes 2,048 steps across 120°, which is 0.059° each, so the formula the whole of §7 depends on would have been measuring nothing at all.

The fix is to take the sine as well. The antisymmetric part of a rotation is R − Rᵀ = 2 sin(θ) [n]×, whose three distinct entries form a vector of length 2|sin θ| — and crucially those entries are computed as differences of matrix entries, so they stay proportional to θ instead of hiding inside a 3. Feed both to atan2:

\[ \theta \;=\; \operatorname{atan2}\!\Big(\tfrac{1}{2}\big\|R - R^{\mathsf T}\big\|,\;\; \tfrac{1}{2}\big(\operatorname{tr} R - 1\big)\Big) \] theta = atan2( |R - R^T| / 2 , (trace(R) - 1) / 2 )

Each half is accurate at one end and atan2 takes whichever is carrying the information. The blue curve in Figure 8 stays at or barely above float resolution across five decades. And because atan2's first argument is a magnitude, the result lands in [0, π] — which is what "the shortest rotation carrying A onto B" means, and why no sign is returned.

Both spellings are in euler.hpp. The textbook one is kept, is called by nothing, and exists so that verify_71 §F can compare against the real thing rather than against a copy of it. §F.2 also sums 2,048 small steps along a path of known total length and gets 120.0003°, which rules out a systematic bias that a per-step accuracy check would miss.

The pattern, met three times in one lesson

√(1 − sin²p) in §5.5, sqrt(1 - sin_pitch²) in the extraction, and acos((tr − 1)/2) here. All three are algebraically perfect. All three subtract two nearly-equal numbers close to 1.0 and destroy the small quantity they exist to report. And all three are fixed the same way: get the small quantity from something that is small — a hypot of two entries that are themselves proportional to it, or the antisymmetric part whose entries are proportional to θ — rather than by subtracting it out of a big one. When a formula's job is to report something near zero, look at what it computes just before it returns, and ask whether that thing is ever small.

9. Implementation

9.1 The type, and what it does not have

Start with the three numbers. 

struct euler_angles
{
    float yaw   = 0.0f;   ///< about world +Y — left and right
    float pitch = 0.0f;   ///< about the yawed +X — nose up and down
    float roll  = 0.0f;   ///< about the yawed-and-pitched +Z — banking
};

The names are aviation's, not x/y/z, and that is worth a sentence. euler_angles{0.5f, 0.2f, 0.0f} read as x, y, z tells you nothing about which axis each number turns about — you would have to remember the convention. Read as yaw, pitch, roll it tells you, and it tells you in a vocabulary the designer typing the numbers already speaks.

There is no order field, and no template parameter for the convention. That is a deliberate refusal. A library that supports all twenty-four conventions is a library in which every caller must state one, which means every caller can state the wrong one, which is the problem we are trying to remove. One convention, fixed on the Conventions page, is the whole value proposition. If you ever genuinely need to read somebody else's, write a named function that says whose it is — rotation_from_aerospace_zyx — so the conversion is visible at the call site rather than hidden in an enum.

9.2 Forwards

[[nodiscard]] inline mat3 rotation_from_euler(euler_angles e)
{
    return rotation_y(e.yaw) * rotation_x(e.pitch) * rotation_z(e.roll);
}

One line, and §4.4's callout has already argued why it is not nine. Worth adding: this function is the only place in the engine that knows the convention. Change this line and the extraction below breaks its round-trip test immediately, which is the right coupling — a convention stated in two places is a convention that can disagree with itself.

9.3 Backwards, and returning your own confidence

The extraction could return three floats. It returns a small struct instead: 

struct euler_extraction
{
    euler_angles angles{};
    float cos_pitch = 1.0f;   // the conditioning of the split: 1 clean, 0 locked
    bool  degenerate = false; // yaw and roll were not separable
};

cos_pitch is computed inside the routine anyway, and every implementation of this you will find elsewhere throws it away. It is the entire difference between "here are your three angles" and "here are your three angles, and here is how much the last two of them mean". Lesson 6.18 learned this in a smaller key — a texture packer that computed its own occupancy and discarded it, leaving no way to judge the result from outside — and the rule that came out of it generalises: if a routine computes a quantity a caller would need in order to judge it, return it.

The body is §6.1 and §6.2 in code. Two details are load-bearing and both are about the number being small: 

const float sin_pitch = std::clamp(-r.at(1, 2), -1.0f, 1.0f);
out.cos_pitch = std::hypot(r.at(0, 2), r.at(2, 2));
out.degenerate = out.cos_pitch < k_euler_lock_epsilon;
out.angles.pitch = std::atan2(sin_pitch, out.cos_pitch);

The clamp is there because a matrix that has been through a few hundred compositions is only a rotation to about 1e-6, and asin(1.0000001) is NaN — a value that then propagates through the frame and turns into a vanished object three systems away. One instruction removes the entire failure mode.

The hypot is §6.3, and the atan2 on the last line is the same argument a third time: asin has infinite slope at ±1, so it loses precision precisely where the pitch is interesting. With an accurate cosine already in hand, atan2 is free and better behaved, and it returns the same branch — atan2 of a non-negative second argument lands in [−π/2, +π/2], which is asin's range exactly, so §6.4's canonical strip is unchanged.

9.4 The Jacobian

[[nodiscard]] inline mat3 euler_rate_jacobian(euler_angles e)
{
    const mat3 after_yaw = rotation_y(e.yaw);
    const mat3 after_pitch = after_yaw * rotation_x(e.pitch);
    return mat3{vec3{0.0f, 1.0f, 0.0f}, after_yaw.c0, after_pitch.c2};
}

Three columns, three axes, and the code says so: the yaw's axis is the world's, the pitch's is the first column of the yaw frame, the roll's is the third column of the yaw-and-pitch frame. Reading .c0 and .c2 off frames rather than multiplying by basis vectors is not a micro-optimisation — it is the doc comment made executable, because Lesson 2.5 established that a matrix's columns are where the basis vectors land.

Nothing else was needed to make gimbal lock measurable. determinant was already there.

9.5 The demo, and the one thing the public API could not do

demos/gimbal/main.cpp follows Lesson 5.12's rules: nothing includes an engine internal, and where the public API falls short, the demo does the work in the open with a comment saying so. There is exactly one such place.

debug_lines (Lesson 5.11) can draw a line, a ray, a box, a sphere and a wire mesh. It cannot draw a circle in an arbitrary plane, which is what a gimbal ring is. sphere() comes close — it is three great circles — but its circles are axis-aligned in world space, and the entire point of a gimbal ring is that its plane has been carried by every rotation outside it. So the demo has a fifteen-line ring() helper built from line() calls, and the finding is recorded rather than quietly absorbed: an ellipse(centre, u, v, colour) taking two in-plane vectors would cover rings, orbits, cones and the field-of-view arcs a camera gizmo wants. Filed for Lesson 9.7, where the editor's gizmos need exactly this.

Two decisions inside the demo are worth naming because both were bugs first.

The weak-axis arrow is not red. The first draft drew it in red, where it was indistinguishable from the pitch ring it crosses — and worse, red means the x axis course-wide (Conventions §10), so a red arrow that is not an x axis is a convention violation as well as a legibility bug. It is magenta, which the engine's debug palette uses nowhere else.

The arrow is drawn at its true length, not normalised. It shows the rotation the weakest knob combination actually produces, so it shrinks as you approach lock and vanishes at it. A normalised arrow would still be pointing confidently somewhere at the exact moment the claim is that it points nowhere.

And one crash, for the record

The first headless --shot run of this demo exited with signal 11 after writing a correct PPM. A --shot run has no window, so ImGui has no context, and ImGui::Begin on no context is a segfault rather than a no-op. debug_ui's own entry points are all safe when it never started — Lesson 5.11 made them so deliberately — but a panel built by hand between those calls is not covered by that. The guard is if (ui_.running()), and the reason it is worth a paragraph is the shape of the failure: the program produced its output correctly and then crashed, so every artifact on disk was right and the exit status was the only thing that said otherwise.

Lesson 5.12 added a configure-time check to engine/CMakeLists.txt: glob every public header, read the #include lines out of engine/engine.hpp, and abort the configure if the umbrella is missing one. It was proved to work by deleting an include and watching CMake stop.

11. Build & run

A new target and a new public header, so CMake has to be re-run once before the first build. Watch the umbrella lint's line go past — if you add the header and forget the #include, this is where you find out.

Expected result. A 960 × 540 window with a small white aircraft at the centre of three coloured hoops — green pivoting about the vertical, red about the wingtip axis, blue about the nose axis — and a panel top-left with three sliders and five numbers.

The thing to actually do, in this order, because the point of the demo is in the sequence rather than in any one frame:

  1. Hold E. That is the joint knob: it drives yaw and roll at the same rate, which §5.3 identified as the weakest combination. At pitch 0 the aircraft spins briskly, and the magenta arrow is at full length.
  2. Hold ↑ to climb toward pitch 90, watching |det J| fall. It is cos(pitch) — check it against the pitch slider beside it.
  3. Hold E again. The rings still turn. The readout still says the knobs are moving. The aircraft stops. Press T first and the nose leaves a trail, so the stopping is a mark on the screen rather than something you have to notice.
  4. Press K to snap to exactly 90° and look at the axles. The blue one lies along the green one. The magenta arrow is gone, because its length is the rotation that combination produces, and that is now zero.

12. The golden, and why it is blind to this lesson

The reference render — eight frames of the Module 2–4 software rasterizer, hashed — comes back byte-identical, E917C06C, as it has since Lesson 5.1. That is the fifth lesson in a row for which that result is a null one, and saying so is the point of this section.

demo::write_reference_shot renders through soft_renderer.cpp, raster.cpp and framebuffer.cpp, driven by demos/common/demo_scene.cpp, and that translation unit includes exactly six engine headers: colour, debug_draw, framebuffer, depth_buffer, raster, soft_renderer. This lesson added a header with no source file that nothing in that set reaches; grep -rn 'math/euler' engine/ returns exactly one line and it is inside engine.hpp, which nothing includes. The other two edits are a comment and a CMake message. The picture cannot move, and a byte-identical result is evidence only that nobody edited a file they did not mean to. It is worth ninety seconds; it is not worth believing anything about.

Beware the grep that looks like evidence

grep -rn euler demos/common does return two hits. Neither is this lesson: they are the Euler characteristic, V − E + F, which Lesson 3.5's mesh validator prints for every loaded model. Two unrelated things named after the same man, in the one directory this argument is about. A structural claim made by grepping a name rather than an include path would have called that a dependency and gone looking for a bug that is not there.

The real instrument for this lesson is verify_71, and the thing that makes its thirty-four green ticks mean something is that six of them are controls — checks whose job is to fail when the thing beside them is measuring correctly. One of them did fail, on the first run: §C.4's control asserted that the joint knob move produces more than 80° of rotation away from lock, and it measured 65.51°. The control was right and the threshold was wrong — two orthogonal 47° turns give 47√2 = 66.47°, so 80 was never reachable. A control that fails for the wrong reason is still a control doing its job; it told us we did not know what number to expect, which we did not.

13. Common pitfalls & debugging

Everything is rotated wrongly, by a consistent-looking amount

Symptom: a model imported from somewhere else faces a plausible but wrong direction. Nudging the numbers gets it close but never right, and a value that works for one asset is wrong for the next.
Cause: you are reading somebody else's convention as yours. §3 measured the cost: from 16° to 83° depending on which of the twenty-four you have been handed.
Fix: find out which one, from the exporter's documentation, and convert explicitly in a named function. Do not tune. A tuned angle is right at one pose.

The camera flips upside-down when you look straight up

Symptom: a free-look camera behaves normally until the pitch passes vertical, then the horizon inverts and the yaw control reverses.
Cause: you have crossed the singularity. Beyond ±90° the extraction returns a different triple naming the same rotation (§6.4), and the roll that was zero is now 180°.
Fix: clamp the pitch to ±89° or so. This is not a workaround — it is the reason §2.2 chose this axis order, and every first-person camera you have used does it.

A blend spins the long way round for no reason

Symptom: a turret tracking a target 20° away performs a full revolution to get there, once, near a particular heading.
Cause: raw subtraction of two angles either side of the ±180° wrap (§7.3). The floats are 340 apart; the headings are 20 apart.
Fix: shortest_angle_delta. And note that this bug is intermittent by construction — it only appears when the pair straddles the wrap, so it will pass every test you write unless one of them is placed there deliberately.

Your "distance between rotations" is always zero

Symptom: a convergence check or a path measurement reports 0 for things that are visibly moving, or reports NaN for two identical inputs.
Cause: acos((tr − 1)/2). It returns exactly zero below about 0.01° (§8), and unclamped it returns NaN on identical inputs, because a composed rotation's trace routinely comes out at 3.0000002 and acos of anything above 1 is NaN. The identical-inputs case is the one every caller hits first.
Fix: the atan2 form, which needs no clamp and works at both ends.

A near-singular pose reports as exactly singular

Symptom: a conditioning number comes out inf, or a degenerate flag fires, at a pose that is visibly fine.
Cause: √(1 − sin²) or √(1 − |sin|). In float, both reach exactly zero about 0.01° before the pose does (§5.5).
Fix: get the cosine from somewhere it is not being subtracted out of 1 — hypot of two matrix entries that are proportional to it, or the algebraically equal |cos| / √(1 + |sin|).

A headless run writes a correct file and then crashes

Symptom: exit status 139 (SIGSEGV), after the expected output has already appeared on disk and on stdout.
Cause: in this demo, ImGui::Begin with no ImGui context, because a --shot run has no window. Generally: teardown or per-frame work that assumes a subsystem the headless path never started.
Fix: guard on the subsystem's own predicate — here if (ui_.running()). And check exit status in your scripts. Every artifact being correct is not evidence that the program succeeded.

14. Exercises

  1. Move the hole. Add rotation_from_euler_xyz — intrinsic x-y-z, the same three angles in the same struct, a different order — and a matching euler_rate_jacobian_xyz. Sweep both Jacobians' determinants over the full range of all three angles and plot where each one hits zero. Then answer in one sentence why this course did not choose x-y-z.

    Hint

    Follow §5.2's derivation with the columns relabelled. The middle angle of the sequence is the one that appears alone.

    Solution sketch

    det J = ±cos(middle angle) for any of the six three-distinct-axis orders — the derivation is structurally identical, because the middle rotation is always the one that is not conjugated by anything. For x-y-z the middle angle is the rotation about Y, which for a camera is the one you sweep continuously. The hole would be at a heading you pass through every few seconds rather than at an attitude you already forbid.

  2. Make the degenerate branch fire in a real scene. Add a key to the demo that reads the pose back out with euler_from_rotation and writes it straight into pose_ — a round trip that should be the identity. Hold it down while sweeping the pitch through 90°. Measure, with angle_between_rotations, how far the orientation moves per round trip at 89°, 89.9° and 89.99°, and explain the shape of what you find using §6.3.

    Hint

    Round-tripping a freshly-built matrix is nearly exact (§6.3's "clean" column). To see the amplification, perturb the matrix first — or compose it through a parent transform, which does the same thing for real.

  3. Find the worst pair. Search for the two orientations, each reachable with |pitch| ≤ 85°, whose Euler lerp has the largest excess turning. Report the pair, the excess, and the speed variation. Is the worst case near the singularity, or somewhere else?

    Hint

    A random search over a few hundred thousand pairs is enough and takes seconds at -O2. Use 256 steps per path while searching and re-measure the winner at 2,048 — but check that the two step counts rank pairs the same way before you trust the cheap one, or you have optimised the search and not the answer.

  4. Give the engine an ellipse. §9.5 names the one thing the public API could not do. Add debug_lines::ellipse(vec3 centre, vec3 u, vec3 v, Uint32 colour, int segments), where u and v are the in-plane semi-axes, and delete the demo's private ring(). Then decide, and write down, how many segments it should default to, and on what basis — a fixed count, a count derived from the radius, or a count derived from the radius in screen space.

    Solution sketch

    The honest answer to the last part is that debug_lines has no camera in its signature — deliberately, per Lesson 5.11 — so screen-space adaptation is not available to it without changing what the class is. A fixed default with an override is the right answer at this point in the course, and noticing why the better answer is unavailable is worth more than the code.

  5. Assert what the extraction requires. euler_from_rotation silently produces meaningless angles for a matrix with scale or shear in it, because the clamp turns nonsense into something plausible. mat4 has is_rigid (Lesson 2.9) and it works on the wrong type. Write is_rotation(const mat3&, float tolerance), decide what it should check and what tolerance a matrix composed through a ten-deep hierarchy actually needs — measure it — and then decide whether euler_from_rotation should assert on it. Argue both sides; the engine core has no exceptions (Lesson 5.3) and an assertion in a header is a choice you impose on every caller.

    Hint

    Two conditions: columns orthonormal, and determinant +1 rather than −1. The second is not optional — a reflection satisfies the first and is not a rotation. For the tolerance, build a hierarchy in the demo and print max|RᵀR − I| at the bottom of it.

15. Recap & next

The engine can now turn three human-sized numbers into an orientation and back, under a convention that is written down rather than implied by whichever call site you happen to read. That is a small header and it closes a real gap: Lesson 5.12's rover had a hand-rolled two-angle convention inside a gameplay system, and demo_scene.cpp has had two more since Module 2.

What the lesson is really for, though, is the other half — and it is worth stating as a list of things you can now do rather than things you now know:

  • You can say where a parameterisation's singularity is, and choose an axis order by deciding where you want it rather than by copying one.
  • You can compute how close you are to it — |det(euler_rate_jacobian(e))|, using a function mat3 has had since Lesson 2.6 — and turn that into the number that actually matters to a control loop: how much knob a unit of aircraft costs.
  • You can measure the distance between two orientations accurately enough to sum thousands of small steps, which is the instrument the next four lessons are going to lean on.
  • You can recognise a formula that is algebraically right and numerically useless, because you have now seen the same cancellation three times in one afternoon.

And the engine stores exactly as many Euler angles as it did before this lesson: none. transform::rotation is still a mat3. §7 is why. Three angles cannot be composed without converting to a matrix anyway, cannot be interpolated without leaving the geodesic by 14% on an ordinary pair and 209% near lock, and have a hole in them. They are an interface, and an interface is exactly the right thing for them to be.

Next is Lesson 7.2, Axis-Angle, and it starts from the thing §8 nearly computed and then deliberately did not. The metric extracts an angle from AᵀB by pulling apart its symmetric and antisymmetric halves — and the vector it throws away on the way, the one whose length is sin θ, is the axis. Four numbers instead of three, no singularity, and a formula (Rodrigues') that turns the pair back into a matrix. It fixes the hole. It does not fix interpolation, and 7.3 through 7.5 are about why that takes complex numbers.

16. Further reading

  • Ken Shoemake, "Euler Angle Conversion", Graphics Gems IV (1994). The source of the twenty-four, encoded in four bits, with conversion code that handles all of them uniformly. If you ever have to consume somebody else's convention, this is the paper that makes it mechanical instead of a puzzle.
  • Akenine-Möller, Haines & Hoffman, Real-Time Rendering 4th ed., §4.2 ("Special Matrix Transforms and Operations") and §4.2.2 ("Euler Transforms"). The standard treatment, including the extraction, with the gimbal-lock discussion kept short and honest.
  • Jim Van Verth & Lars Bishop, Essential Mathematics for Games, ch. 5. The most careful book treatment of why three parameters cannot work, and the one that is clearest that the failure is in the map rather than in the rotations.
  • David Eberly, "Euler Angle Formulas" (Geometric Tools). All twelve orders, forwards and backwards, with the degenerate branches written out. The document to check your extraction against when you implement an order this lesson did not.
  • NASA, Apollo 11 Mission Report, and Michael Collins' Carrying the Fire. The inertial platform had three gimbals rather than four, and the crew flew around a forbidden cone drawn on the instrument. Worth reading for the fact that the mitigation was procedural — the same move §2.2 makes, at a cost nobody could argue with.
  • Scratchapixel, "Transforming Objects using Matrices". Good complementary coverage of the composition order and of the row-vector convention this course does not use — useful precisely because seeing the other convention written out makes ours legible as a choice.
