Near-Plane Clipping

Every scene so far has been polite enough to stay in front of the camera. This lesson takes that away. Walk into the floor and it disappears; delete the guard that makes it disappear and it detonates across the screen instead. Both symptoms are one bug, and the fix is not a better guard — it is a pair of scissors applied at exactly the right moment in the pipeline.

1The Problem

Load the demo, press C until the scene is the checkered floor from Lesson 3.2, and hold = to walk forward. For the first few steps everything behaves. Then, all at once, the ground vanishes. Not a corner of it, not a flicker — the entire floor, gone, leaving the sky and the faint world grid and nothing to stand on.

Figure 1’s middle panel is not a hypothetical. It is src/main.cpp as it stood at the end of Lesson 3.2, and the line responsible has been sitting in collect_triangles with a comment pointing at this lesson:

You might reasonably ask what happens if we simply delete it. That is Figure 1’s right panel, and it is worse. The floor does not come back; it collapses into a thin folded band, with the near half of the ground missing and the geometry turned inside out. One hundred and eighty-eight pixels survive out of fifty-seven thousand six hundred.

So we have two failures that look nothing alike, produced by the same geometry, and the “obvious” fix for one is the other. That is a good sign that neither is really the bug — that something upstream is wrong and both are symptoms. It is, and the rest of this lesson is about what.

This is not an edge case

It is tempting to file this under “handle it later”, because the geometry in every scene so far has been a polite little object floating in the middle of the view. But the moment a camera can move through a world, geometry crossing the near plane is not the exception — it is every frame. Every wall you walk along, every floor you stand on, every doorway you pass through has part of itself behind your eye. A renderer that cannot draw those is a renderer that cannot draw a room.

There is also a smaller, quieter version of the same bug that has been in the demo since Lesson 2.9 and which you may have already noticed: walk over one of the faint world gridlines and it disappears rather than running off the bottom of the screen. Same cause, one dimension down. We will fix both, and the line version first, because it is where the idea is easiest to see.

2Building Intuition

2.1 A ray does not know it is going backwards

Lesson 2.10 built perspective projection out of similar triangles, and the picture it used was a pinhole: an eye, and a flat piece of film in front of it. To find where a point in the world lands on the film, you draw the straight line from the point to the eye and see where it crosses the film.

That construction is completely well behaved as long as the point is in front of the eye. The line goes point → film → eye, in that order, and the crossing sits between the two. Now take a point behind the eye and run the same construction. The line from the point to the eye no longer meets the film on its way — but the line does not stop at the eye. Extend it and it crosses the film anyway, on the opposite side of the axis.

That is the entire failure, and it is worth sitting with for a moment before any symbols appear. The projection formula is a ratio, x / (−z). Divide by a positive number and you shrink a coordinate toward the centre. Divide by a negative number and you shrink it and flip it to the other side. Divide by something very close to zero and you fling it to the far side of infinity. The formula does not contain a notion of “in front”; that was an assumption we were making, and we never wrote it down.

So the right question is not “how do I make the divide safe?” It is “how do I guarantee the divide is only ever asked a question it can answer?” That is not a check inside the divide. It is a precondition, and preconditions are enforced by whoever comes before.

2.2 Why a guard cannot work, and scissors can

The guard the engine has been carrying since Lesson 2.10 asks a yes/no question about a whole triangle: are all three of your corners in front of the near plane? If yes, draw it; if no, discard it. And that is where it goes wrong, because a triangle that has one corner behind the eye is not entirely behind the eye. Most of it may be right in front of you, filling your view. A yes/no answer cannot describe a partly-true situation.

The fix is to stop answering yes or no. Cut the triangle along the near plane, throw away the part behind, and rasterize the part in front. That part is a genuinely different shape, with corners the mesh never contained — corners we will have to construct, complete with whatever the original corners were carrying.

Two things follow from Figure 3 and they will shape all the code below. First, the new corners are ordinary corners in every respect — they need a position, a depth, a texture coordinate, a colour, and (as we will see) a w. Second, a function that clips a triangle cannot return a triangle. It has to return “nothing, or one triangle, or two”, and pretending otherwise is how a clipper ends up quietly dropping half of every cut surface.

3The Theory

3.1 Three regimes of w, and a discontinuity

Recall from Lesson 2.10 what the projection matrix actually does with the fourth coordinate. Its bottom row is (0, 0, −1, 0), which copies −z_view into w. So w is not an abstract bookkeeping number — it is the distance in front of the camera, signed:

\[ w_{\text{clip}} \;=\; -z_{\text{view}} \qquad \begin{cases} > 0 & \text{in front of the eye} \\ = 0 & \text{on the plane through the eye} \\ < 0 & \text{behind the eye} \end{cases} \] w_clip = -z_view : positive in front of the eye, zero on the plane through the eye, negative behind it

Now watch what the divide does as a point slides along a straight edge from in front of the eye to behind it. The numerator x_clip changes smoothly and finitely — it is just a matrix multiply. The denominator w passes through zero. So the ratio does what ratios do at a zero denominator: it runs off to +∞, is undefined for an instant, and comes back from −∞.

Figure 4 makes the choice concrete. If we clip at the green line — the near plane — every value the divide ever sees lies in the flat, bounded part of the curve. If we clip at the red line instead, we admit the whole vertical run-up, where the numbers are finite but enormous. And if we clip nowhere, we hand the rasterizer the far side of the asymptote, where the sign is wrong.

3.2 Why clipping happens before the divide

Here is the observation that fixes the architecture of the whole lesson. Look at Figure 4 again and ask: given only the output value, can you tell which side of the asymptote it came from?

You cannot. x_ndc = −0.432 is B, one unit behind the camera. It is also a perfectly ordinary point slightly left of centre and comfortably in front. The divide has collapsed two very different situations onto one number, and there is no residue left over to tell them apart. The perspective divide destroys the information that clipping needs.

So clipping cannot be a post-process. It has to run in the last space where the information still exists, and that space has a name we have been using since Lesson 2.8: clip space. It is called that for this reason. A vertex in clip space is a vec4 whose w is meaningful and may be negative — and it is the only place in the pipeline where that is true.

3.3 Which plane: deriving z_clip = 0

We know we must cut, and we know where in the pipeline. What remains is which plane, expressed in clip-space coordinates so the test is cheap.

Go back to Lesson 2.10’s projection matrix. Its third row exists to map depth, and it was built by demanding two things: that device depth come out 0 at the near plane and 1 at the far one. Written out, the row produces

\[ z_{\text{clip}} \;=\; A\,z_{\text{view}} + B, \qquad A = \frac{f}{n - f}, \qquad B = \frac{f\,n}{n - f} \] z_clip = A*z_view + B, with A = far/(near-far) and B = far*near/(near-far)

Both A and B are negative, because near < far makes the common denominator negative. Now simply ask where z_clip is zero:

\[ A\,z_{\text{view}} + B = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; z_{\text{view}} = -\frac{B}{A} = -\frac{f n /(n-f)}{f/(n-f)} = -n \] A*z_view + B = 0 gives z_view = -B/A = -near

The (n − f) and the f cancel, and what is left is exactly z_view = −near: the plane near units in front of an eye that looks down −z. The near plane, in clip space, is the coordinate plane z_clip = 0. The signed distance to it is not something we compute — it is a component we read.

Numbers — the demo’s own projection

The scene uses near = 0.3, far = 100, so n − f = −99.7 and

A = 100 / (−99.7) = −1.00300908
B = (100 × 0.3) / (−99.7) = −0.30090272
−B/A = −0.30000001   ✓ that is −near

Pushing two points through the real matrix confirms both ends of the range:

z_view = −0.3  → z_clip = 0.000000000, w = 0.3,   z_ndc = 0.000000000
z_view = −100 → z_clip = 100.000008,   w = 100, z_ndc = 1.000000119

And behind the eye, where z_view > 0: both A z_view and B are negative, so z_clip is negative with no extra test needed. Swept over every z_view from 0 to 20 in the harness, z_clip < 0 throughout.

3.4 Why w ≥ 0 is not good enough

There is an obvious rival test. If w is the distance in front of the eye, why not simply require w > 0 and be done? It reads like the direct statement of “in front of me”, and it is one comparison.

It is a different plane. w = −z_view, so w = 0 is z_view = 0: the plane through the eye. The near plane is near units further out. Between them lies a slab that the w test admits and the near test rejects — and everything in that slab divides by a number smaller than near.

The cleanest way to see the relationship is to plot the two quantities against each other. Since w = −z_view, substituting into the depth row gives z_clip = −A w + B: for a fixed projection, every point in the universe lies on one straight line in the (w, z_clip) plane.

How bad is that band? The harness walks a point through it and reports where it lands on a 320-pixel-wide framebuffer:

Every one of those rows passes w > 0. The last lands 691,714 pixels from the left edge of a framebuffer 320 pixels wide — more than two thousand screens away, from a point one ten-thousandth of a unit in front of the camera.

That is not merely ugly. Lesson 2.2 pinned the safe range of edge_function at roughly ±16,000 before its integer products overflow, and Lesson 2.1 noted that a software rasterizer walks its bounding box. Coordinates like these break both. Clipping to the near plane bounds the divide, at w ≥ near; clipping to the eye plane merely postpones the explosion.

The near plane is a w test after all

Figure 6 shows something the algebra also says: because the scene lies on a line, z_clip ≥ 0 is equivalent to w ≥ near. So you may implement it either way and get the same cut. We test z_clip because it generalises: the other five frustum planes are |x_clip| ≤ w and |y_clip| ≤ w and z_clip ≤ w, all of the same shape, and Exercise 3.3.4 turns the one-plane clipper into a six-plane one by changing a single distance function.

3.5 The crossing parameter

Cutting an edge means finding the one point on it where the distance to the plane is zero. Both endpoints already know their distance — it is z_clip, which we read for the inside test — so the work is a single interpolation.

Call the two distances da and db. A clip-space position is a linear function of the view-space position, and a straight edge is affine in its own parameter, so the distance is affine too: d(t) = da + t(db − da). Setting that to zero:

\[ t \;=\; \frac{d_a}{d_a - d_b} \] t = d_a / (d_a - d_b)

Read it out loud and it is obvious rather than memorised: da is how far inside we start, da − db is how much distance we lose over the whole edge, and the ratio is the fraction of the edge spent getting to zero. When the endpoints are on opposite sides — the only case we ever call this for — the two distances have opposite signs, so the denominator is strictly larger in magnitude than the numerator and t lands strictly inside (0, 1).

There is a small delight hiding here. Substitute the depth row and watch what happens:

\[ d_a = A z_a + B = \frac{f}{n-f}\,(z_a + n), \qquad t = \frac{\tfrac{f}{n-f}(z_a+n)}{\tfrac{f}{n-f}\big[(z_a+n)-(z_b+n)\big]} = \frac{z_a + n}{z_a - z_b} \] t = (z_a + near) / (z_a - z_b) — the factor far/(near-far) cancels

The whole far/(near − far) factor is common to both distances and cancels. The crossing parameter does not depend on the far plane, on the field of view, or on the aspect ratio at all — it is pure view-space geometry, exactly the fraction of the way from za to zb at which depth reaches −near. That is a useful sanity check to carry: if your crossing parameter changes when you change the far plane, you have a bug.

Once t is known, the new vertex is a plain lerp — of everything:

\[ \mathbf{p}_x = \mathbf{p}_a + t\,(\mathbf{p}_b - \mathbf{p}_a) \quad\text{(all four components)}, \qquad \mathbf{uv}_x = \mathbf{uv}_a + t\,(\mathbf{uv}_b - \mathbf{uv}_a), \quad \ldots \] crossing position = lerp(p_a, p_b, t) over all four components; every attribute lerps with the same t
This t is not a screen-space fraction

Lesson 3.2 spent itself proving that screen position is a/w, which is not affine in the 3-D parameter. So the crossing vertex will not appear at the fraction t along the on-screen segment, and it must not. In the worked example below, t = 0.783 and yet the crossing lands well outside the segment A–B as drawn. Clipping before the divide and correcting attributes after it are the same fact seen from two directions: clip space is where things are still linear.

3.6 A worked example, by hand

Take an edge of a wall running left-to-right and toward the camera. In view space, with the demo’s projection (fovy = 55°, aspect = 16/9, near = 0.3, far = 100, and the full 320×180 viewport):

A = (−0.40, −0.15, −5.00)   five units in front, u = 0
B = ( +0.40, −0.15, +1.00)   one unit behind the eye, u = 1

Step 1 — the two distances

z_clip = A z_view + B with A = −1.00300908 and B = −0.30090272:

d_a = (−1.00300908)(−5.00) + (−0.30090272) = +4.714142
d_b = (−1.00300908)(+1.00) + (−0.30090272) = −1.303912

Opposite signs, so the edge crosses. Also note the w values, which are just −z_view: w_a = +5, w_b = −1. B’s w is negative, which is the whole problem in one number.

Step 2 — the crossing parameter

t = d_a / (d_a − d_b) = 4.714142 / (4.714142 + 1.303912) = 4.714142 / 6.018054 = 0.7833333

Check it against the view-space form, which needs no matrix at all: t = (z_a + n)/(z_a − z_b) = (−5 + 0.3)/(−5 − 1) = −4.7 / −6 = 47/60 = 0.7833333. The two agree exactly, as §3.5 promised.

Step 3 — the new vertex

In view space the crossing is A + t(B − A) with B − A = (0.8, 0, 6):

x = −0.40 + 0.8 × 0.7833333 = +0.2266667
y = −0.15 (unchanged)
z = −5.00 + 6 × 0.7833333 = −5 + 4.7 = −0.3000000  ✓ exactly the near plane

Its w is therefore 0.3, and lerping the clip-space w values gives the same thing: 5 + 0.7833333 × (−1 − 5) = 5 − 4.7 = 0.3. Its texture coordinate is u = 0 + 0.7833333 × 1 = 0.7833333, which is the ray–plane intersection’s answer and not an approximation of it.

Step 4 — where the three points land

Divide and apply the viewport (f = 1.9209821, f/aspect = 1.0805524):

A   w = +5.0   ndc.x = −0.0864442  → pixel (146, 95)
X   w = +0.3   ndc.x = +0.8164183  → pixel (291, 176), depth 0.000000
B   w = −1.0   ndc.x = −0.4322210 → pixel (91, 64)

Look at B. In view space it is at x = +0.40, to the right of the eye and below it. Divided by its negative w, it lands at ndc.x = −0.43 — to the left of centre — and ndc.y = +0.29, above centre. Both signs reversed, exactly as Figure 2 drew it.

The consequence is Figure 7: the edge that ought to run from pixel 146 down-and-right to pixel 291 is instead drawn from 146 up-and-left to 91. Not stretched, not slightly off — pointing the other way.

3.7 Sutherland–Hodgman

We can cut an edge. A triangle has three of them, and cutting each one independently would give us a bag of fragments rather than a polygon. The algorithm that assembles them correctly is Sutherland–Hodgman (1974), and it is one loop.

Walk the polygon’s edges in order. For each edge, from prev to cur, decide what the output polygon should contain, based only on which side of the plane those two vertices are on:

prevcuremitwhy
insideinsidecurthe edge stayed inside; keep where it arrived
insideoutsidethe crossingthe boundary is where we leave
outsideinsidethe crossing, then curwe re-enter, then arrive
outsideoutsidenothingthis edge never touched the inside

Three observations make the table stop being something to memorise. First, a vertex is emitted when an edge arrives at it and it is inside — so each vertex is considered exactly once and nothing is emitted twice. Second, a crossing is emitted whenever the two ends disagree, in either direction. Third — and this is how the code below is written — the four rows collapse into two independent questions:

  • Did this edge cross the plane? Then emit the crossing.
  • Is cur inside? Then emit cur.

Four branches that are really two is an invitation to typo one of them, so we write the two.

The output is still convex and still wound the same way as the input, which buys us two things. It lets the caller fan it into triangles as (out[0], out[k−1], out[k]), and it means Lesson 3.4’s back-face test will still see the winding it expects. Neither is obvious enough to assume; both are checked in the harness by sliding a triangle through the plane and asserting the sign of its screen-space area never changes.

Try it. The widget below is the same algorithm, viewed from above: the camera sits at the bottom looking up the page, the gold line is the near plane, and the strip on the right is the screen. Drag the triangle’s corners through the plane and watch what each mode does.

3.8 Only the near plane, and why that is not laziness

A view frustum has six planes. We are clipping against exactly one, and that asymmetry deserves an argument rather than a shrug.

The near plane is a correctness requirement. Without it the divide is undefined, or defined and wrong. There is no picture you can draw of a triangle straddling the eye without cutting it; the arithmetic does not have an answer to give.

The other five are an optimisation. A triangle that falls off the left of the screen is drawn correctly today: fill_triangle computes a bounding box, clamps it to the framebuffer, and iterates the part that remains. The pixels that would have been off-screen are simply never visited. What we waste is the bounding-box arithmetic and, for a triangle that hangs far off the edge, some scanline setup. Nothing is drawn wrongly and nothing is lost.

That distinction is worth internalising because it recurs. A renderer has a small number of things it must do to be right, and a large number of things it may do to be fast, and conflating them is how engines acquire code nobody can delete. Lesson 3.4’s back-face culling is squarely in the second category: it removes triangles the z-buffer would have handled correctly anyway.

What real hardware does — guard bands

GPUs make exactly this trade and then refine it. Clipping is expensive in silicon, so hardware defines a guard band: a region considerably larger than the screen inside which triangles are not clipped at all, because the rasterizer’s coordinates can represent them and scissoring will discard the off-screen pixels anyway. Only geometry that exceeds the guard band gets the full clipper — and the near plane, whose violation cannot be scissored away, is always clipped. That is precisely the correctness / optimisation split, implemented in transistors. Fabian Giesen’s pipeline series (§10 of Further Reading) has the details.

3.9 The escape route, priced

Before writing the clipper, it is worth taking seriously the obvious alternative: keep the dropping rule, and just make the triangles small. The strip that gets thrown away is one cell deep, so cutting cells in half halves it. Subdivide enough and surely the hole becomes invisible?

It does. That is the honest answer, and the interesting one is what it costs. Here is the demo’s own floor, measured by rendering the scene twice — once clipped, once dropped — and counting the pixels that differ, at every camera position the demo allows:

And it only works at all because the demo will not let the camera approach closer than one unit — move that limit and the table moves with it. The clipper below is about thirty lines and it is exact: it does not shrink the artifact, it removes the reason for it.

A measurement that changed what this section says

The first draft of this lesson asserted that “no amount of subdivision removes the artifact”. The harness disagreed: at 8×8 on the demo’s floor, dropping and clipping produced bit-identical frames. The reason is specific and instructive — on a ground plane the strip that straddles the near plane is the one under and behind your feet, so once it is thin enough it falls off the bottom of the view and costs nothing. The claim was wrong; the numbers above are what replaced it, and they make a better argument than the false version did. Pedagogy §5 works both ways: show the failure, and let the measurement correct you when it is you who is wrong.

4Implementation

The theory gives us four things to build: a vertex type that lives in clip space, a signed distance, a crossing parameter, and an interpolation. Then two consumers — a segment clipper and a polygon clipper — and finally a rewiring of the demo’s pipeline so the divide happens after the cut instead of before it.

4.1 A second vertex type, on purpose

engine::vertex (raster.hpp) is a screen-space type: integer pixels, a device depth in [0,1], and an inv_w that has already been computed. Every one of those fields assumes the divide has happened, which is exactly what makes it the wrong type to clip with. So the clipper gets its own:

Two types rather than one flexible type is a real decision and worth defending. A single struct with a “have I been divided yet” flag would compile, and would let a screen-space vertex be handed to the clipper — which would silently produce nonsense, because the clipper’s arithmetic is only valid before the divide. Making the two states two types makes that call fail to compile instead. The pipeline now reads:

model → world → view → CLIP → [clip_vertex] → divide → viewport → [vertex]

4.2 Three small functions that carry the whole idea

The distance to the near plane is not computed — it is read, which is the payoff from §3.3. Writing it as a named function rather than inlining p.z everywhere is not ceremony: it is the one place the argument for which plane can live, and it is what Exercise 3.3.4 replaces to get the other five.

near_crossing takes both distances rather than recomputing them, for two reasons. The caller has already evaluated them to decide there was a crossing, so recomputing is waste; and having them side by side makes the classic sign slip — writing db / (db - da) — visible rather than plausible.

Then the interpolation. Every component uses the same t, w included:

Two details in there are easy to skip and expensive to skip. The first is that the position lerp covers all four components. Interpolating x, y, z and forgetting w gives a vertex in the right place along the edge with the wrong depth scale, so the triangle comes out the right shape and the wrong size — and only when it is clipped, which is the worst possible time to acquire a bug.

The second is that colour blends in linear light, decoding and re-encoding around the lerp. That is Lesson 1.6’s rule and Lesson 2.4’s measurement applied one level up: a stored sRGB value is not a quantity of light, so averaging stored values does not average light. Doing it the lazy way would darken the manufactured corner, and the darkening would appear only on clipped geometry. The cost is two table lookups and one pow per clipped edge — per frame, not per pixel.

4.3 The line case first

A segment against a plane has exactly three outcomes, and you can hold all three in your head at once. This is worth writing before the polygon version, both because the demo genuinely needs it (the world grid, the object axes, every wireframe edge) and because it is where the idea is unobscured.

The common case is tested first deliberately. Almost every line in almost every frame is wholly in front of the camera, and a renderer whose fast path is its last branch pays for the exceptional case on every ordinary one.

Note also which endpoint moves. The outside one is replaced; the inside one is left untouched, bit for bit. If both were recomputed — even by a lerp that should be the identity — the visible end of the line would shift by a rounding, and a wall’s edge would crawl as you walked toward it.

4.4 The polygon case

Now the loop from §3.7, written as the two questions rather than the four cases:

(d_prev < 0) != (d_cur < 0) is “the signs disagree”, with zero counting as inside on both sides — so a vertex lying exactly on the plane never manufactures a spurious crossing.

There is no capacity check inside that loop, and that is a claim rather than an oversight. Emissions are (vertices that end up inside) + (crossings); one plane produces at most one crossing in each direction around a convex polygon; a triangle has three vertices. The worst case is therefore 2 + 2 = 4, which is exactly k_clip_max_vertices. The bound is a proof, not a margin — and Lesson 3.2 left a note about why that matters: a limit that silently truncates turns a capacity bug into a rendering bug. The right move is to make the bound provable, not to clamp.

C++ in place — a raw array converts to a std::span

The caller writes clip_vertex src[3] and clip_vertex poly[4] and passes them straight in:

n = engine::clip_polygon_near(src, poly);

No length argument, and no chance of passing the wrong one. std::span has a constructor template that takes a reference to an array of known size, so the compiler reads the length out of the type clip_vertex(&)[3] and builds the span itself. This is the C++20 replacement for the (pointer, count) pair that C APIs pass, and its whole value is that the count cannot disagree with the pointer.

The conversion works for std::array and std::vector too, which is why mesh (Lesson 2.12) can view either. It does not work for a pointer — a pointer has no length — and that is the point: a span is a view with a size, and anything that cannot supply a size has to say so explicitly.

4.5 Rewiring the pipeline

The demo’s project() used to do three things in one breath: matrix, divide, viewport. It has to be split, because the cut happens between the first and the second.

screen_from_clip assumes its input has already been clipped. That is the whole contract, and it is why there is no guard: a w at or behind the eye is not a case to branch on here, it is a case that must not reach here. The nudge off exact zero is demo scaffolding for the deliberately-broken mode below, not engine policy — under clip that branch is unreachable, because the near plane sits a whole near in front of w = 0.

Notice what screen_point lost: its visible flag. Before this lesson the projection answered “…and is this point usable at all?”, which was the polite way of saying the pipeline had no plan for geometry near the eye. Now it has one, and it runs a stage earlier.

Three parameters now travel together everywhere — the projection, the viewport, and the near-plane policy — so they become one object. This is the same argument fill_style made in Lesson 3.2, one level up, and it is also the shape Module 5’s renderer has:

Every function that draws in 3-D — line3, line3_world, draw_world, draw_mesh, draw_axes3, collect_triangles — now takes one const projector& where it used to take two loose arguments. Adding this lesson’s knob made the call sites shorter.

4.6 Fanning, in collect_triangles

The per-vertex loop now stops at clip space instead of running through to pixels, and the divide moves inside the per-triangle loop, after the clipper:

k = 2 … n-1 is a triangle fan: three vertices give one triangle, four give two, zero gives none, and the loop needs no special cases. It is correct because Sutherland–Hodgman preserves both convexity and winding, so every piece comes out wound the way the original was.

Moving the divide inside the triangle loop costs something worth naming: a shared vertex is now divided once per triangle that uses it, so the icosahedron does 60 divides instead of 12. That is paid knowingly. The alternative is a two-pass scheme — divide the unclipped vertices, then re-divide only the clipped ones — which is more code, more state, and wrong in ways that are easy to miss. Real hardware pays it too: the GPU runs the vertex shader, clips, and then divides, which is exactly this order.

One more detail, small and load-bearing. The painter’s-algorithm sort key from Lesson 3.1 is computed before clipping and shared by every piece:

Give the pieces their own keys and one half of a wall can sort in front of the other half. They are the same surface; a sort must not be able to tell them apart.

4.7 Two guards against undefined behaviour

The none mode exists to be shown failing, and a failure you cannot look at is useless. But letting the divide run means w can approach zero, which means an interpolated 1/w can pass through zero, which means a per-pixel reciprocal can be an infinity — and 0 × ∞ is a NaN. Converting either to an int is undefined behaviour: not “some arbitrary number”, but a program with no defined meaning at all.

Three places needed a guard, and each one is a genuine hardening rather than a demo hack. First, pixel coordinates:

The bound is 8,000 rather than something larger for a second reason: edge_function multiplies coordinate differences, and at ±8,000 the products stay comfortably inside a 32-bit int. Signed overflow is undefined too. One constant holds off two undefined behaviours.

Second, the checker pattern, which converts a floored uv to an int:

Magenta is the long-standing debug convention for “this value is invalid”, and here it is genuinely informative: it marks the exact pixels where the divide broke. Note the shape of the test — !(x < limit) rather than x >= limit, because every comparison against a NaN is false and only the negated form catches it.

Third, colour encoding, which converts a float to a Uint8:

std::clamp cannot do this job: it finds a NaN neither below the low bound nor above the high one, and hands it straight back. For every value that is not a NaN the line changes nothing — negatives and zero both encoded to 0 before, and they still do.

Why harden the engine for a broken demo mode?

Because the NaN is not really the demo’s. Module 6 puts an HDR pipeline through linear_to_srgb_u8, and Module 7’s physics will produce the occasional NaN the way all physics does. A cast that is undefined for a value the program can reach is a latent bug regardless of which lesson first reaches it. Fixing it here, where we can name exactly how the NaN arrives, is cheaper than fixing it in Module 6 where we could not.

The interpolation comparison in Lesson 3.2 and the hidden-surface comparison in Lesson 3.1 could share a single list of triangles, because only the shading changed. 7Common Pitfalls & Debugging

Triangles smear across the whole screen when you approach a wall

Cause. No near-plane clipping, or clipping that runs after the perspective divide. A vertex behind the eye has w < 0, the divide flips both its signs, and the triangle is drawn to a point on the opposite side of the frame. §3.6 has the numbers: a corner at view x = +0.40 lands at ndc.x = −0.43.

Why post-divide clipping cannot work. The divide has already collapsed “slightly left of centre, in front” and “behind the camera” onto the same number. There is nothing left in the output to test.

Fix. Clip in clip space, against z_clip ≥ 0, before perspective_divide is ever called. The test that proves it: sweep the camera through the geometry and assert no output vertex has |z_clip / w| greater than a rounding.

Almost fixed — but a thin sliver still flickers wildly at the very edge

Cause. You clipped against w ≥ 0 instead of z_clip ≥ 0. Those are different planes: w = 0 passes through the eye, and the near plane is near units further out. Everything in the gap survives your test and divides by a number smaller than near.

Why it is so hard to spot. The gap is near units thick — 30 cm in this demo — so almost nothing is ever in it. When something is, it is there for one or two frames and produces a flash that is gone before you can pause. §3.4’s table shows a point 0.0001 units in front of the eye landing 691,714 pixels from the left edge.

Fix. Test z_clip. If you prefer to think in w, the equivalent test is w ≥ near — never w ≥ 0.

The clipped part is the right shape but the wrong size, or its texture is scaled oddly

Cause. The crossing vertex interpolated x, y, z and not w. It is an easy omission because three of the four components are the ones you think of as “the position”.

Why the symptom is confusing. The vertex lands in the right place along the edge in 3-D, so a wireframe of the clipped polygon looks plausible. What is wrong is the scale it is divided by, so the error appears only after the divide, and only on clipped geometry — which is to say, only when you walk into something.

Fix, and the test. Lerp the whole vec4. Then assert the thing that must be true: a vertex on the near plane has w exactly equal to near, because w = −z_view and z_view = −near there. The harness checks it on 68,594 random crossings; worst error 1.7×10⁻⁶.

Half of every cut surface is missing

Cause. The clipper was written to return a triangle. Two corners in front of the plane produce a quadrilateral, and if the caller reads three vertices and stops, it draws half of it.

Why it survives testing. The one-corner-in-front case — which is what you get when you poke a single vertex through the plane while debugging — does produce a triangle. The bug only appears when two corners cross, which needs the camera to be genuinely inside the surface’s extent.

Fix. Return a count, size the output array at k_clip_max_vertices (4 for one plane), and fan with for (k = 2; k < n; ++k). Then test all four configurations explicitly: 3 in → 3 out, 2 in → 4 out, 1 in → 3 out, 0 in → 0 out.

Clipped triangles disappear once back-face culling is switched on

Cause. The clipper reversed the winding, so the surviving pieces face the wrong way. Usually this is an emission-order slip — emitting cur before the crossing on a re-entering edge — or fanning as (k-1, k, 0) instead of (0, k-1, k).

Why you will meet this in Lesson 3.4 and not now. With no culling, a backwards triangle still fills: fill_triangle measures the sign of the area and orients itself. The bug is invisible until something starts reading the winding.

Fix, and the test that actually works. Do not compare the clipped polygon’s screen area against the unclipped triangle’s — the unclipped one has a vertex behind the eye, so its projection is exactly the garbage this lesson exists to prevent, and the comparison is meaningless. (That test was written for this lesson, “failed”, and the clipper was innocent.) Instead slide a triangle steadily through the plane, clipping at every step, and assert the sign of the clipped area never changes. It cannot: the shape does not turn inside out as it moves.

“I’ll just push the near plane out and avoid the whole thing”

Cause. A reasonable instinct, and it does reduce how often geometry straddles — by moving the plane further from the eye, where there is less scene.

Why it is a bad trade in both directions. It does not remove the case, it makes it more visible when it happens: a larger near means the cut edge is further away and the missing wedge is bigger. And it is not free elsewhere — Lesson 3.1 §3.6 measured the depth-precision cost of moving the near plane, and it is the dominant term: near from 1 to 0.1 costs 10.09× the precision everywhere in the scene, so the reverse buys it. You are trading one real problem for another to avoid thirty lines.

Fix. Choose near for depth precision, which is what it is for, and clip. They are independent decisions and this lesson is what makes them independent.

8Exercises

3.3.1Move the near planewarm-up

Change k_scene_near in src/main.cpp from 0.3f to 3.0f. Predict what happens to the floor scene with clipping switched on, then run it.

Solution

A wedge of ground disappears from in front of you — and the renderer is behaving perfectly. Clipping removes what is nearer than the near plane, and you have just declared that everything within three metres is nearer than the near plane. The cut edge is a clean straight line across the bottom of the view, which is the visual signature of a near plane that is too far out.

The point of the exercise is the pairing. Lesson 3.1 §3.6 measured that pushing near outward is the single most effective thing you can do for depth precision — ten times the resolution for one decimal place. This lesson shows what it costs: geometry you wanted to see, cut away. The two pressures are real and opposed, and the reason engines agonise over the near plane is that there is no setting that avoids both. What clipping buys you is the freedom to choose near on precision grounds alone, instead of picking a value small enough that nothing ever crosses it.

3.3.2Fan the quad the other waywarm-up

When the clipper returns four vertices we fan from poly[0]. Fan from poly[1] instead — (poly[1], poly[2], poly[3]) and (poly[1], poly[3], poly[0]) — and compare the two images pixel for pixel. Then press I and compare again.

Hint

The demo already has the machinery: render both and call count_differences. Think about what a triangle’s interior is a function of, under each interpolation mode.

Solution

Under perspective-correct interpolation the two images are identical, because the interpolated value at a pixel reconstructs the surface, and the surface does not know how you cut it into triangles. Under affine interpolation they are different, because affine interpolation reconstructs the triangulation rather than the surface — which is Exercise 3.2.1’s finding, arriving again from a completely different direction.

This is worth doing because it is a decisive test you can apply to any renderer, including one you did not write: if the output depends on how a polygon happened to be cut into triangles, something is interpolating in the wrong space.

3.3.3A surface that faces youmedium

§3.9 measured how dropping behaves on a ground plane, and found that fine tessellation hides it — because the straddling strip is the one under your feet. Add a scene_kind::corridor: two vertical walls flanking the camera’s path, tessellated the same way the floor is, so T subdivides them. Then measure drop against clip across the camera range and compare your curve to the floor’s.

Hint

build_floor is almost the function you want — parameterise which two axes the grid runs along, and derive the uvs from the world position exactly as it does, so subdivision changes the geometry and not the pattern. For the camera, the walls need to run along the dolly direction (in z) rather than across it: a wall perpendicular to your view is either wholly in front of the near plane or wholly behind it, and straddles only within a window near units wide.

Solution sketch

Expect the same qualitative shape and a different constant. The dropped strip is still one cell deep and still shrinks like 1/N; what changes is where on screen it is. On the floor it is at the bottom edge and leaves the frame early; on a wall beside you it is at the side, and on a wall angled across your path it is nearer the centre and survives longer.

The lesson to take from your own numbers is the one §3.9 draws: subdivision is not a fix, it is a bet that the hole will land somewhere the player is not looking. It is also unavailable for any geometry you did not author — a mesh loaded from disk in Lesson 3.5 has the triangle count it has.

3.3.4All six planesopen

Generalise the clipper to the whole frustum. In clip space the six planes are −w ≤ x ≤ w, −w ≤ y ≤ w, 0 ≤ z ≤ w, so each one is a signed distance of the same shape: for example w − x for the right plane, and x + w for the left. Run Sutherland–Hodgman six times, ping-ponging between two buffers. Then measure whether it was worth it.

Hint

The output bound grows: each plane can add at most one vertex, so a triangle against six planes needs 9 slots, not 4. Everything else is unchanged — near_crossing and lerp do not care which plane produced the distances, which is exactly why they take distances rather than computing them.

For the measurement: the rasterizer already clamps its bounding box, so what you are buying is the setup for pixels that were never going to be visited. Time the same camera sweep with and without, and be honest about the result.

Solution sketch

The code is a small refactor: an array of six distance functions (or one function taking a plane index), two clip_vertex buffers of nine, and a loop that swaps them. The interesting part is that you will probably find it is slower at this resolution. Six polygon passes per triangle is real work, and the bounding-box clamp it replaces is four min/max operations. The win appears when triangles are large relative to the screen and mostly off it — which is why hardware uses a guard band instead: skip the side planes entirely unless the triangle is enormous.

Either result is a good outcome, as long as it is measured. §3.8’s distinction is the thing to hold on to: near-plane clipping had to be right; this one only had to be worth it, and “worth it” is a number.

3.3.5Find the guard bandopen

Implement guard-band clipping. Clip the near plane always; clip the five side planes only when a triangle’s screen bounding box exceeds ±G pixels. Find the G that is fastest for this rasterizer, and find the G above which the renderer becomes incorrect rather than merely slow.

Hint

The upper limit is not a performance question. edge_function (raster.hpp) multiplies coordinate differences in 32-bit integers, and its documented safe range is about ±16,000. That is a hard ceiling on G and it is worth computing exactly rather than trusting the comment — work out the largest coordinate for which (bx−ax)*(py−ay) cannot overflow, and compare.

Solution sketch

A good answer states two numbers and how each was obtained: a correctness ceiling derived from the integer range, and a performance optimum found by measurement. Real hardware picks its guard band the same way — the size is set by the number of subpixel bits in the rasterizer’s fixed-point coordinates, not by taste.

If you want to go further: what happens to to_pixel’s ±8,000 clamp once the guard band exists? It should become unnecessary for correct geometry — and that is a nice property to assert rather than assume.

9Recap & Next

The engine can now be walked through. That sentence is worth more than it looks: until this lesson every scene had to be arranged so the camera stayed politely outside it, and that restriction was invisible precisely because we kept obeying it.

  • The perspective divide has a precondition, not a guard. x/w cannot be made safe from the inside; it can only be promised an input it can answer. That promise is enforced one stage earlier.
  • Clip space earns its name. It is the last place where w’s sign still exists, so it is the only place clipping can happen. The divide is the one information-destroying step in the whole pipeline.
  • The near plane is z_clip = 0, because Lesson 2.10’s depth row was built to make it so. Not w ≥ 0, which is the plane through the eye and admits coordinates in the hundreds of thousands.
  • Sutherland–Hodgman is one loop and two questions, it preserves convexity and winding, and a triangle against one plane comes back as at most four vertices — a bound that is proved, not assumed.
  • A crossing vertex is a whole vertex. All four position components, every attribute, one shared t — and that t is a 3-D parameter, not a screen-space one, which is Lesson 3.2’s theorem wearing a different hat.
  • Correctness and optimisation are different categories. The near plane is the first; the other five are the second; and knowing which is which is what lets you decide when to skip one.

The engine now draws every triangle it is given, correctly, from any camera position. Which raises an obvious question: does it need to draw all of them?

Lesson 3.4 — Back-Face Culling is the answer, and it also settles a debt. Lesson 3.1 measured that the painter’s algorithm and the z-buffer disagreed by 29 pixels on the solids scene, tracked it to silhouette ties between front and back faces, and predicted that removing back faces would take it to zero. We will remove them — using the winding this lesson was careful to preserve, and the cross product from Lesson 1.7 — and check whether the counter reads what 3.1 said it would.

10Further Reading

  • Ivan Sutherland and Gary Hodgman, “Reentrant Polygon Clipping”, Communications of the ACM 17(1), 1974. The original, and short. Worth reading for the framing: the algorithm is presented as a pipeline of one-plane clippers, each feeding the next, which is exactly the structure Exercise 3.3.4 asks you to build.
  • James Blinn and Martin Newell, “Clipping using homogeneous coordinates”, SIGGRAPH 1978. The paper that established clipping before the divide as the right answer rather than a convenience. If §3.2 felt like the interesting idea in this lesson, this is where it comes from.
  • Jim Blinn, Jim Blinn’s Corner: A Trip Down the Graphics Pipeline — the clipping chapters. Blinn works through homogeneous clipping with more care about degenerate cases than anyone else, including what happens to points with w = 0 exactly.
  • Fabian Giesen, “A trip through the Graphics Pipeline 2011” — the primitive-assembly and clip/cull instalment. How a real GPU does it, and the clearest available explanation of guard-band clipping and why hardware avoids the general clipper wherever it can.
  • Real-Time Rendering, 4th edition — the clipping and geometry-stage sections. Short, and good on where clipping sits relative to culling in a modern pipeline.
  • Cohen–Sutherland and Liang–Barsky line clipping. The 2-D ancestors of §4.3, and worth half an hour: Cohen–Sutherland’s outcodes are the trick that lets a clipper reject most geometry with a bitwise AND, which is how a six-plane clipper stays cheap.
  • Scratchapixel, “Rasterization: a Practical Implementation”. Covers the same ground with the same from-first-principles bent, and is a useful second voice on the near plane specifically.
