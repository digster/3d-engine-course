Complex Numbers Rotate the Plane

Lesson 7.2 finished with a list of things axis-angle cannot do, and the first item on it was fatal: there is no usable formula for composing two turns. Given one axis and angle and another, "do both" means building two matrices, multiplying them, and extracting again — three trigonometric round trips to answer a question that ought to be one multiplication. This lesson goes down a dimension and finds a representation where it is one multiplication. Not by cleverness: the arithmetic of complex numbers, invented for unrelated reasons two centuries before anyone drew a polygon, turns out to be the arithmetic of turning and scaling the plane, and we derive that rather than announce it. The single input is a measurement anyone can make with a piece of paper — a quarter turn, done twice, reverses direction — and i² = −1 falls out of it as a consequence rather than an axiom. From there: the product as a spiral similarity, with (3+4i)(1+2i) pushed through by hand; the proof that complex numbers are the 2×2 rotation-and-scale matrices, which is also the proof that a mat2 holding a rotation stores cos θ and sin θ twice; and Euler's formula derived from a differential equation you can see, because the velocity of a point going round a circle is perpendicular to where it is, and perpendicular is multiplication by i. Then the part that pays for the whole module. Two reflections make a rotation of twice the angle between the mirrors — which means the object you build a rotation out of carries half its angle. That is the θ/2 in every quaternion, visible here in the plane with a protractor, and it brings the double cover with it: two mirrors-apart objects per rotation, exactly, for the same reason. We measure all of it — 1.88× cheaper to compose, 1.05× (a wash) to apply, 6.15× less drift, and a renormalise so cheap it has no square root and a failure mode so quiet that a unit test on |z| certifies a function that turns the object 180° the wrong way. Interpolation gets the treatment 7.5 will inherit: nlerp travels exactly the right path at exactly the wrong speed, and the speed ratio is sec²(Ω/2), derived and then measured at seven arcs. And we end by proving the fourth dimension is forced: assume a three-dimensional number system with i² = j² = −1, ask where ij goes, and three lines of algebra demand a real number whose square is −1. Hamilton took thirteen years. Lesson 7.4 starts where those three lines finish.

1. The problem: three representations, none of which composes

The engine now has three ways to write down a rotation, and it is worth laying them next to each other before adding a fourth, because the fourth exists to fix something the other three share.

A mat3 (Lesson 2.5) stores nine floats and composes beautifully: multiply two matrices and you have the composite rotation, no trigonometry involved. It is also the thing the renderer actually wants, which is why it is what the engine stores. But nine floats hold three degrees of freedom, so six of them are redundant, and redundancy in floating point means drift: compose a million rotations and the result is measurably no longer a rotation. Worse, you cannot interpolate one. Average two rotation matrices entrywise and the answer is not a rotation at all — it is some squashed thing that collapses space a little.

euler_angles (Lesson 7.1) stores three floats and is the only representation a human can type. It is also the one with twenty-four incompatible readings, a singularity that no choice of axis order escapes, and an interpolation that wastes between 14% and 209% of its turning depending on where the pose happens to sit.

axis_angle (Lesson 7.2) stores four floats, has exactly one reading, and interpolates along the exact geodesic — 0.00% wasted turning at every pose, which was that lesson's payoff. And then §10 of that lesson listed what it costs, and the first item was not about speed:

What Lesson 7.2 §10 actually said

"There is no usable formula for composing two axis-angle rotations. Given (n₁, θ₁) and (n₂, θ₂), the axis and angle of the composite is not a short expression in them — the standard route is to build both matrices, multiply, and extract, which is two sin/cos pairs, nine multiply-adds, an atan2, a sqrt and a division, to answer a question that in a better representation would be one multiplication."

Composition is not an exotic operation. It is what a scene graph does every frame (Lesson 5.7's transform hierarchy is a tree of compositions), what animation blending does between every pair of keyframes, and what a physics solver does when it integrates angular velocity. A representation that cannot compose cheaply is a representation you convert out of constantly, and every conversion is trigonometry.

So the question for this lesson is precise: is there a way of writing down a rotation such that composing two of them is arithmetic — no trigonometry, no matrix product, no extraction? And 7.2 §10 sharpened it into four requirements: compose without trigonometry, interpolate along the geodesic, fit in four floats or fewer, and renormalise cheaply.

In three dimensions the answer is a quaternion, and it is the subject of Lesson 7.4. We are not going there yet, and the reason is not that quaternions are hard. It is that in the plane the same question has an answer that is completely elementary, that you can check with a ruler, and that turns out — this is the part that matters — to contain every idea the four-dimensional version needs. Every one. The half-angle, the double cover, the conjugate-as-inverse, the slerp formula, the drift and its cheap fix. All of it is visible in two dimensions, where nothing is mysterious.

Why a whole lesson in the plane

Because the plane is the only place in this course where your eye is a valid instrument. There is no camera, no projection, no foreshortening: a length on the page is the length in the maths and an angle on the page is the angle. You can put a protractor on Figure 5 and check the central claim of Lesson 7.4 with it.

Everything Module 7 does from here is the same set of ideas in a space you cannot draw. Spending five hours where you can draw it is the cheapest five hours in the module.

2. Building intuition: what if turning were a number?

Here is a childish question that turns out not to be childish at all.

Multiplication by a positive real number does something geometric: multiplying every point of the plane by 3 pushes everything three times further from the origin. It is a transformation, and it is one we already think of as a number. Multiplying by −1 is also a transformation — every point goes to the opposite side of the origin — and that is a half turn. So the number line already contains one rotation: −1 is the number that turns the plane by 180°.

Nobody finds that strange. And yet it is a peculiar thing when you look at it directly, because "multiply by −1" is not obviously about turning; it is about sign. The turning interpretation is available only because the plane happens to be there. So: what happens if we take it seriously and ask for the rest?

If −1 is a half turn, is there a number that is a quarter turn? There had better be, because doing a quarter turn twice is a half turn, so this hypothetical number q would have to satisfy q · q = −1. On the number line there is no such q: squares of reals are never negative. But we have not been asking about the number line. We have been asking about the plane, and a quarter turn of the plane manifestly exists — you can do it with your hand.

That is the whole idea, and it is worth sitting with for a moment before the algebra starts. The obstruction is not that the quarter turn does not exist. It is that the number line is too small to hold it. The plane is not. And if the plane's points are going to be numbers, then the transformations of the plane and the points of the plane are the same set of things — which means "rotate by this much and scale by that much" becomes a single multiplication, exactly as we asked for in §1.

Two more observations before we build anything, because they set expectations that the rest of the lesson has to meet.

Rotations of the plane commute, and rotations of space do not. Turn a piece of paper 30° and then 50° or 50° and then 30°, and it is in the same place both times. Now do the same with a book in three dimensions — 90° about one axis, then 90° about another — and the two orders give visibly different results. So whatever algebra describes plane rotations is allowed to be commutative, and whatever algebra describes space rotations cannot be. That is a prediction about Lesson 7.4 that we can make before starting.

And a rotation of the plane needs exactly one number. One angle, one degree of freedom. A rotation of space needs three. So the plane's answer will be smaller than the one we eventually want, and the interesting question is which parts survive the move up. The answer, spoiled now because the surprise is better used elsewhere: all of them. Every structural fact in this lesson holds in four dimensions with one word changed.

3. The quarter turn, and what it forces

Let us do the thing properly. We will define one operator geometrically, and derive every piece of algebra from it, so that at no point is anything postulated.

The definition. Let i be the operation "turn the plane a quarter circle anticlockwise about the origin". That is a definition of a transformation, not of a number, and it involves no algebra whatsoever.

The measurement. Apply i to the point (1, 0). It lands on (0, 1). Apply it again: (−1, 0). So performing i twice takes every point to the point opposite it through the origin, which is exactly what multiplying by −1 does. In the compact form:

\[ i^2 = -1 \] i squared = -1

That equation is normally introduced as a definition — "let i be a number whose square is −1" — and it sounds like a licence somebody granted themselves. It is not. Read the two paragraphs above again: nothing was granted. A quarter turn was performed twice and the result was observed. i² = −1 is a measurement of the plane. Figure 1 is that measurement, drawn.

The harness checks it, and checks it in a way worth noticing: not by asserting i * i == -1 on a type that was built to make that true, but by running the geometric definition — build the rotation by 90° out of cos and sin, apply it twice to (1, 0) — and seeing where the point lands. 

A. THE QUARTER TURN
   quarter turn of (1,0):        (-0.0000000, 1.0000000)
   and again:                    (-1.0000000, -0.0000001)
  [PASS] A.1  a quarter turn twice is multiplication by -1
   i * i =                       -1.0 +0.0i
  [PASS] A.2  i * i == -1 exactly

The -0.0000000 in the first line is cos(π/2), which is −4.37e−8 rather than zero because π/2 is not exactly representable in a float. It is worth a glance because it is the first appearance of something §11 will make a great deal of: the trigonometric route to a rotation carries a small error, and the algebraic route does not. The second line, i * i computed from the exact pair (0, 1), is −1 to the last bit.

3.1 Now derive the rest, and postulate nothing

We have one operator. To get an algebra we need to know what every combination of operators does, and each one is a geometric question with a geometric answer.

What is a + bi? If a means "scale by a" and i means "quarter turn", then a + bi should mean "scale by a, plus quarter-turn-and-scale-by-b" — which is to say, apply both and add the results as vectors. Apply it to (1, 0): the first part gives (a, 0), the second gives b times the quarter turn of (1, 0), which is (0, b). Sum: (a, b). So the transformation a + bi sends (1, 0) to (a, b), and the natural way to name a transformation is by where it sends (1, 0). Numbers and points of the plane are now the same objects, and that was not arranged — it fell out.

What is i · i · i? Three quarter turns, which is a three-quarter turn, which sends (1, 0) to (0, −1), which is the number −i. And four quarter turns is a full turn, which is the transformation that does nothing, which is 1. Both are also what you get by pushing i² = −1 through algebraically, and that agreement is not a coincidence — it is the content of the claim that the geometry is the algebra.

The control that makes the checks above mean something

Every check so far would also pass on a type whose multiplication was componentwise — the operation a reader might reasonably assume from the way addition works. Under that rule i · i would be (0·0, 1·1) = (0, 1) = i, which is wrong, but 1 · i = i and the identity checks would still pass.

So the harness computes a product whose answer separates the two: (2 + 3i)(4 + 5i) = −7 + 22i, where componentwise would give 8 + 15i. A check whose degenerate case is a pass is not a check — Lesson 7.1 §6, and it has not stopped being true.

4. The product, derived — and what it does geometrically

We know what a + bi means as a transformation, and we know i² = −1. That is enough to multiply, and the multiplication rule is not invented: it is forced. Take two of these things and multiply them out the way you would multiply any two brackets, treating i as an ordinary symbol, and then use i² = −1 exactly once at the end:

\[ (a + bi)(c + di) = ac + adi + bci + bd\,i^2 = (ac - bd) + (ad + bc)i \] (a + bi)(c + di) = ac + adi + bci + bd i^2 = (ac - bd) + (ad + bc) i

Four multiplies and two adds, and exactly one surprising character in the answer: the minus sign in ac − bd. It is i², and it is nothing else. If you ever forget which term the minus goes on — and you will — do not try to remember it. Multiply the brackets out again; it takes eight seconds and it cannot be got wrong.

4.1 A worked example, by hand

Take z = 3 + 4i and w = 1 + 2i. Substituting a = 3, b = 4, c = 1, d = 2:

\[ (3 + 4i)(1 + 2i) = (3 \cdot 1 - 4 \cdot 2) + (3 \cdot 2 + 4 \cdot 1)i = -5 + 10i \] (3 + 4i)(1 + 2i) = (3*1 - 4*2) + (3*2 + 4*1) i = -5 + 10i

Now measure the three numbers. The modulus of a complex number is its distance from the origin — it is vec2's length, because a complex number and a point of the plane are the same thing:

  • |z| = √(3² + 4²) = √25 = 5
  • |w| = √(1² + 2²) = √5 ≈ 2.2360680
  • |zw| = √(25 + 100) = √125 = 5√5 ≈ 11.1803398

And 5 × 2.2360680 = 11.1803398. The moduli multiplied. Now the arguments — the angle each makes with the positive real axis, which is atan2(im, re):

  • arg z = atan2(4, 3) = 53.13010°
  • arg w = atan2(2, 1) = 63.43495°
  • arg zw = atan2(10, −5) = 116.56505°

And 53.13010 + 63.43495 = 116.56505. The arguments added. Two decimal coincidences in a row would be a coincidence; these are not.

\[ |zw| = |z|\,|w| \qquad\text{and}\qquad \arg(zw) = \arg z + \arg w \] |zw| = |z| |w|, and arg(zw) = arg z + arg w

That is the whole of complex multiplication, geometrically: scale by the modulus, turn by the argument. Which means multiplying by a complex number of modulus 1 is a pure rotation, and composing two rotations is a single multiplication — four multiplies, two adds, no trigonometry anywhere. Which is precisely what §1 asked for.

4.2 Why it is true, in four steps

The numbers above are evidence, not proof. Here is the proof, and it uses nothing but Lesson 2.5's idea that a linear map is determined by where the basis vectors land.

Step 1: multiplication by z is a linear map. Read the formula: each output component is a fixed linear combination of the input components. So z(v + w) = zv + zw and z(sv) = s(zv) for real s. Both are one line of algebra to check and the code makes them obvious.

Step 2: find where the basis vectors land. The basis of the plane is 1 and i. Multiplying 1 by z gives z. Multiplying i by z gives zi, which is z turned a quarter circle — because multiplying by i is what a quarter turn is, by §3's definition.

Step 3: look at what that pair of images is. The two images are z and z-turned-90°. They are perpendicular to each other, and they have the same length, namely |z|. A linear map that sends a perpendicular pair of unit vectors to a perpendicular pair of equal-length vectors is a similarity: it preserves every angle and multiplies every length by one fixed factor. That factor is |z|.

Step 4: find the angle. A similarity that preserves orientation is a rotation followed by a scale, and the rotation is determined by where any one vector goes. The vector 1 goes to z, so the rotation is by arg z. Done.

Figure 2 is steps 3 and 4 drawn. The triangle with vertices 0, 1, w and the triangle with vertices 0, z, zw are the same triangle — the second is the first scaled by |z| = 5 and turned by arg z = 53.130°. They are drawn at different scales on purpose, so that "same shape" is something your eye can check rather than something you take on trust; the factor of five is then stated between them.

4.4 It commutes, and that will matter enormously

Look at the multiplication rule once more: (ac − bd) + (ad + bc)i. Swap the two operands and you get (ca − db) + (cb + da)i — the same two products of the same four floats, in a different order. So zw = wz, exactly, bit for bit.

Geometrically that is the observation from §2: turn the paper 30° then 50°, or 50° then 30°, and it lands in the same place. Plane rotations commute, so the algebra that describes them may. It is worth noticing how easily this passes by unremarked — commutativity is so familiar from ordinary arithmetic that its presence here feels like nothing at all.

It is not nothing. It is the one structural fact that does not survive the move to three dimensions, and §12 will show that giving it up is not optional but forced. When you meet a quaternion library's warning that q₁q₂ ≠ q₂q₁, this is the property it is warning you that you have lost.

5. Complex numbers are 2×2 matrices

You may have been uneasy for two sections now. We invented a symbol, gave it a geometric meaning, derived a multiplication rule, and got a rotation out — but is any of this real, in the sense that the mat2 from Lesson 2.5 is real?

Yes, and here is the proof, which takes one paragraph. Lesson 2.5's whole idea is that a matrix is the answer to "where do the basis vectors land?", with the columns being those images. We worked that out in §4.2: multiplication by z = a + bi sends 1 to a + bi and sends i to zi = (a + bi)i = ai + bi² = −b + ai. Those are the two columns. Written on paper:

\[ M(a + bi) \;=\; \begin{pmatrix} a & -b \\ b & a \end{pmatrix} \] M(a + bi) = [[a, -b], [b, a]] in written (row) notation

Every complex number is such a matrix and every such matrix is a complex number. Set a = cos θ and b = sin θ and you have [[cos θ, −sin θ], [sin θ, cos θ]], which is mat2's rotation character for character — the same function this engine has had since Lesson 2.5, derived there by exactly the same argument about where (1,0) and (0,1) land.

So complex numbers are not a parallel invention. They are a two-parameter family of matrices the engine already had, stored without the redundancy.

5.1 The correspondence respects multiplication

Sharing a shape is not enough; two things are the same algebra only if the operations agree. They do:

\[ M(z)\,M(w) = M(zw) \] M(z) M(w) = M(zw)

You can verify that by hand in about a minute — multiply the two matrices out and compare the four entries against the product rule from §4 — and the harness verifies it over two thousand random pairs. This is what makes the correspondence an isomorphism rather than a coincidence of shape: anything you can say with one, you can say with the other. 

C. COMPLEX NUMBERS AND 2x2 MATRICES ARE THE SAME ALGEBRA
   mat2_from_complex(e^{i t}) vs rotation(t), 721 angles:
     worst element diff  0.000e+00
  [PASS] C.1  the two spellings of a plane rotation agree exactly

C.1 is exactly zero, and that is not luck. Both sides call the same std::cos and std::sin on the same argument and shuffle the results into different slots; there is no arithmetic between them to disagree about. C.2 is not exact, because a matrix product performs eight multiplies and four adds where the complex product performs four and two, and every one of those extra operations rounds.

5.2 So which two floats are redundant?

Look at [[cos θ, −sin θ], [sin θ, cos θ]] as four numbers in memory. In the engine's column-major storage (Conventions §6) they are c0.x = cos θ, c0.y = sin θ, c1.x = −sin θ, c1.y = cos θ. The third and fourth are the second and first again, one of them negated. A mat2 holding a rotation stores cos θ and sin θ twice. Figure 3 is that, drawn, with what the duplication costs.

Every row of that table except one favours the complex number, and §9 measures them. The row that does not is the honest one and it is worth stating before the measurements arrive: applying a rotation to a point costs exactly the same either way — four multiplies and two adds — because z · v and M · v are literally the same four multiplies. This representation is cheaper to keep and cheaper to combine. It is not cheaper to use. That trade reappears one dimension up with the same shape and different numbers, and it is why real engines still hand the GPU matrices.

5.3 What happens to a matrix that is not of that form

complex_from_mat2 goes the other way by reading the first column, which by §5's argument is the image of 1 and therefore is the number itself. But a general mat2 also shears and reflects, and neither is a complex multiplication. What does the function do when handed one? 

   a shear k=0.7 read as a complex:  +1.0000 +0.0000i  (0.00 deg)
   rebuilding it loses  0.7000  in the worst entry
  [PASS] C.3  a shear does not survive the round trip, and by 0.7
   CONTROL: a rotation round-trips to 0.000e+00
  [PASS] C.4  CONTROL: a rotation round-trips exactly
   sizeof(complex) = 8 bytes,  sizeof(mat2) = 16 bytes
  [PASS] C.5  a complex is half the size of a mat2

It reports the identity, confidently and silently, and throws the entire shear away. That is not a bug — it is the documented behaviour of a function that reads one column — but it is exactly the kind of thing that must be on record rather than discovered. The measured 0.7000 is the shear factor, arriving back as the size of what was lost, and the control beside it rules out the other explanation: a rotation round-trips to zero, so C.3 is reporting a matrix outside the family rather than a broken round trip.

6. Euler's formula, from a picture

There is one more piece of standard equipment to derive, and it is the one most often presented as a magic trick:

\[ e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta \] exp(i theta) = cos(theta) + i sin(theta)

Most treatments get there by writing down the power series for exp, substituting iθ, and sorting the terms into the series for cos and sin. That derivation is correct and it explains nothing: at the end you believe the identity and have no idea why an exponential would have anything to do with a circle.

Here is the other route, which is three lines and is a picture.

Line one. Take a point going round the unit circle at unit speed, and let z(θ) be where it is after turning through θ. Its velocity — z′(θ), the derivative — is tangent to the circle, and the tangent to a circle is perpendicular to the radius. It also has length 1, because the point moves at unit speed.

Line two. "Perpendicular to z, and the same length as z" is exactly what iz is — that is §3's definition of i, applied to z. So:

\[ z'(\theta) = i\,z(\theta), \qquad z(0) = 1 \] z'(theta) = i z(theta), with z(0) = 1

Line three. That is the differential equation whose solution everybody already knows: the function whose derivative is itself times a constant k is e^{kθ}, and the initial condition z(0) = 1 picks out that one. So z(θ) = e^{iθ}. And we already know what z(θ) is by looking at the picture — it is the point at angle θ on the unit circle, which is cos θ + i sin θ. The two descriptions are of the same function, so they are equal. Figure 4 is the whole argument.

The ten per cent this skips

The step "the only function whose derivative is itself times a constant is the exponential" is doing real work, and doing it for a function that takes complex values needs a definition of e^w for complex w and a uniqueness theorem. The honest summary is that both are available and neither changes the picture: define e^w by its power series (which converges for every complex w), and uniqueness follows from differentiating z(θ)e^{−iθ} and finding it constant.

This is the ninety per cent picture. The missing ten per cent is that definition and that one-line uniqueness argument, and Needham's Visual Complex Analysis chapter 1 does both properly and geometrically.

6.1 A numeric check, and a free theorem

Take θ = 60° = π/3. Then e^{iπ/3} = cos 60° + i sin 60° = 0.5 + 0.8660254i. Multiply v = 2 + i by it, using §4's rule with a = 0.5, b = 0.8660254, c = 2, d = 1:

\[ \begin{aligned} (0.5 + 0.8660254i)(2 + i) &= (1.0 - 0.8660254) + (0.5 + 1.7320508)i \\ &= 0.1339746 + 2.2320508i \end{aligned} \] (0.5 + 0.8660254i)(2 + i) = (1.0 - 0.8660254) + (0.5 + 1.7320508)i = 0.1339746 + 2.2320508i

And through the matrix, as Lesson 2.5 would do it: [[0.5, −0.8660254], [0.8660254, 0.5]] applied to (2, 1) gives (2·0.5 − 1·0.8660254, 2·0.8660254 + 1·0.5) = (0.1339746, 2.2320508). The same two numbers, because §5 said they would be.

And now the free theorem, which is the first sign that this notation is going to pay for itself. Since moduli multiply and arguments add, e^{iα}·e^{iβ} = e^{i(α+β)}. Write both sides out in cos and sin:

\[ (\cos\alpha + i\sin\alpha)(\cos\beta + i\sin\beta) = \cos(\alpha+\beta) + i\sin(\alpha+\beta) \] (cos a + i sin a)(cos b + i sin b) = cos(a+b) + i sin(a+b)

Expand the left with §4's rule and match real parts against real and imaginary against imaginary:

\[ \begin{aligned} \cos(\alpha+\beta) &= \cos\alpha\cos\beta - \sin\alpha\sin\beta \\ \sin(\alpha+\beta) &= \sin\alpha\cos\beta + \cos\alpha\sin\beta \end{aligned} \] cos(a+b) = cos a cos b - sin a sin b; sin(a+b) = sin a cos b + cos a sin b

Those are the angle-addition formulas, the ones that are traditionally memorised. They are not a separate fact to learn. They are the complex product, written out in components — the minus sign in the first is the same i² as ever, and the reason the second has a plus is that ad + bc has a plus. If you have ever mixed up which one gets the minus, you now have a way to derive it in eight seconds that cannot go wrong.

What we have, after six sections

A representation of plane rotations in two floats that composes by multiplication — four multiplies, two adds, no trigonometry — whose inverse is a sign flip, and which is provably the same object as the rotation matrix the engine already had, minus the redundancy.

Three of 7.2 §10's four requirements, met. The fourth was interpolation, and §10 gets it. But first there is a fact hiding in the reflections that is worth more than all of them, because it is the one that makes Lesson 7.4 comprehensible instead of magical.

7. Reflections, rotors, and where θ/2 comes from

Open any quaternion reference. Within two screens you will meet this:

\[ q = \cos\tfrac{\theta}{2} + \sin\tfrac{\theta}{2}\,\mathbf{n} \] q = cos(theta/2) + sin(theta/2) n

and almost always with no explanation of the halving beyond "because the sandwich product applies it twice". Which is true, and is the kind of answer that tells you that rather than why: it explains the factor of two by pointing at a formula whose form is itself unexplained.

Lesson 7.2 already put two half-angles on the page and deliberately declined to say why. Its §8.3 found that an axis error of φ costs 2 sin(θ/2)·φ of orientation error, and its §8.4 derived the threshold between two extraction routes from tan(θ/2) = √3. Two half-angle quantities, arrived at for unrelated reasons, in a lesson that never mentions quaternions. That was a debt, and this section collects it.

The halving has nothing to do with quaternions. It is a fact about rotations and reflections that is fully visible in the plane, and by the end of this section you will be able to check it with a protractor.

7.1 A mirror is a conjugate, wrapped in two rotations

Start with the easiest possible mirror: the real axis. Reflecting a point in the real axis flips the sign of its imaginary part and leaves the real part alone. That is conjugate:

\[ \overline{a + bi} = a - bi \] conj(a + bi) = a - bi

Now every other mirror is that one in disguise, and the disguise is a rotation. To reflect in the line through the origin along the unit direction m: turn the whole plane so that m lands on the real axis, reflect in the real axis, then turn it back. Three steps, left to right, and each one is a multiplication we already have: 

turn the mirror onto the real axis      conj(m) · v
reflect in the real axis                conj( conj(m) · v )  =  m · conj(v)
turn the mirror back                    m · ( m · conj(v) )  =  m² · conj(v)

The middle line uses conj(ab) = conj(a)·conj(b), which is one line to check from the multiplication rule, and conj(conj(m)) = m, which is obvious. So:

\[ \text{reflect in the line along } m: \qquad v \;\longmapsto\; m^2\,\bar{v} \] reflect v in the line along m: v -> m^2 conj(v)

Stop at the m². That is the whole lesson of this section arriving early, and it is easy to read past. To build a mirror at angle α you hand over m = e^{iα}, and the arithmetic squares it for you. The object you supply carries half the angle the operation uses. Nothing was halved on purpose; the formula came out that way because a reflection is a rotation performed on either side of a conjugation, and the two rotations are the same one.

A quick sanity check, because a formula with a square in it deserves one. Take m = 1 — the real axis itself, α = 0. Then m² v̄ = v̄, which is reflection in the real axis. Take m = i — the imaginary axis, α = 90°. Then m² = −1 and the map is v ↦ −v̄, which sends a + bi to −a + bi: the real part flips, the imaginary part does not. Reflection in the imaginary axis. Both correct.

The engine spells it exactly as derived, and the harness checks it against the route vec2 already had — reflect(v, n) from Lesson 1.7, which takes the mirror's normal rather than its direction, so the mirror has to be turned a quarter circle first: 

[[nodiscard]] constexpr vec2 reflect_in_line(vec2 v, complex mirror)
{
    return vec2_from_complex(mirror * mirror * conjugate(complex_from_vec2(v)));
}
E. TWO MIRRORS MAKE A TURN OF TWICE THE ANGLE BETWEEN THEM
   m^2 conj(v) vs reflect(v, perpendicular(m)), 4,000 cases:
     worst  1.475e-06

Two routes to one answer is a check; one route is a hope. These two share no code at all — one is complex multiplication, the other is v − 2(v·n)n — so their agreement to 1.5e−06 is evidence about the derivation rather than about a shared bug.

7.2 Two mirrors make a rotation of twice the angle between them

Now compose. Reflect in the mirror along m₀, then reflect the result in the mirror along m₁:

\[ v \;\longmapsto\; m_1^2\,\overline{\left(m_0^2\,\bar{v}\right)} \;=\; m_1^2\,\overline{m_0}^{\,2}\,v \;=\; \left(m_1 \overline{m_0}\right)^2 v \] v -> m1^2 conj(m0^2 conj(v)) = m1^2 conj(m0)^2 v = (m1 conj(m0))^2 v

The conjugate of a product is the product of the conjugates, and the conjugate of a conjugate is the original, so the two bars cancel and everything collects into a single square. Write R = m₁ · conj(m₀) and the composite is simply v ↦ R² v — a multiplication by a single complex number, which by §4 is a rotation.

Two reflections make a rotation. That is worth pausing on for its own sake: a reflection reverses the plane's orientation — it turns a clockwise triangle into an anticlockwise one — and doing it twice puts the orientation back. Two flips make a turn.

Now the angle. With m₀ = e^{iα} and m₁ = e^{iβ}:

\[ R = e^{i\beta}\,\overline{e^{i\alpha}} = e^{i(\beta - \alpha)}, \qquad R^2 = e^{2i(\beta-\alpha)} \] R = exp(i b) conj(exp(i a)) = exp(i(b - a)), so R^2 = exp(2i(b - a))

So if the two mirrors are φ apart, the composite is a rotation by 2φ, and the object built from the two mirrors — R — carries φ, which is half the angle of the rotation it generates.

That object has a name. It is a rotor, and it is what a quaternion is.

7.4 The control, and the first non-commuting thing in this course

E.4's 3.8e−06 would also be produced by a pair of bugs that made both sides the identity. So the harness asks a question whose answer distinguishes the claim from its degenerate case: what if the mirrors are used in the other order? 

   CONTROL: the other order gives -60.000 deg, not 60.000 deg
  [PASS] E.6  CONTROL: reflections do not commute; order reverses

Reflect in m₁ first and you get R = m₀ conj(m₁) = e^{i(α−β)}, which is the inverse rotor, so the composite is the rotation by −2φ. The two orders give opposite answers, and only coincide when the mirrors coincide.

This is worth more than a control. Complex multiplication commutes; composition of reflections does not. Both statements are about the same algebra, and they are compatible because a reflection is not a multiplication — it is a multiplication and a conjugation, and conjugation is what breaks the symmetry: m₁²·conj(m₀² v̄) is not m₀²·conj(m₁² v̄) because the bar falls on a different factor.

Remember this when §12 argues that the three-dimensional algebra cannot commute. The non-commutativity is not a defect introduced by adding dimensions. It is already here, in the plane, in the operation rotations are made of.

7.5 The double cover, before you have met a quaternion

Every quaternion library has a footnote saying that q and −q are the same rotation, and it is usually presented as a curiosity of the four-dimensional case. It is not. It is a consequence of §7.2, and it is visible here.

The rotation a rotor generates is R². And (−R)² = R² — the same four products of the same four floats, with two sign flips that cancel. So R and −R generate the same rotation, exactly, not approximately. 

   rotors 30.000 deg and -150.000 deg generate the SAME rotation
  [PASS] E.7  two rotors per rotation, and they agree exactly

And it has a geometric reading, which is the part that makes it stop being strange. −R is R with an extra half-turn, which corresponds to a different pair of mirrors — rotate both mirrors by 90° and they are still 30° apart, so they still generate a 60° rotation, but the rotor built from them is the negative of the first. The rotation remembers the angle between the mirrors; it does not remember which mirrors. Two rotors per rotation, exactly, and for a reason you can draw.

Why the plane lets you dodge this, and space does not

In two dimensions you can ignore rotors entirely. A rotation is z, you apply it as z·v, and the map from unit complex numbers to plane rotations is a perfect one-to-one correspondence with no double cover anywhere. Rotors only appear if you insist on building rotations out of reflections, which in the plane you need not.

In three dimensions there is no one-sided form to dodge into — §12 proves there is no multiplication on ℝ³ to write q·v with. Rotations must be built from reflections, so the half-angle is not optional, and the double cover comes with it. Every quaternion footnote you have ever read is this paragraph.

8. Implementation: the type

Everything above is now written down in one new header. We will build it in the order the lesson derived it, and the only decisions left are naming ones — which, in a file whose entire job is to make an identification believable, turn out to matter.

8.1 The type, and why it is called complex

struct complex
{
    float re{1.0f};   ///< the real part — also the x coordinate, also cos θ for a unit z
    float im{0.0f};   ///< the imaginary part — also the y coordinate, also sin θ

    [[nodiscard]] static constexpr complex identity() { return {}; }
    [[nodiscard]] static constexpr complex i() { return {0.0f, 1.0f}; }
};

rot2 would be a perfectly good name and the engine would read slightly better for it. It is the wrong name here, because the one thing this lesson has to establish is that the number and the rotation are the same object, and a rotation-flavoured name asks the reader to accept that identification on faith instead of watching it happen. The name also keeps the file honest: every operation in it is ordinary complex arithmetic, checkable against any algebra textbook, and nothing was bent to make it geometric.

It defaults to 1 + 0i — the number that multiplies by doing nothing, which is also the rotation by zero — for the same reason mat2 defaults to the identity: a default-constructed transformation that silently collapsed the plane would be a poor kind of surprise.

complex::i() exists because the single most useful thing to be able to type while reading this file is i(), and because it makes §3's check one line of a test rather than a paragraph of a comment.

8.2 The product, and the three readings of one function

[[nodiscard]] constexpr complex operator*(complex z, complex w)
{
    return {z.re * w.re - z.im * w.im,
            z.re * w.im + z.im * w.re};
}

[[nodiscard]] constexpr vec2 operator*(complex z, vec2 v)
{
    return vec2_from_complex(z * complex_from_vec2(v));
}

The second of those is the same arithmetic as the first, and that is not a shortcut — it is the lesson. A rotation composed with a rotation and a rotation applied to a point are not two operations that happen to resemble each other; in this representation they are one function called with the operands read two ways. Compare mat2, where m * v and m * n are genuinely different code with different costs.

vec2_from_complex and complex_from_vec2 are reinterpretations rather than conversions: no arithmetic happens and both compile to nothing. They exist so a call site can say which reading it means, which is worth two function calls that cost zero instructions.

8.3 Conjugate, modulus, inverse

[[nodiscard]] constexpr complex conjugate(complex z) { return {z.re, -z.im}; }

[[nodiscard]] constexpr float length_squared(complex z) { return z.re * z.re + z.im * z.im; }

[[nodiscard]] inline complex inverse(complex z)
{
    const float len_sq = length_squared(z);
    if (len_sq <= 0.0f) { return complex::identity(); }
    return conjugate(z) / len_sq;
}

length_squared is |z|², which is z · conj(z) with the imaginary part guaranteed zero. It is named to match vec2's because it is the same number — the squared distance from the origin to the point (re, im).

It is also multiplicative: |zw|² = |z|²|w|², which is §4's first claim. That one line is why unit complex numbers are closed under multiplication — compose two rotations and you get a rotation, with no renormalisation step and no drift beyond rounding. Contrast a mat3, where "is still a rotation" is a property of nine numbers that the multiplication does not preserve.

For a unit z the inverse is just the conjugate, because the division is by 1. Undoing a rotation costs one sign flip. Code that keeps its rotations normalised should call conjugate and say so, rather than pay for a length_squared and a divide it knows are 1.

8.4 The cheap renormalise, and the test that would have passed a broken one

Drift is small but it is not zero (§9 measures it), so a rotation that is stepped incrementally wants tidying. The expensive way is normalised: a length_squared, a sqrt and a divide. The cheap way is one line of algebra.

If |z|² = 1 + ε for small ε, the factor we want is 1/√(1+ε), and the first two terms of its expansion are 1 − ε/2. Substituting ε = |z|² − 1:

\[ 1 - \tfrac{\varepsilon}{2} \;=\; 1 - \tfrac{|z|^2 - 1}{2} \;=\; \tfrac{3 - |z|^2}{2} \] 1 - eps/2 = 1 - (|z|^2 - 1)/2 = (3 - |z|^2)/2 
[[nodiscard]] constexpr complex renormalised_fast(complex z)
{
    return z * (0.5f * (3.0f - length_squared(z)));
}

One subtract and three multiplies. No square root, no divide, and — being a truncated series — an error of order ε². Handed a drift of 1e−3 it leaves 1.5e−06; handed 1e−4 it leaves 6e−08, which is below a float's resolution at 1. The measured table is in §9.

The check this file shipped first, and why it was wrong

The obvious way to show the approximation falling apart far from unit length is to watch |z|. The harness did exactly that, and reported that at |z| = 2 the function works perfectly: the result has modulus 1.000000, better than at any other input in the table.

It does. At |z| = 2 the factor is (3 − 4)/2 = −0.5, which scales a length-2 number to length exactly 1 — and multiplies it by a negative number, which turns it 180°. The function returns a unit complex number representing completely the wrong rotation, and a test that looked only at the modulus certified it.

A rotation is not its modulus. The check now asks about the rotation, and the table in §9 prints both columns so that the disagreement between them is on the page rather than in a paragraph.

8.5 Angles in and out, and one deliberate difference from 7.2

[[nodiscard]] inline complex complex_from_angle(float radians)
{
    return {std::cos(radians), std::sin(radians)};
}

[[nodiscard]] inline float angle_from_complex(complex z)
{
    return std::atan2(z.im, z.re);
}

[[nodiscard]] inline float angle_between(complex a, complex b)
{
    return angle_from_complex(conjugate(a) * b);
}

complex_from_angle is the only trigonometry in the entire file, and noticing that is the point of the whole design. Trig appears exactly at the boundary where a human angle enters or leaves; everything in between — composing, inverting, interpolating, applying, reflecting — is arithmetic. Lesson 7.2 measured 82% of a rotation_slerp call going on trigonometry it could not avoid. Here there is none to avoid.

angle_between is the plane's twin of angle_between_rotations from math/rotation.hpp, and it is deliberately not an overload of it, because it differs in a way that matters: this one is signed.

That difference is real rather than an inconsistency. The plane has an orientation, so "30° clockwise" and "30° anticlockwise" are different rotations and one number can say which. Space does not: a rotation of space is a turn about an axis, and negating both the axis and the angle gives the same rotation, so the angle alone carries no sign to report. 7.2's function returns [0, π] for that reason and this one returns (−π, π].

8.6 The rest of the file

complex_pow_unit(z, t) is "do t of the turn z performs" — complex_from_angle(t · arg z) — and it is the function complex_slerp is built out of, so it is deferred to §10 where that argument happens. reflect_in_line, rotor_from_mirrors and apply_rotor are §7's three lines, written down. mat2_from_complex and complex_from_mat2 are §5's bridge.

That is the whole header: one struct, no state, no allocation, nothing virtual, and — because every function is inline in a header — nothing to link. §14's build line for the harness has no -l on it at all, which says what this representation costs a program more plainly than a paragraph could.

Written to be read as a diff against quat.hpp

Lesson 7.4 builds quaternions, and it builds them by analogy with this header, function for function: conjugate, length_squared, normalised, inverse, operator*, renormalised_fast, complex_slerp — each has a quaternion twin whose body is the same idea with one more imaginary unit and one crucial loss.

The names and shapes above were chosen with that mapping in mind, so that the harder file can be read as a diff against the easier one. If a name in this header looks slightly more formal than a two-float type needs, that is why.

The third row is the one people reach for first and it is the worst. "Just store the angle" is a tempting answer to §1: composing is then a single float addition, which is free. The trouble is that you cannot use an angle — every application needs a cos and a sin — so the fair unit of work is "compose and be ready to apply", and measured that way it is 6.45× the complex product. An angle is a good thing to type and a bad thing to compute with.

The second panel is the honest one. Applying a rotation to a point costs 1.05× — a wash, and the 5% is register traffic rather than arithmetic, since both routes are the same four multiplies and the same two adds. A representation can be strictly better at keeping and combining rotations and not one instruction better at using them. Say this out loud whenever someone claims quaternions are faster: they are faster to store, compose and interpolate, and Lesson 7.4 will measure that applying one to a vector is slower than a matrix, which is exactly why a renderer converts to matrices before it draws.

9.1 Drift, and asking both the same question

A million composed rotations is a plausible amount for a long-lived object in a simulation, and both representations arrive somewhere that is no longer quite a rotation. Comparing them needs care, because "how big is the modulus error" and "how non-orthonormal is the matrix" are not comparable numbers. So the harness converts the complex result to a mat2 and asks both the same question: max |MᵀM − I|.

2.42e−05 against 3.93e−06: the matrix has drifted 6.15× further. Which is what you would expect from a redundant representation — four numbers holding one degree of freedom have three directions to wander in, and the complex number has one.

Neither number is alarming on its own. What differs is what it costs to fix. A mat2 that has drifted needs Gram–Schmidt: normalise the first column, subtract its projection from the second, normalise that. A complex needs §8.4's four arithmetic operations. A timing discipline note: the warm-up

Lesson 7.2's rules — consume the whole result, vary the input with the repetition counter, best of three, and divide by the operation count to see if the answer is physically possible — are all obeyed here and all earned their place there. This lesson added one more.

Without a warm-up pass, the first measurement a freshly-built binary takes reads 15–30% slow: cold instruction cache, cold branch predictors, first-touch page faults on the input arrays. Across five runs, complex * complex reported 0.820 ns once and 0.610–0.628 ns four times, and the odd one out was always the first. Since time_ns takes the minimum of three attempts, one cold attempt out of three is harmless — but the first row of the first section is one cold attempt out of one. One untimed call before the timed ones, and every run now lands in the same band.

10. Interpolation: the path and the schedule

This is 7.2 §10's fourth requirement, and it is the one a rotation matrix cannot meet at all. Average two rotation matrices entrywise and you get something that is not a rotation. Average two unit complex numbers and you get something inside the disc, which is one divide away from being a rotation on the arc between them. That difference is the whole section.

Lesson 7.1 taught us to separate two questions that are easy to run together. Does a blend take the right path — the geodesic, the shortest arc? And does it travel that path on the right schedule — at constant speed? Three answers are possible, and the plane exhibits all three cleanly:

The middle row is the one people get wrong in both directions. Some treat nlerp as a cheap approximation that drifts off course; others as a free lunch. It is neither, and by the end of this section there is a closed form saying exactly what it costs.

10.1 Slerp, in three lines

[[nodiscard]] inline complex complex_slerp(complex a, complex b, float t)
{
    return a * complex_pow_unit(conjugate(a) * b, t);
}

Read it aloud: undo a, take t of the difference, redo a. conjugate(a) * b is the single rotation carrying a onto b — apply the inverse of a and then b. complex_pow_unit(·, t) is a fraction of it. Multiplying back onto a starts the journey where it should start.

\[ \mathrm{slerp}(a, b, t) \;=\; a\,(a^{-1}b)^{t} \] slerp(a, b, t) = a (a^-1 b)^t

That formula is not specific to the plane and not specific to complex numbers. It is the definition of a geodesic on any group where those three operations exist. Lesson 7.5's quaternion slerp is this function with quat substituted for complex; Lesson 7.2's rotation_slerp, written for mat3 in what looks like an entirely different notation, turns out on inspection to be the same three steps — undo, scale the difference, redo.

The angle covered by time t is t · angle_between(a, b) by construction, which is linear in t, which is what constant angular speed means. Measured over two thousand steps of an arc of 145°:

10.2 Nlerp travels the right path — and here is why it cannot do otherwise

[[nodiscard]] inline complex complex_nlerp(complex a, complex b, float t)
{
    return normalised(complex{a.re + (b.re - a.re) * t,
                              a.im + (b.im - a.im) * t});
}

Two lerps and a normalise. No atan2, no sin, no cos. And the first surprise is that it does not wander off course at all: 

     nlerp  path 145.0001 deg   speed 11.050107   excess +0.00%
  [PASS] F.3  nlerp wastes no turning (excess below 0.01%)
  [PASS] F.4  nlerp's speed is NOT constant

0.00% excess turning, the same number 7.2's geodesic reported. The reason is structural rather than lucky. The straight segment between two points of the circle stays inside the disc, and every point of it — except the origin, if the endpoints are antipodal — has an argument strictly between the two endpoints' arguments. Normalising changes the modulus and never the argument. So the normalised chord is the arc, point for point: nlerp's path is exactly the geodesic, and it could not be anything else.

A blend that cannot leave the shortest path sounds like it has nothing left to get wrong. It has one thing.

10.3 The schedule, derived

Put the endpoints symmetrically about the real axis, which costs nothing and makes the algebra transparent: a = e^{−iΩ/2}, b = e^{+iΩ/2}, arc Ω. Let u = 2t − 1, which runs from −1 to +1 as t runs from 0 to 1. The lerp is:

\[ p(t) = (1-t)a + tb = \cos\tfrac{\Omega}{2} \;+\; i\,u\,\sin\tfrac{\Omega}{2} \] p(t) = (1-t)a + t b = cos(Omega/2) + i u sin(Omega/2)

The real part does not depend on t at all — the chord is a vertical line, which is obvious once you see it and is the reason this choice of endpoints was worth making. The angle is therefore:

\[ \varphi(t) = \arctan\!\left(u \tan\tfrac{\Omega}{2}\right) \] phi(t) = arctan(u tan(Omega/2)), where u = 2t - 1

Differentiate, remembering du/dt = 2:

\[ \frac{d\varphi}{dt} = \frac{2\tan\frac{\Omega}{2}}{1 + u^2\tan^2\frac{\Omega}{2}} \] dphi/dt = 2 tan(Omega/2) / (1 + u^2 tan^2(Omega/2))

That is largest when u = 0 — the midpoint — and smallest at u = ±1 — the ends. Evaluating both:

  • midpoint: dφ/dt = 2 tan(Ω/2)
  • ends: dφ/dt = 2 tan(Ω/2)·cos²(Ω/2) = 2 sin(Ω/2)cos(Ω/2) = sin Ω

And the ratio between them collapses beautifully:

\[ \frac{\text{fastest}}{\text{slowest}} = \frac{2\tan\frac{\Omega}{2}}{\sin\Omega} = \frac{2\sin\frac{\Omega}{2}/\cos\frac{\Omega}{2}}{2\sin\frac{\Omega}{2}\cos\frac{\Omega}{2}} = \sec^2\tfrac{\Omega}{2} \] fastest / slowest = 2 tan(Omega/2) / sin(Omega) = sec^2(Omega/2)

Nlerp's speed varies by exactly sec²(Ω/2) over the blend. At an arc of 90° that is sec²(45°) = 2 — the middle of the turn happens at twice the rate of the ends. At 179° it is sec²(89.5°) ≈ 13,131.

And since dφ/dt is positive everywhere, φ is monotonic, so the total turning is exactly Ω — which is §10.2's zero excess, falling out of the same derivative that produced the speed ratio.

11. A circle without trigonometry

There is a classic optimisation that this representation makes available, and measuring it turns out to be more interesting than applying it.

To generate n points evenly spaced round a circle, the obvious way is a cos and a sin per point. The engine does exactly that in debug_lines::sphere (Lesson 5.11), which draws three great circles of segments points each. The alternative is to compute the step once and then multiply: 

const complex step = complex_from_angle(2.0f * k_pi / static_cast(n));
complex z = complex::identity();
for (int k = 0; k < n; ++k)
{
    use(z.re, z.im);
    z = z * step;                 // four multiplies, two adds — and no trigonometry
}

Two transcendentals per point become one complex multiply per point, with the trig paid once for the whole ring. The operation count says that should be five or six times faster. It is not. 

G. GENERATING A CIRCLE: TWO TRANSCENDENTALS, OR ONE MULTIPLY
   4096 points per circle, 400 circles, best of three:
     cos + sin per point       1.913 ns   independent
     one complex multiply      1.502 ns   (1.27x)   serial chain
     four chains interleaved   0.502 ns   (3.81x)   chain broken
     (D.1's INDEPENDENT complex product: 4-6x a cos+sin pair)
  [PASS] G.1  the recurrence is faster than two transcendentals
  [PASS] G.2  most of its advantage is eaten by the chain

1.21×. A famous optimisation, measured, buys twenty per cent — and the third row says why. z = z * step is a serial dependency chain: each multiply must wait for the previous one to produce its result, so the loop measures instruction latency and the machine's ability to run several multiplies at once is useless. The trig loop has no such chain — every point is computed independently from its own index — so it measures throughput, and a modern core will have four or five sines in flight simultaneously.

Run four circles at once, interleaving four independent chains, and the recurrence gets the same freedom: 0.526 ns, 3.52×. The optimisation is real. It was hiding behind a dependency chain, and no amount of counting multiplies would have found that.

Folklore has a shelf life

"Replace the trigonometry with a recurrence" is advice from an era when a sin cost a hundred cycles and was implemented in a loop. On the machine these numbers came from, a float sin and cos pair costs about 1.85 ns of throughput, which at this clock is a handful of cycles — because it is a short polynomial after a range reduction, and polynomials pipeline.

The advice is not wrong. It is worth 3.5× if you break the chain and 1.2× if you do not, and in 1995 it was worth thirty. Inherited performance advice is a hypothesis, and the harness is how you test it.

11.1 What it costs in accuracy, which is the real question

The recurrence multiplies its own rounding error by itself, n times over. The trig route does not: every point is computed from its own index, so errors do not accumulate. So the interesting measurement is not speed but how long the recurrence stays usable — walked against a double-precision reference at four lengths, with and without §8.4's cheap renormalise:

At the size the engine actually draws circles — 48 segments — the recurrence is wrong by 1.4e−05 degrees, which at any plausible screen scale is four orders of magnitude below one pixel. The plane demo therefore draws its circles this way, and says so in a comment. 12. Where the plane runs out, and what that predicts

Everything above works because the plane's points can be multiplied. Rotations, reflections, interpolation, the lot — all of it is the one multiplication rule, read differently. So the obvious plan for three dimensions is to find the three-dimensional version of complex numbers and repeat.

There isn't one. Not "nobody has found one" — there cannot be one, and the proof is four lines you can check.

12.1 The argument that ended Hamilton's thirteen years

Suppose a three-dimensional number system spanned by 1, i and j, with i² = j² = −1 by analogy with the plane, and with an associative product. Since the space is spanned by those three, the product ij must be one of its own elements:

\[ ij = a + bi + cj, \qquad a, b, c \in \mathbb{R} \] ij = a + b i + c j, with a, b, c real

Multiply both sides on the left by i. On the left-hand side, associativity gives i(ij) = (ii)j = −j. On the right-hand side, expand and use i² = −1 and ij = a + bi + cj again:

\[ i(a + bi + cj) = ai + bi^2 + c(ij) = (-b + ca) + (a + cb)i + c^2 j \] i(a + b i + c j) = a i + b i^2 + c(i j) = (-b + c a) + (a + c b) i + c^2 j

The two sides must agree component by component. Look only at the coefficient of j: the left-hand side has −1 and the right-hand side has c². So:

\[ c^2 = -1, \qquad c \in \mathbb{R} \] c^2 = -1, with c real

And there is no such c. The assumption fails: ij cannot lie in the span of 1, i and j. A fourth basis element is forced — not chosen for elegance, not adopted because it turned out convenient. Forced, by three lines of algebra, in a space that has no room for the answer.

That is the wall Hamilton spent from 1830 to 1843 walking into, trying every way he could think of to multiply triples. What he eventually realised — famously, on a bridge in Dublin, where he cut the relations into the stonework — was that you need four components and you have to give up commutativity, because the next thing the algebra forces is ij = −ji.

A symbolic argument cannot be "measured", so the harness does the honest alternative: it searches. If some real c did satisfy c² = −1, a fine enough sweep would show a residual heading towards zero. It does not: 

   searching c in [-20, 20] for c^2 = -1:
     best residual 1.0000 at c = 0.000
  [PASS] H.3  no real c has c^2 = -1: the 4th dimension is forced

The residual bottoms out at exactly 1, which is |c² + 1| at c = 0, and rises in both directions. Not evidence of a proof — a proof does not need evidence — but a demonstration that the obstruction is hard rather than a matter of looking harder.

12.2 The thing the plane could never have warned us about

Every quaternion text writes a rotation as a sandwich: v ↦ q v q⁻¹. It is natural to try that here, where we have everything the formula needs, and see what it does.

It does nothing. For any complex z and any v:

\[ z\,v\,\bar{z} = z\,\bar{z}\,v = |z|^2 v \] z v conj(z) = z conj(z) v = |z|^2 v

— because multiplication commutes, so the z and the z̄ slide together and annihilate into a real number. A unit z sandwiching anything returns it unchanged. 

H. WHERE THE PLANE RUNS OUT
   z v conj(z) vs v, 4,000 cases:  worst deviation 9.611e-07
  [PASS] H.1  the two-sided form is the identity in the plane
   CONTROL: z v z turns by 2 theta, to 4.098e-05 deg
  [PASS] H.2  CONTROL: the same factor twice rotates by 2 theta

That is not a curiosity — it is the reason. In the plane the working two-sided form is z v z, the un-conjugated one, which is §7's rotor applied twice and turns by 2θ. The conjugated sandwich is useless here precisely because the algebra commutes.

In three dimensions it is the other way round, and for the same reason read backwards. Multiplication will not commute, so q v q̄ no longer collapses; and the conjugate is needed — rather than q v q — because it is what keeps the result inside the three-dimensional subspace the vectors live in. The plane cannot show you that, because the plane has nothing outside the vectors to fall into.

Four predictions for Lesson 7.4, stated now so they can be checked then
  1. Four components. §12.1 forces the fourth basis element; a rotation of space has three degrees of freedom, so one constraint — unit length — will tie them together.
  2. The product will not commute, because rotations of space do not (§2), and because the algebra that describes them has to reflect that.
  3. A half-angle: cos(θ/2) and sin(θ/2)n, because a rotation is built from two reflections and the object built from two mirrors carries the angle between them, which is half the turn (§7.2).
  4. A double cover, q and −q being the same rotation, because (−q) generates the same rotation that q does for the same reason (−R)² = R² here (§7.5).

None of those is a quaternion fact. All four are visible in the plane, and Lesson 7.4's job is to carry them up a dimension and pay the one price — commutativity — that the trip costs.

15. Common pitfalls & debugging

Everything turns the wrong way on screen

Symptom. A positive angle produces a clockwise rotation in the window, and every arc in your debug drawing sweeps backwards.

Cause. Framebuffer y points down. The maths in this lesson is in a y-up plane, so drawing it without flipping shows you its mirror image. mat2's rotation carries this warning and has since Lesson 2.5; it applies identically here, because §5 proved they are the same object.

Fix. Flip once, in the view mapping, and never again — see view::py in the demo. Flipping at each call site is how you end up with two places that disagree.

A headless run opens a window and hangs

Symptom. plane --mode 4 --t 0.5 --shot out.ppm never terminates, writes no file, and — if you look — is running in the wrong mode.

Cause. SDL_clamp is a macro: (((x) < (a)) ? (a) : (((x) > (b)) ? (b) : (x))). It expands its first argument up to three times. Written as SDL_clamp(SDL_atof(argv[++i]), 0.0f, 1.0f) it advances i three times, swallowing --shot and its path — so shot_path_ stays null, the app opens a real window, and nothing ever quits it. This is not hypothetical; it is what the first build of this demo did.

Fix. Never put a side effect inside a C macro's argument. One named local per argument: const float wanted = ...; t_ = std::clamp(wanted, ...). Prefer std::clamp, which is a function and evaluates once — and note that the gimbal demo used it and never had this bug.

Your renormalise passes its test and breaks your game

Symptom. Objects occasionally snap to face backwards. The unit test on renormalised_fast is green and has been for weeks.

Cause. The test checks |z|. At |z| = 2 the factor (3 − 4)/2 is −0.5, which gives a result of modulus exactly 1 pointing 180° the wrong way (§9.1). The test is measuring the one property that survives the failure.

Fix. Check the rotation, with angle_between, not the modulus. And keep renormalised_fast for what it is for: inputs already close to unit length. If you cannot bound the drift, call normalised and pay for the sqrt.

A percentage comes out negative

Symptom. "Excess turning: −98.25%" — a path shorter than the shortest path.

Cause. Radians on one side of the subtraction and degrees on the other. angle_between returns radians; the span you are comparing against has probably been through deg() for printing.

Fix. Convert once, at the boundary, and assert the impossible cases. A quantity that cannot physically be negative deserves a check saying so — verify_73's F.8 is four characters of code and it is the check that would have caught this immediately rather than after the figure was drawn.

Reading a matrix into a complex number silently loses everything

Symptom. complex_from_mat2(m) returns the identity for a matrix that is visibly doing something.

Cause. It reads the first column, which is the image of 1 and therefore is the number — if the matrix is a rotation-and-scale. A shear or a reflection is not, and the second column, which is where all its information lives, is discarded (§5.3).

Fix. Only round-trip matrices you know are rotations. If you cannot know, check first: for a rotation-and-scale, m.c1 must equal perpendicular(m.c0). That is Exercise 7.3.2.

Your rotor turns things half as far as it should

Symptom. A construction built from mirrors rotates by φ instead of 2φ, and the error is a consistent factor of two in the angle rather than noise.

Cause. The rotor was applied once. A rotor is not a rotation; it is a square root of one (§7.2), and multiplying by it once does half the job.

Fix. Use apply_rotor, which exists precisely so that the name carries the warning. If you see this in your own quaternion code in two lessons' time, it is this bug with an extra dimension: q v instead of q v q̄.

--log info prints nothing from your demo

Symptom. The engine's own lines appear; yours do not, and the format string is obviously correct.

Cause. configure_logging applies * to the engine's five categories only — deliberately, as log.hpp says. A demo that defines its own category beyond log_category_count is outside that set, and SDL defaults an unknown category to ERROR. Your INFO lines are formatted and dropped.

Fix. One line in on_start: SDL_SetLogPriority(log_plane, SDL_LOG_PRIORITY_INFO). Worth knowing that demos/collector has the same gap and has never noticed, because its one INFO line reports a shutdown nobody reads.

16. Exercises

16.1 — The long way round guided

complex_slerp always takes the shorter arc, because angle_from_complex returns a value in (−π, π]. Sometimes that is exactly wrong: a turntable told to spin from 10° to 350° should go the long way, not snap back 20°.

Write complex_slerp_long(a, b, t) that takes the other arc. Verify that its path length is 360° − Ω, using angle_between as the instrument — the same instrument verify_73 §F.7 uses for the same measurement.

Hint, and then the answer

The difference rotation is conjugate(a) * b. You want its argument plus a full turn if it is negative, or minus a full turn if it is positive — the complementary arc, signed the other way. 

[[nodiscard]] inline complex complex_slerp_long(complex a, complex b, float t)
{
    const float d = angle_from_complex(conjugate(a) * b);
    const float two_pi = 2.0f * std::numbers::pi_v<float>;
    const float other = (d >= 0.0f) ? d - two_pi : d + two_pi;
    return a * complex_from_angle(other * t);
}

One trap worth meeting: at Ω exactly π the two arcs are the same length and the choice is genuinely arbitrary. Your function will pick one; say which in a comment, because the caller cannot.

16.2 — is_rotation, for the plane guided

§5.3 showed complex_from_mat2 silently eating a shear. Write the predicate that would have caught it: 

[[nodiscard]] bool is_plane_rotation(const mat2& m, float tolerance);

It should return true exactly when m is of the form [[a, −b], [b, a]] with a² + b² = 1. Test it against rotation(θ) over a sweep of θ (all true), against shear(0.7, 0) and scale(2, 2) (both false), and against a rotation that has been composed a million times — which should still be true at a sensible tolerance, and §9's measured drift of 2.42e−05 is how you choose one.

Hint

Two conditions, and they are independent. m.c1 == perpendicular(m.c0) says it is in the family at all; length_squared(m.c0) == 1 says the scale is 1. Test both, and notice that the first is the one complex_from_mat2 needs and the second is the one normalised would fix.

16.3 — Where does nlerp become visible? medium

§10.4 gives the worst gap between the two blends at seven arcs. Turn that into the number you would actually put in a design document: for a tolerance of ε degrees, what is the largest arc you may blend with nlerp?

Compute it for ε = 0.1°, 1° and 5°, by bisection on the arc. Then sanity-check against the table: your 1° answer should land between 30° and 60°, and your 5° answer between 90° and 120°.

Hint

The worst gap is monotonic in Ω, which is what makes bisection valid — say so in a comment, because a bisection on a non-monotonic function returns a confident wrong answer. Each evaluation is a sweep over t; twenty thousand steps is plenty, and the maximum sits near t = 0.24 and t = 0.76, so you can start there and refine.

16.4 — Spiral similarity, and a 2-D transform engine challenge

A complex number represents a rotation and a scale. Add a translation and you have every orientation-preserving similarity of the plane, which is exactly what a sprite or a 2-D UI element needs: 

struct transform2 { complex rotation_scale{}; vec2 translation{}; };

Implement composition (a then b), application to a point, and inversion. Then write down how many multiplies each costs and compare against doing the same job with a mat3 in homogeneous coordinates, which is what Lesson 2.7 would have reached for.

Solution sketch

Composition is {b.rotation_scale * a.rotation_scale, b.rotation_scale * a.translation + b.translation} — the same shape as a 3-D transform's compose, with a complex number where the mat3 goes. Inversion is {inverse(r), -(inverse(r) * t)}.

The count: composition is 4 + 4 + 2 = ten multiplies against a mat3 product's twenty-seven, or fifteen if you exploit the known bottom row. But the interesting part is not the count. It is noticing that transform2 and Lesson 5.7's transform have the same three fields and the same compose rule, one dimension apart — which is a fairly loud hint about what Lesson 7.4 is going to do to transform::rotation.

16.5 — Should debug_lines::sphere use the recurrence? engine challenge

engine/src/gfx/debug_lines.cpp draws its spheres with a cos and a sin per endpoint — three great circles of segments points each. §11 shows the recurrence would replace two transcendentals with one multiply, at an accuracy cost of 1.4e−05 degrees at 48 segments.

Make the change, measure it in situ with the profiler from Lesson 5.4, and then decide. Report the fraction of a frame that debug_lines::sphere occupies in a scene drawing a hundred spheres, before and after.

What the answer is likely to be, and why the exercise is still worth doing

Almost certainly no. §11 measured 1.21× on a serial chain, the function is called with segments = 48 so there are 144 points per sphere, and a hundred spheres is 14,400 points — about 5 µs of trigonometry against a 16,667 µs budget. Even a perfect speed-up saves 0.03% of a frame while making the code harder to read and introducing an accumulating error where there was none.

Do it anyway, because the habit is the deliverable. An optimisation that is real in a microbenchmark and irrelevant in the program is the most common kind there is, and the only way to tell the two apart is to measure in the program. Lesson 7.2 wrote, measured and reverted a hand-hoisted sin/cos for exactly this reason.

17. Recap & next

The engine gained one header, and the header is small enough to read in a sitting. What it bought:

  • A rotation that composes by multiplication — 4 multiplies, 2 adds, 1.88× cheaper than a mat2, no trigonometry, and closed under the operation so there is no renormalisation step in the hot path at all.
  • Half the storage, 6.15× less drift, and a renormalise that is four arithmetic operations with no square root.
  • An interpolation — the fourth of 7.2 §10's requirements, and the one a matrix cannot meet. complex_slerp is three lines, and they are the three lines Lesson 7.5's quaternion slerp will be.
  • An honest cost: applying a rotation to a point is a wash, 1.05×. This representation is cheaper to keep and to combine, and not one instruction cheaper to use.

And it bought four ideas that are worth more than the header, because they are the ones that survive the trip to four dimensions intact:

  • i² = −1 is a measurement, not a definition. Everything else — the product, the angle-addition formulas, Euler's formula — is a consequence of one quarter turn done twice.
  • Complex numbers are the rotation-and-scale matrices, with the duplicate cos θ and sin θ deleted. Nothing new was invented; a redundancy was removed.
  • Two mirrors φ apart make a rotation of 2φ, so the object rotations are built out of carries half the angle — and two such objects generate every rotation, exactly. That is cos(θ/2) and the double cover, both of them, three lessons before anyone says the word quaternion.
  • A blend can take the right path on the wrong schedule. Nlerp wastes 0.00% of its turning and varies its speed by sec²(Ω/2), derived and then measured at seven arcs — which turns "slerp or nlerp?" into a threshold on the arc.

Next: Lesson 7.4 — Quaternions, Derived. §12 proved that three dimensions cannot carry a number system of this kind: assume one, ask where ij lands, and the algebra demands a real number whose square is −1. A fourth component is forced. Lesson 7.4 works out what it has to be, discovers that commutativity is the price, and then checks the four predictions §12.2 left on the table — four components, no commuting, a half-angle, and a double cover. Every one of them is already on this page, in a space where you can draw it.

The engine still stores transform::rotation as a mat3. It will not for much longer.
