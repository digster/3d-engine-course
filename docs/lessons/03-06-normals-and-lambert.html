1The Problem

Here is the function that has coloured every solid surface in this course since Lesson 3.1.

Read what it takes as input: a colour, and the index of the triangle. Not a normal. Not a light. Not a position. The brightness of a surface in our engine currently depends on nothing except where it happened to sit in an index buffer.

It has been useful — it is the only reason adjacent faces of the cube were ever distinguishable — and it has one property that gives it away completely:

The failure mode — and it is a strange one, because nothing looks broken

Spin the object and the shading does not move. A face that was the third brightest step stays the third brightest step forever, because face % 5 does not change when the object turns. Real surfaces do the opposite: turn a book towards a window and the cover brightens.

That is the tell for fake lighting, and it is worth learning to see, because the symptom is not "wrong colours" — every individual frame looks plausible. The symptom is the absence of a relationship between the geometry and the light. Once you notice it you cannot unnotice it, and you will spot it in other people's screenshots.

There is a second, quieter cost. Look at the torus from Lesson 3.5 under that palette (Figure 3 shows the three pictures side by side later on): 2,304 triangles, each assigned one of five brightnesses by its index, produces a checkerboard of noise laid over a shape. You can see the silhouette and nothing else — no sense of which parts bulge toward you and which fall away. The palette is not merely fake, it is actively hiding the geometry that Lesson 3.5 worked so hard to load.

And there is an unspent asset. Lesson 3.5's loader read a vn for every vertex of every model and put them in mesh::normals, and the header said plainly at the time that nothing reads them yet. 1,150 normals in torus.obj, parsed, de-duplicated, index-matched, and unused. This lesson is where they do something.

What this lesson is not

It is not "how to make things look good". Lambert is the diffuse term only — it describes a perfectly matte surface, like chalk or unfinished paper, and it is deliberately the entire model today. No highlights (that is 3.7), no shadows cast by other objects (that is Module 6), no bounced light (Module 6 as well, and the constant we add in its place is labelled a fudge in the code). Building the simplest honest model first is what makes the next three lessons' additions legible rather than magical.

2Building Intuition

2.1 Light does not get dimmer, it gets spread

Everyone knows a surface facing a light is brighter than one at an angle. The question is why, and the usual answer — "less light hits it" — is wrong in a way that matters.

Take a torch and point it straight down at a table. It makes a bright circle. Now tilt the torch. The circle becomes an ellipse — larger than the circle was. Not one photon has been lost; the torch is emitting exactly what it emitted before. The same light is now spread over more table.

That is the whole of Lambert's law, and it contains no physics beyond arithmetic: brightness is power per unit area, and tilting the surface increases the area without changing the power. Figure 1 is that sentence as a picture.

Notice what the angle is measured from. It is the angle between the incoming light and the surface's normal — not the surface itself. That is not a convention someone picked; it falls out of the picture. Head-on means the beam is parallel to the normal and the footprint is smallest; graze the surface and the normal is nearly perpendicular to the beam and the footprint runs away to infinity.

And that is why a renderer needs normals at all. The normal is not decoration — it is the single quantity that determines how a surface meets light.

2.2 The terminator, and what lies beyond it

Follow the tilt further. At 90° the surface is edge-on to the light: the footprint is infinite, the brightness is zero, and the beam is skimming past. Tilt beyond that and the surface has turned away — it is now facing into its own shadow, and the light cannot reach it at all.

The line where that happens has a name borrowed from astronomy: the terminator, the boundary between the lit and unlit sides of a body. On the moon it is the edge of the crescent. Figure 2 walks a normal around a curved surface and shows what the cosine does as it crosses.

Two things to take from Figure 2 before any code. First, the falloff is smooth — that gradient across a curved surface is the entire reason a lit sphere reads as a sphere, and the debug palette has none of it. Second, the negative region is real arithmetic that has to be dealt with deliberately, because a dot product does not know it has stopped being physical.

3The Theory

3.1 Lambert's cosine law, derived

Figure 1 has already done the work; this is just writing it down. A beam of light with cross-sectional area A carries some power P. It strikes a surface whose normal makes an angle θ with the beam. The patch of surface it lands on has area A / cos θ — that is elementary trigonometry, the same adjacent-over-hypotenuse that gives a ramp its length. So the power arriving per unit area is

\[ E \;=\; \frac{P}{A/\cos\theta} \;=\; \frac{P}{A}\cos\theta \] E = P / (A / cos(theta)) = (P/A) * cos(theta)

In prose: the irradiance on a surface is the beam's own intensity times the cosine of the angle between the beam and the surface normal. That is Lambert's cosine law, published by Johann Heinrich Lambert in 1760, and it is the entire physical content of today's lesson.

Worked example — pushing three angles through by hand

Take a beam of unit width and unit power, so P/A = 1.

  • θ = 0° — footprint 1 / cos 0° = 1.000, so E = 1.000. Head-on: all of it, on the smallest possible patch.
  • θ = 30° — footprint 1 / 0.8660 = 1.1547, so E = 1/1.1547 = 0.8660. And cos 30° = 0.8660 ✓
  • θ = 60° — footprint 1 / 0.5 = 2.000, so E = 0.500. Exactly half, over exactly twice the area ✓

The harness computes the footprint geometrically and compares it against cos θ at ten angles rather than assuming the two agree — because "obviously the same thing" is how a factor of cos ends up in the wrong place.

3.2 …and why the dot product is already the answer

We need cos θ, and we have two directions. Lesson 1.7 introduced the dot product geometrically — as the length of one vector's shadow on another — and derived

\[ \mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b} = \|\mathbf{a}\|\,\|\mathbf{b}\|\cos\theta \] dot(a, b) = length(a) * length(b) * cos(theta)

If both vectors are unit length, the two magnitudes are 1 and the dot product is the cosine. Nothing new is needed: the quantity Lambert's law asks for is a quantity we have been able to compute since Module 1.

\[ E \;=\; \max\!\left(0,\; \mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{l}\right) \] E = max(0, dot(n, l)), with n and l both unit length

In prose: the diffuse term is the dot product of the unit surface normal with the unit vector pointing toward the light, floored at zero.

Toward the light, not the way the light is going

l is the vector from the surface to the light. A directional light is usually authored the other way round — as the direction its rays travel, because that is what a physical description of sunlight is. Midday sun travels downward, so its direction is (0, −1, 0) while the l in the formula is (0, +1, 0).

Get the sign wrong and the scene lights from precisely the opposite side. Everything is smoothly, plausibly shaded — nothing looks broken — and the light appears to be behind the camera when the sun is meant to be in front of it. This is the most common lighting bug there is. Our directional_light stores the travel direction and exposes to_light(), so the negation has a name and cannot be quietly skipped.

Both vectors must be normalised, and that is a real requirement rather than tidiness. If n has length 1.4, the dot product is 1.4 times the cosine and the surface is 40% too bright — a brightness bug with no visible cause, since the geometry is fine and the light is fine. §3.5 is about a matrix that changes a normal's length as a matter of course, so this is not hypothetical.

3.3 The clamp is not a detail

A dot product happily returns negative numbers, and Figure 2 shows exactly where: everywhere past the terminator. Physically those surfaces receive no light. Arithmetically they receive a negative amount, and if you let that through it gets multiplied by the light colour and subtracted from the result.

Worked example — what the missing clamp actually does

Take a surface pointing directly away from the light: dot(n, l) = −1. With an albedo of 0.8 in the red channel, a white light of intensity 1, and our ambient term of 0.06:

r = 0.8 × (1.0 × 1.0 × −1) + 0.06 = −0.740

Measured, in verify_36.cpp §B. A negative amount of light. It clips to black when written to an 8-bit pixel, so on the far side of an object you see… black, which is what you wanted anyway. The bug hides completely on the unlit side.

Where it shows is at the edges. Near the terminator the term passes through small negative values that the ambient term can no longer lift above zero, so a band of pixels that should be dimly ambient-lit goes to pure black instead — a hard dark rim eating into the lit region, with a visibly wrong, too-crisp boundary. Once you know the artifact you can name the cause on sight, which is the entire reason for showing it.

3.4 Albedo, and why every multiply is linear

Lambert gives how much light arrives. What leaves is that times the fraction the surface reflects, which is called its albedo — a per-channel number in [0, 1]. A surface with albedo 0.2 in red and 0.8 in green reflects a fifth of the red light and four fifths of the green, which is what "being green" physically is.

\[ L_{\text{out}} \;=\; \rho \cdot \big( I_{\text{light}}\max(0, \mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{l}) \;+\; I_{\text{ambient}} \big) \] out = albedo * (light_intensity * max(0, dot(n,l)) + ambient)

In prose: reflected light equals the albedo times the sum of the direct term and the ambient term. Albedo is a ratio, not a colour you can see — the same surface emits nothing in the dark and a great deal under a bright light, and keeping that straight is most of what makes lighting code behave.

The ambient term is a fudge, and the code says so

In a real room the side of an object facing away from the window is not black: light bounces off the walls and floor and arrives from everywhere. That is global illumination, it is expensive, and it is Module 6's subject. A constant added everywhere is the cheapest possible stand-in — it gets "not black" right and everything else wrong, since real bounced light varies with position and orientation.

It earns its place today for one specific reason: with pure Lambert and no ambient, the unlit half of an object is exactly the background colour, and the silhouette disappears into it. That would make this lesson's pictures worse at showing the thing the lesson is about.

Now the part that Lesson 1.6 spent a whole lesson preparing. Every multiplication above is a statement about light, so every one of them must happen in linear space. An sRGB-encoded byte is not proportional to light — it is a perceptually-spaced code — so multiplying it by 0.5 does not halve the light, it produces something roughly 73% as bright. Do lighting in encoded values and every gradient is subtly wrong, every falloff too slow, and the terminator sits in the wrong place.

So the shading function decodes once, does all its arithmetic in linear_rgb, and re-encodes once. That is exactly the discipline face_shade already followed for its fake ramp, which is why swapping it out changes the picture's content without changing its colour management.

3.5 The matrix that transforms normals

Here is where the lesson stops being about light and starts being about geometry — and where the obvious answer is wrong.

Our normals arrive in model space, straight out of the file. The light is in world space. Something has to carry the normal from one to the other, and the obvious candidate is the model matrix M, since that is what carries the positions. Lesson 2.7 even taught the rule that makes it look settled: positions are points (w = 1, so they translate), normals are directions (w = 0, so they do not). Use M and drop the translation. Done.

That is correct for a tangent — a vector lying along the surface — and it is wrong for a normal. Figure 3 shows why, with a squash.

So what is the right matrix? Rather than guess, go back to the only thing that defines a normal in the first place. A normal is not an arrow someone drew on the surface — it is defined by a relationship: it is perpendicular to every tangent. Transform the surface, and it is that relationship which has to survive.

Write it down. Tangents go to M·t — we know that much, because a tangent joins two nearby points on the surface and points are what M transforms correctly. Suppose normals go to X·n for some matrix X we are looking for. The requirement is that perpendicularity is preserved:

\[ (M\mathbf{t}) \cdot (X\mathbf{n}) = 0 \qquad\text{whenever}\qquad \mathbf{t}\cdot\mathbf{n} = 0 \] dot(M*t, X*n) = 0 whenever dot(t, n) = 0

Now use the fact that a dot product is a matrix product in disguise: a · b = aᵀb. The left-hand side becomes

\[ (M\mathbf{t})^{\mathsf{T}} (X\mathbf{n}) \;=\; \mathbf{t}^{\mathsf{T}} M^{\mathsf{T}} X \,\mathbf{n} \] transpose(M*t) * (X*n) = transpose(t) * transpose(M) * X * n

We need that to be zero exactly when tᵀn is zero — that is, we need the middle to do nothing at all. Setting Mᵀ X = I gives it, and solving for X:

\[ X \;=\; \left(M^{\mathsf{T}}\right)^{-1} \;=\; \left(M^{-1}\right)^{\mathsf{T}} \] X = inverse(transpose(M)) = transpose(inverse(M))

In prose: normals are transformed by the inverse transpose of the model matrix's linear part. The two orderings are the same matrix — inverting and transposing commute — so the name "inverse transpose" is unambiguous. That is the whole derivation, and it took three lines because we started from what a normal is rather than from what looked plausible.

Worked example — the slab's scale, by hand

Our demo's slab is scaled by (1.8, 0.35, 0.9). Take a surface at 45° in the yz plane, so its normal is n = (0, 0.7071, 0.7071) and a tangent along it is t = (0, 0.7071, −0.7071). Check they start perpendicular: 0.7071×0.7071 + 0.7071×(−0.7071) = 0 ✓

The tangent, transformed by M (correct — a tangent is a piece of the surface):

M·t = (0, 0.35×0.7071, 0.9×(−0.7071)) = (0, 0.2475, −0.6364)

The normal, transformed by M (the mistake):

M·n = (0, 0.2475, +0.6364)

Angle between them: cos φ = (0.2475×0.2475 − 0.6364×0.6364) / (0.6828 × 0.6828) = (0.06126 − 0.40500) / 0.46621 = −0.7373, so φ = 137.50°. Not 90°. The "normal" is now leaning into the surface by 47.5°.

The normal, transformed by the inverse transpose. For a diagonal matrix the inverse is the reciprocal of each entry and the transpose changes nothing, so X = diag(1/1.8, 1/0.35, 1/0.9) = diag(0.5556, 2.857, 1.111):

X·n = (0, 2.857×0.7071, 1.111×0.7071) = (0, 2.0203, 0.7857)

Angle to the tangent: (0.2475×2.0203 + (−0.6364)×0.7857) = 0.50003 − 0.50002 ≈ 0, so φ = 90.00° ✓ — and note it is nowhere near unit length any more, which is why the shading function normalises.

Swept over 20,000 random (normal, tangent) pairs under this scale: the inverse transpose never deviates from 90° by more than 0.00003°, while the naive transform reaches 67.96° off.

3.6 When it does not matter — which is why it ships

A fair question at this point: if this is so fundamental, how does anyone ever get away without it? The answer is that for most transforms the two matrices agree, and it is worth knowing exactly which.

A pure rotation. A rotation matrix is orthonormal, which means its inverse is its transpose. So X = (R⁻¹)ᵀ = (Rᵀ)ᵀ = R — the normal matrix is the model matrix, exactly. Measured: max |R − normal_matrix(R)| = 5.96e−08, which is float noise.

A uniform scale. For M = sR, the inverse transpose works out to (1/s)R. That is a different matrix — but it points every normal in exactly the same direction as R does, differing only by a positive scale factor that normalisation removes. Measured: 0.0000° of difference.

So the bug is invisible until something is squashed

Rotation and uniform scale cover an enormous fraction of everything in a typical scene. Our own demo is a fair sample: of its three objects, the icosahedron is uniformly scaled and shows a worst-case normal tilt of 0.03° (noise), while the slab (1.8, 0.35, 0.9) reaches 67.99° and the plinth (1.2, 0.25, 1.2) reaches 66.46°.

That is the shape of the bug: the hero object looks perfect and the set dressing is wrong. Nobody reports it, because the thing you are looking at is fine.

Rendered, the difference is not subtle. Squash the torus into a flattened ring and light it both ways, and 97.5% of the object's covered pixels differ, by up to 135 of 255 in a channel. The naive version does not look like noise — it looks like a correctly lit round tube, with a crisp bright rim and a hard dark band. It is lighting the shape the object had before the squash, which is precisely what failing to transform the normals means.

Try it. Squash the surface and watch the two normals separate. The tangent always does the right thing; the question is only ever what happens to the normal.

naive angle to tangent — correct — normals differ by —

The horizontal axis is fixed at the slab's z scale of 0.9 and the slider is its y, so dragging to 0.35 reproduces the worked example exactly: 137.5° and 90.0°. Drag instead to 0.90 — making the scale uniform — and the two normals coincide, the "differ by" readout falls to 0.0°, and the whole problem vanishes. That is §3.6's point in one gesture, and it is why this is a bug you can carry for years.

3.7 Where the normal comes from: per-face or per-vertex

One question remains before code: which normal does a given pixel use?

There are two sources available, and they are genuinely different data. A face normal is computed from the triangle itself — cross(b − a, c − a), which our winding convention makes point outward. It is exact for the triangle and knows nothing about its neighbours. A vertex normal is authored: it comes from the file, and for a curved surface it describes the direction of the underlying smooth surface at that point, not of any particular triangle.

This is not yet "flat vs Gouraud vs per-pixel"

Figure 4 is about where the normal comes from. There is a second, independent question — where the lighting equation is evaluated: once per face, once per vertex with the resulting colour interpolated, or once per pixel with the normal interpolated. Those are different axes, they interact, and comparing them properly is Lesson 3.8.

Today we evaluate per vertex and let Lesson 2.4's existing attribute interpolation carry the colour across the triangle — which costs no new rasterizer code at all, and gives both of Figure 4's pictures depending only on which normal we hand it.

And now a payoff from Lesson 3.5 that is worth pausing on. Load cube.obj, shade it with face normals, shade it with vertex normals, and compare the two renders: 0 pixels differ. They are bit-identical.

The reason is the index problem. A cube's 8 positions became 24 vertices precisely because the three faces meeting at each corner disagreed about the normal — so each of those 24 vertices carries its own face's normal, and the "per-vertex" normal at a corner is the face normal. The same experiment on torus.obj, whose positions carry genuinely smooth normals, differs on 5,576 pixels.

The sentence this module has been building toward

A faceted mesh is faceted because of the vertex split, not because of the shading model. Whether a surface looks smooth or angular was decided by the exporter, in the file, before your renderer ever saw it — and Lesson 3.5's de-duplicating map is where that decision becomes geometry.

4Implementation

One new header, two new functions in the maths library, and a change to how the demo assigns vertex colours. The rasterizer is not touched at all, and §4.3 explains why that is the interesting part rather than a coincidence.

4.1 A light, and a shading function

The comment shouts about the sign because that bug is worth one paragraph of noise forever. Everything else in the struct is a consequence of §3.4: the colour is linear_rgb because it is about to be multiplied, and intensity is separate from the colour so hue and brightness can be adjusted independently.

Three lines, and every one of them was derived. The max is §3.3's clamp; the "both must be unit" precondition is §3.2's; and the fact that this is the cosine is Lesson 1.7's dot product, unchanged.

The normalisation inside shade is defensive on purpose. After §3.5's inverse transpose a normal is emphatically not unit length — the worked example produced one of length 2.16 — and after interpolation across a triangle it will not be either, since the average of two unit vectors is shorter than either. A caller who forgets gets a brightness scaled by the normal's length: a lighting bug with no lighting cause.

C++ in place — designated initialisers and default member initialisers

Every member of directional_light and lighting has a default in the declaration, so engine::lighting lights; is immediately a valid, sensible light rather than a struct full of garbage. Combined with C++20's designated initialisers ({.intensity = 2.0f}) you get named, order-independent, partial construction with no constructor written by hand.

The rule to keep: a default member initialiser should be the value that is correct when you have not thought about it. Lesson 3.4 broke that rule once, deliberately, for fill_style::cull — because no cull mode is universally correct, so the default is the safe one instead. Knowing which of the two you are choosing is the whole point.

4.2 normal_matrix, and where it lives

§3.5's derivation, as two functions in mat4.hpp:

It goes in the maths library rather than in the renderer because it is a statement about matrices, not about lighting — normal mapping (Module 6) and physics (Module 7) will both want it. And it needs no new machinery: mat3 has had inverse and transpose since Lesson 2.5, where the general 3×3 inverse was built via cofactors and the adjugate.

Why the 3×3 inverse is enough, when mat4 has none

mat4.hpp says explicitly that it carries no general 4×4 inverse, on the grounds that nothing has needed one and the view matrix's inverse can be written down directly from its structure. That still holds here: a normal has no position, so the translation column is irrelevant, and linear_of drops it before we invert anything. The lesson that generalises: when you think you need a general inverse, check whether the problem actually lives in a subspace where a cheaper one exists. It usually does.

4.3 Shading per vertex, in world space

Here is the change in collect_triangles, and the first thing to notice is where it is not: the rasterizer is untouched.

Two decisions worth defending.

Once per vertex, not once per triangle corner. The icosahedron's 12 vertices are used by 20 triangles, so shading per vertex is 12 evaluations instead of 60 — the identical argument indexed geometry made in Lesson 2.12, now applied to lighting. This is the entire performance case for vertex lighting, and it is why an era of hardware did it this way.

In world space, not view space. A light's direction is authored in world space, so shading there means it does not have to be re-derived every time the camera moves. It also makes a testable claim available: orbit the camera and the shading must not change, because Lambert does not depend on where you are standing. Measured in verify_36.cpp §F — the brightest lit pixel is the same value from two different camera angles, while swinging the light changes 6,104 pixels.

The rasterizer did not change, and that is the point

fill_style gained no field. fill_triangle gained no branch. The lighting result is a vertex colour, and interpolating vertex colours across a triangle is something the rasterizer has done since Lesson 2.4.

That is not luck — it is the vertex/fragment split, arriving on its own. Lighting is a vertex-stage computation whose output feeds the fragment stage, and the reason it fits so neatly is that our pipeline already had that boundary without naming it. Module 4 makes it literal by giving each stage its own shader; the thing to notice today is that fill_style is starting to look like a place where lighting cannot go, which is what will eventually make a material system necessary rather than tidy.

4.4 Meshes with no normals

Three of our meshes carry normals; the rest do not. cube_mesh(), quad_mesh(), icosahedron_mesh() and Lesson 3.2's generated floor were all authored before normals existed, and quirks.obj has faces with no vn at all. mesh::normal_at returns the zero vector for all of them.

The fallback is the face normal, computed from the triangle's own edges:

Zero is a usable sentinel here for the reason normal_at documented back in Lesson 3.5: a zero vector has no direction, so it can never be mistaken for a real normal, and it survives a matrix multiply as zero. No second flag has to travel alongside the data.

Note that the face normal goes through the same normal matrix as everything else. A face normal is a normal; it obeys §3.5's rule exactly like an authored one, and transforming it with M instead would reintroduce the whole bug on precisely the meshes that have no authored normals to check against.

What we are not doing: generating smooth normals

The fallback gives a mesh with no normals flat shading, which is correct and honest but means such a mesh can never look smooth. Generating smooth normals means averaging the face normals around each vertex, weighted by area — which needs to know which faces touch which vertex, i.e. the adjacency that validate() already builds in Lesson 3.5. Reusing that rather than rebuilding it is Exercise 3.6.4.

4.5 The three modes, and two comparisons

The debug palette stays, behind G. That is the eighth time this engine has kept a wrong thing on a key — after draw_line_naive (2.1), Pong's unswept collision (1.8), the encoded blend space (2.4), the w toggles (2.7), the composition orders (2.8), affine interpolation (3.2), the near-plane modes (3.3) and the forward-axis facing test (3.4) — and the first time the wrong thing was ever the default. Flipping between the palette and Lambert while the object spins is the fastest way to see what "the shading does not respond to the geometry" means.

And the normal matrix gets the same treatment, with a number attached:

The HUD reports two things: the worst-case angle by which the naive transform would tilt a normal in this scene, and the pixel count between the two renders. The second is guarded on the first being non-zero — with no non-uniform scale present the two matrices agree to the last bit, so there is nothing to render twice. That guard is the lesson: it is a machine-checkable statement of exactly when the bug can bite.

What you should see

The scene now opens lit. Press L to jump to the torus, and the first thing to do is press G twice to walk the three modes in order:

  • debug palette (3.1) — a checkerboard of five brightnesses scattered over a shape. You can find the silhouette and nothing else.
  • LAMBERT, flat — the torus becomes an object. It has a lit side, a dark side and a terminator between them, and you can see which parts bulge toward the light. Every one of its 2,304 facets is individually visible, because each has one constant brightness.

That is not a bug — Lambert is view-independent, and 3.7's specular term will be the first thing in this engine that responds to where you are standing.

On the solids scene with per-vertex Lambert, the HUD's bottom line reads [G] LAMBERT, per-vertex [J] inv-transpose tilt 68.0 deg dif 0 px. Press J: the label turns red and reads NAIVE M, and dif jumps to a few thousand. Watch which objects change — the icosahedron is uniformly scaled and is pixel-identical either way; the slab and the plinth are not, and their shading visibly detaches from their shape.

That is the bug in miniature. The hero object looks perfect in both, which is exactly why nobody catches it.

7Common Pitfalls & Debugging

The scene is lit from the wrong side — everything looks fine, but backwards
Symptom
Shading is smooth and plausible, but the lit side of every object is the side facing away from where you put the light.
Cause
The sign of l. The cosine law needs the vector toward the light; a directional light is usually authored as the direction its rays travel. The two are negatives of each other.
Fix
Ask for it by name — light.to_light() — rather than remembering to negate. To confirm, set the light to travel straight down (0, −1, 0) and check that the tops of objects are bright.
A hard black rim eats into the lit side — too crisp to be a shadow
Symptom
Near the terminator, a band of pixels goes to pure black with a sharp edge, rather than fading into the ambient level.
Cause
The missing max(0, ·). Past the terminator the dot product is negative, so the direct term subtracts light and cancels the ambient term before clipping to black (§3.3: a measured −0.740 in red).
Fix
Clamp the dot product, not the final colour. Clamping at the end hides it — the rim becomes ambient-coloured instead of black — while the light is still being subtracted.
Squashed objects are lit as though they were not — the hero object looks perfect
Symptom
A flattened or stretched object has shading that belongs to its unsquashed shape: a flattened ring lit like a round tube, with a rim and a hard dark band that do not follow its actual silhouette. Everything uniformly scaled looks right.
Cause
Transforming normals with the model matrix instead of its inverse transpose (§3.5). Measured: 97.5% of the object's covered pixels differ, by up to 135/255 in a channel.
Fix
normal_matrix(model). To confirm you have the right one, take any tangent, transform it with M and the normal with your candidate, and check the dot product is still zero — that is the definition, and it is a two-line test.
Everything is too bright, or brightness varies with scale — no obvious cause
Symptom
Surfaces are uniformly over- or under-lit, and scaling an object changes how bright it is even though its orientation has not changed.
Cause
An un-normalised normal. dot(n, l) returns |n| cos θ, so a normal of length 2.16 (which the inverse transpose readily produces — see the worked example) makes the surface 116% too bright.
Fix
Normalise after transforming, not before. Normalising in model space and then applying the matrix does not help — the matrix is what changed the length.
Lighting looks washed out and the falloff is too slow — gradients are wrong, colours are not
Symptom
Lit surfaces are plausible at the extremes but the mid-tones are too bright, and the terminator is in the wrong place — too far around the object.
Cause
Multiplying sRGB-encoded values instead of linear ones (§3.4). Halving an encoded byte gives roughly 73% of the light, not 50%, so every falloff is compressed.
Fix
Decode once, do all arithmetic in linear_rgb, encode once. Test with a single light at intensity 1 on a white surface at 60°: it must read 0.5 in linear terms, which is about 188 as an sRGB byte — not 128. If you get 128, you are shading in the wrong space.
Loaded models are black — but the hand-typed ones are fine
Symptom
A mesh from a file renders as a silhouette in the ambient colour, with no shading at all, while cube_mesh() and friends light correctly.
Cause
The mesh has no normals — either the file had no vn statements, or the loader dropped them. normal_at returns the zero vector, which normalises to zero and gives dot(n, l) = 0 everywhere: exactly ambient.
Fix
Fall back to the face normal (§4.4). The diagnostic is in the loader's own report — normals is the count of vn statements read, and a zero there explains the picture completely.

8Exercises

3.6.1Predict the terminatorwarm-up

Without running anything: with the light travelling along (0, −1, 0), which parts of the torus are exactly at the terminator? Give the condition on the surface normal, then predict what fraction of the visible surface is lit when you look straight down at it. Check by pressing A/D until the light is overhead.

Hint

The terminator is where dot(n, l) = 0, i.e. where the normal is perpendicular to l. For a light straight down, that is every point whose normal is horizontal — on a torus, two whole circles.

Solution

The outer equator and the inner equator: the two circles where the tube's normal points horizontally outward and horizontally inward. Everything above them is lit, everything below is not, so from directly overhead you see exactly the lit half — but note the inner wall of the hole is also partly lit on its far side, which is a hint that a torus's self-shadowing is not something Lambert models at all. (Nothing in this engine casts shadows yet; that is Module 6.)

3.6.2Break the normal matrix on purposeguided

Give the slab a scale of (1, 0.05, 1) — nearly a sheet of paper — and compare J's two modes. Then work out, on paper, the angle by which the naive transform tilts a normal that started at 45°, and check it against the HUD's tilt readout.

Hint

For a diagonal scale the two transforms are diag(1, s, 1) and diag(1, 1/s, 1). Take n = (0, 0.7071, 0.7071) and compute the angle of each result from the z axis.

Solution sketch

With s = 0.05: M·n = (0, 0.0354, 0.7071), which is 2.9° from the z axis, while X·n = (0, 14.14, 0.7071), which is 87.1°. The two normals are 84.2° apart — nearly perpendicular. The naive version lights a near-flat sheet as though it were still a 45° wedge, which is the most extreme form of "lighting the shape it used to be".

3.6.3A second lightmedium

Add a second directional light — a dim, cool "fill" from roughly the opposite side — and sum its contribution. Then answer with a measurement: does adding the fill light ever make a pixel brighter than the sum of both lights' intensities? Should it be able to?

Hint

Lighting is additive: each light's contribution is computed independently and summed, because photons from different sources do not interact. That means shade() wants a loop, and the ambient term should be added once rather than once per light — a mistake that is easy to make and looks like "the fill light is too strong".

Solution sketch

No pixel can exceed the sum of the intensities, because each term is bounded by its own light's intensity (the cosine is at most 1) — which makes it a checkable invariant, and a good one to assert in a test the way verify_36.cpp §G does for one light. The design question worth sitting with is where the loop lives: putting it inside shade() means the function takes a span of lights, which is the shape Module 6 needs anyway once lights have positions and ranges.

3.6.4Generate the normals a file forgotmedium

Write generate_normals(mesh_data&, bool smooth). For smooth, each vertex's normal is the area-weighted average of the face normals of every triangle that uses it. Run it on a mesh loaded with no vn statements and confirm it now shades like torus.obj rather than flat.

Hint

Do not normalise the face normals before accumulating them. cross(b−a, c−a) has length equal to twice the triangle's area, so simply summing the raw cross products is the area weighting — big triangles get more say, which is what you want. And note you need to know which faces touch a vertex, which is the adjacency validate() already walks in Lesson 3.5.

Solution sketch

The subtlety is which vertices to average across. Averaging by vertex index gives nothing on a mesh whose seams are already split — every vertex belongs to one smoothing region already. Averaging by welded position smooths across the seam, which is right for a uv seam and wrong for a genuine crease: it would round off the edges of a cube. Real exporters solve this with smoothing groups (OBJ's s statement, which we skip) or an angle threshold. Picking one and saying why is the exercise.

3.6.5Where does the energy go?open

A Lambertian surface with albedo 1 reflects all the light that reaches it. Our shade() returns albedo × intensity × cos θ — but a real diffuse surface scatters that light over a whole hemisphere. Find out what the correct constant is, why it is 1/π, and what would have to change elsewhere for the picture to look the same after you insert it.

Solution sketch

Integrating a constant radiance over the hemisphere with the cosine weighting gives π, so a physically normalised Lambertian BRDF is albedo/π — and every image in this lesson would go dark by that factor. Real renderers absorb the π into the light's units instead, which is why "intensity" in a game engine is rarely in watts. This is the first crack in the model that Module 6's radiometry lesson opens properly, and the honest summary for today is: our numbers are self-consistent rather than physical, and the difference is one constant and a change of units.

9Recap & Next

What the engine can do now that it could not before

  • Light. A directional light and Lambert's cosine law, derived from a spreading beam rather than quoted — with the clamp, the linear-space discipline, and the toward-the-light sign convention all made explicit rather than inherited.
  • Correct normals under any transform. normal_matrix, derived from the one property that defines a normal, together with a measured account of exactly when it matters (non-uniform scale: up to 68° of tilt and 97.5% of pixels) and when it provably does not (rotation, uniform scale).
  • Two normal sources, one evaluation point. Per-face and per-vertex normals both shade correctly, with the face normal as the fallback for geometry that carries none — and the measurement that cube.obj cannot tell them apart, because Lesson 3.5's split already decided the answer.
  • A rasterizer that did not have to change. Lighting produced vertex colours, and interpolating vertex colours is Lesson 2.4's job. The vertex/fragment boundary showed up on its own.

Next: 3.7 — Specular and Blinn-Phong. Everything today is view-independent: orbit the camera and nothing changes, because a matte surface scatters light equally in all directions. Real surfaces are not matte. A highlight is light that bounced off in a preferred direction — which means the shading finally has to know where you are standing, and the model has to grow a second term. We will also be honest about what Blinn-Phong gets wrong, because that debt is what Module 6's microfacet theory is paid to settle.

  • Scratchapixel Introduction to Shading — the same cosine law, derived the same way, with a more careful treatment of solid angle than we needed today.
  • Real-Time Rendering Chapter 5, Shading Basics — and §9.9 for where Lambert sits inside the general BRDF framework that Module 6 builds.
  • The classic Eric Lengyel, The Mathematics of 3D Game Programming, §4.5 — the inverse-transpose derivation done in the same preserve-the-relationship style, generalised to any linear transform.
  • Primary source J. H. Lambert, Photometria (1760). Worth knowing that the law predates every piece of technology in this course by two centuries, and was derived by measuring candles against each other by eye.
  • Ahead Naty Hoffman's Physics and Math of Shading course notes (SIGGRAPH) — the clearest short account of why albedo/π is the physically normalised diffuse term, which Exercise 3.6.5 sets up and Module 6 pays off.
