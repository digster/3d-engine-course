Quaternions, Derived

Lesson 7.3 ended with a proof, not a promise: no three-dimensional number system can do this job. Assume one spanned by 1, i and j, ask where ij has to land, and three lines of algebra demand a real number whose square is −1. So there is a fourth component and it is forced. This lesson starts exactly there and asks the question that proof leaves open: forced to be what? The answer comes from a single geometric demand — space has no preferred direction, so every unit imaginary must be a half-turn and square to −1 — and that one sentence forces anticommutativity in three lines, forces every entry of the multiplication table, and hands back ijk = −1 as a consequence rather than as Hamilton's flourish. Multiplying two of them out then produces something startling: the dot product and the cross product are both inside the quaternion product, and the cross product is precisely the term that changes sign when you swap the operands. Three sentences turn out to be one fact — quaternions do not commute, the cross product is antisymmetric, rotations of space do not commute — and that is the whole of what the fourth dimension charges. The half-angle is not postulated either: 7.3 derived it in the plane with two mirrors and a protractor, and here the same argument with planes instead of lines produces cos(θ/2) + sin(θ/2) n̂ and the sandwich q v q̄ and the double cover, none of them chosen, all three falling out of composing two reflections. Then we measure everything: 1.57× cheaper to compose, 1.57× dearer to apply — so there is a crossover, and it is 7.6 vectors — and an extraction with no bad case anywhere, because four candidates that sum to 4 always leave one of them at least 1, where Lesson 7.2's three needed a derived crossover at 120°. The surprises are honest ones. An unrepaired quaternion walk drifts worse than a matrix walk, and the reason is better than the result. 7.3's sharpest warning — renormalised_fast lying with a perfect modulus — inverts: here it is exact at |q| = 1 and exact again at |q| = 2, and catastrophic between, so a test sampling the two obvious points certifies a broken function. And the engine change this header has promised since Module 2 does not happen in this lesson, because the narrower type found something first: the field called rotation has a caller that does not put a rotation in it.

1. The problem: forced to be what?

Lesson 7.3 spent most of its length in two dimensions, and it ended by proving that two dimensions were not going to be enough. The proof is short and worth having in your head before we go on, because everything below starts where it stops.

Lesson 7.3 §12.1, in four lines

Suppose there were a three-dimensional number system spanned by 1, i and j, with i² = j² = −1 and an associative product. Then ij is a number in that system, so write it as a + bi + cj. Multiply on the left by i. Associativity gives i(ij) = (ii)j = −j on one side; expanding i(a + bi + cj) and collecting gives c² as the coefficient of j on the other. Matching them demands a real c with c² = −1. There is none.

So ij cannot live in the span of 1, i and j. It is a new basis element, call it k, and the system has four components. That is settled, it was settled in the previous lesson, and this lesson does not repeat it.

What it leaves open is the interesting part. Knowing that a fourth component is forced tells you nothing about what the arithmetic has to be. We know i² = j² = −1. We have named ij = k. We do not yet know ji, or k², or ik, or any of the other nine entries — and if you have met quaternions before, you have almost certainly met them as a table someone wrote down and asked you to accept.

We are not going to do that. There is a single geometric demand that fixes every entry, and it is a sentence about space rather than a sentence about algebra. Finding it is §3.

Lesson 7.3 also left four explicit predictions on the page, in a callout, phrased so they could be checked rather than re-motivated. Here they are again, because the rest of this lesson is largely the business of discharging them:

The four predictions, from Lesson 7.3 §12.2
  1. Four components. Proved above.
  2. The product will not commute. §4 shows it, and identifies the single term responsible.
  3. cos(θ/2) + sin(θ/2) n̂. §5 derives it from two mirrors, the same argument 7.3 ran in the plane with a protractor.
  4. A double cover — two objects per rotation, exactly. §5 produces it inside the derivation, as a sign nobody chose, before §8 names it.

2. Building intuition: a rotation is two reflections

Before any algebra, here is the geometric fact the whole lesson rests on, and you can check it at a table with two hands.

Hold your left hand flat and vertical — that is a mirror. Hold your right hand flat and vertical too, at some angle to the first, and note that the two planes meet along a vertical line. Now take an object and reflect it in the left plane, then reflect the result in the right plane. The object ends up rotated about the line where the two planes meet, and — this is the part that matters — it is rotated by twice the angle between the planes, not by the angle between them.

Lesson 7.3 §7 established exactly this in two dimensions, where a mirror is a line rather than a plane and you can draw the whole thing on graph paper. It measured it: mirrors 20° and 50° apart produce a rotation of 60°, over 4,000 random mirror pairs at an agreement of 3.8×10⁻⁶. It also drew the consequence that mattered:

The sentence 7.3 wrote three lessons early

Two mirrors φ apart generate a rotation of 2φ. So the object a rotation is built out of carries half the angle of the rotation it generates. That is the θ/2 in every quaternion, and it is a fact about reflections rather than a fact about quaternions.

Everything in §5 is that sentence carried up one dimension. Nothing is halved on purpose; nothing is a normalisation convention chosen to make a formula tidy. A rotation is two reflections, a reflection has an angle, and the arithmetic doubles it.

Keep one more thing from 7.3 in view, because it removes the other mystery before we meet it. That lesson asked why every quaternion text writes a rotation as the two-sided q v q̄ rather than the one-sided z · v the plane uses, and answered it from the other end: in the plane, z v z̄ collapses to the useless |z|² v — measured at 9.6×10⁻⁷ over 4,000 cases — precisely because complex multiplication commutes. The two-sided form is the normal case. The plane was the special one, and it was special for exactly the reason that is about to disappear.

3. The table, forced by isotropy

We have 1, i, j, k, and we know i² = j² = −1 and ij = k. Sixteen products, four known, twelve open.

3.1 The one demand

Here is the sentence that closes all twelve, and it is not an algebraic axiom:

Isotropy

Space has no preferred direction. There is nothing special about the x axis, so if i — the imaginary unit along x — squares to −1, then every unit imaginary must square to −1, whichever way it points.

Notice what kind of statement that is. It is not "let us also require…", bolted on to make the algebra close. It is a description of the thing we are trying to model. A number system for the rotations of space that treated one direction differently from another would be describing a different space from the one we live in. If the demand failed, the algebra would be wrong, not merely inconvenient.

And it is checkable. §A.1 of this lesson's harness draws 20,000 unit vectors uniformly on the sphere — by rejection rather than by a spherical-coordinate pair, because that pair clusters at the poles and the whole question here is whether some direction is special — turns each into a pure quaternion, and squares it: 

A. THE TABLE, FORCED
   worst |u^2 + 1| over 20000 axes:  2.980e-07
  [PASS] A.1  every unit imaginary is a half-turn

3.2 What it forces, in three lines

Take u = (i + j)/√2. Its length is 1, so by the demand u² = −1. Therefore

\[ (i + j)^2 \;=\; 2u^2 \;=\; -2. \]

Now expand the left-hand side the ordinary way, treating i and j as symbols and being careful not to assume they commute:

\[ (i+j)^2 \;=\; i^2 + ij + ji + j^2 \;=\; -2 + (ij + ji). \]

Match the two lines and the bracket has to vanish:

\[ ij + ji = 0, \qquad\text{that is,}\qquad ji = -ij = -k. \]

In prose, in case the typesetting is unavailable: (i+j)² must equal −2 because (i+j)/√2 is a unit imaginary; expanding it gives −2 plus ij + ji; so ij + ji = 0 and ji = −ij.

This is the load-bearing step of the lesson

Anticommutativity was not assumed. It is what isotropy costs. Most presentations introduce ji = −ij as part of the definition, which makes the failure to commute look like a design choice somebody made and you have to live with. It is the opposite: it is forced by the demand that space be the same in every direction, and any commuting alternative would be describing a space that is not ours.

3.3 And then the rest of the table falls out

With ji = −ij and associativity, every remaining entry is one line. Watch the k² one especially, because it is the one that looks like it ought to need its own assumption:

\[ \begin{aligned} k^2 &= (ij)(ij) = i(ji)j = i(-ij)j = -(ii)(jj) = -(-1)(-1) = -1 \\ jk &= j(ij) = (ji)j = (-ij)j = -i(jj) = i \\ ki &= (ij)i = -(ji)i = -j(ii) = j \\ ik &= i(ij) = (ii)j = -j \\ kj &= (ij)j = i(jj) = -i \end{aligned} \]

Read the middle three and a pattern appears that is worth naming: ij = k, jk = i, ki = j — the three cyclic products go forwards, and the three anticyclic ones carry a minus. If that reminds you of the cross product, hold the thought for one section; it is not a resemblance.

3.4 Hamilton's formula, as a consequence

Multiply the first two together:

\[ ijk = (ij)k = k\,k = k^2 = -1. \]

That is the formula William Rowan Hamilton is said to have cut into the stone of Broom Bridge in Dublin in October 1843, after thirteen years of trying. It is quoted in nearly every treatment as the defining relation of the quaternions — the thing you are handed. Here it arrived as the last line of a derivation whose only input was that space has no preferred direction.

3.5 The control

Everything above would be equally true of a commuting algebra if the derivation had gone wrong somewhere — A.1 and A.4 would both pass. So §A.5 checks the thing the whole construction exists for: 

   i*j = (0.0, 0.0, 0.0, 1.0)
   j*i = (0.0, 0.0, 0.0, -1.0)
  [PASS] A.2  ij = k and ji = -k
  [PASS] A.3  jk = i, ki = j, ik = -j, kj = -i
   i*j*k = (-1.0, 0.0, 0.0, 0.0)
  [PASS] A.4  i^2 = j^2 = k^2 = ijk = -1
  [PASS] A.5  CONTROL: the product does not commute
   worst |(ab)c - a(bc)| over 20000:  1.788e-07
  [PASS] A.6  the product is associative

A.6 is there because the derivation of k², jk and ki leans on associativity at every step and nothing above actually verified that the implementation has it. Associativity is easy to lose by a sign slip and impossible to notice from a single product.

4. The product, and the two famous halves inside it

We now have the whole table, so we can multiply any two quaternions. Doing it by expanding sixteen products of components is correct and unreadable. There is a much better way to write it, and the better way is also where the lesson's next surprise lives.

4.1 Split it into a scalar and a vector

Write a quaternion as a real part and a vector part: q = w + v, where w is a number and v = xi + yj + zk is a vector of space wearing a coat. Then multiplying two of them is ordinary distribution:

\[ (w_1 + v_1)(w_2 + v_2) \;=\; w_1w_2 \;+\; w_1v_2 \;+\; v_1w_2 \;+\; v_1v_2. \]

The first three terms need no rules at all — a real times a real, and a real scaling a vector. Everything interesting is in the last one, v₁v₂, the product of two pure quaternions. Expand it against the table from §3, with v₁ = (a₁, b₁, c₁) and v₂ = (a₂, b₂, c₂):

\[ \begin{aligned} v_1v_2 = (a_1 i + b_1 j + c_1 k)(a_2 i + b_2 j + c_2 k) \end{aligned} \]

There are nine terms. Three of them are squares — a₁a₂ i², b₁b₂ j², c₁c₂ k² — and each contributes its coefficient with a minus sign, because all three units square to −1. Collect those three and you have

\[ -(a_1a_2 + b_1b_2 + c_1c_2) \;=\; -(v_1 \cdot v_2). \]

The other six are the mixed products, and they pair up. The k terms are a₁b₂\,ij + b₁a₂\,ji = (a₁b₂ − b₁a₂)k, using ji = −ij from §3.2 and nothing else. The i and j terms do the same thing with the indices rotated. So the six mixed terms collect into

\[ (b_1c_2 - c_1b_2)\,i + (c_1a_2 - a_1c_2)\,j + (a_1b_2 - b_1a_2)\,k \;=\; v_1 \times v_2. \]

Putting the two halves together:

\[ v_1 v_2 \;=\; -(v_1 \cdot v_2) \;+\; (v_1 \times v_2) \]
Where the dot and cross products came from

That line is not an analogy and it is not a repackaging. The dot product and the cross product are the two halves of the quaternion product, and they were extracted from exactly this expression by Josiah Willard Gibbs and Oliver Heaviside in the 1880s, who argued — persuasively, and they won — that most physics needs one half or the other and rarely both at once. Every vector-algebra course since has taught the two pieces without ever mentioning the thing they were cut out of. You have been using half a quaternion product since Lesson 1.7.

Substituting back, the whole product is:

\[ q\,p \;=\; \big(\, w_1w_2 - v_1\!\cdot\! v_2,\;\; w_1v_2 + w_2v_1 + v_1 \times v_2 \,\big) \]

That formula is the storage decision. It is why quat below is {float w; vec3 v;} rather than four loose floats: written that way, operator* is two lines that read as the formula, and written the other way it is sixteen indexed products that nobody can check. The bytes in memory are identical either way; the only thing the choice changes is whether the code can be read.

4.2 The one price, located exactly

Now swap the operands and go through the formula term by term. w₁w₂ is unchanged. v₁·v₂ is unchanged, because the dot product is symmetric. w₁v₂ + w₂v₁ is unchanged, because addition is. And v₁ × v₂ flips sign, because the cross product is antisymmetric. So:

\[ q\,p \;-\; p\,q \;=\; \big(\,0,\;\; 2\,(v_1 \times v_2)\,\big) \]

In prose: the difference between the two orders is purely a vector, and it is exactly twice the cross product of the two vector parts.

Three sentences, one fact

Quaternions do not commute. The cross product is antisymmetric. Rotations of space do not commute. These are not three related observations. They are one observation said three ways, and the equation above is the identification. The price the fourth dimension charges is a single term, and you have known that term's sign convention since Lesson 2.4.

4.3 A worked example, by hand

Take q = 2 + i − j + 3k and p = −1 + 2i + k. In the split notation, w₁ = 2, v₁ = (1, −1, 3), w₂ = −1, v₂ = (2, 0, 1).

The scalar part is w₁w₂ − v₁·v₂. The dot product is 1·2 + (−1)·0 + 3·1 = 5, so the scalar part is 2·(−1) − 5 = −7.

The vector part is w₁v₂ + w₂v₁ + v₁ × v₂, three vectors added:

  • w₁v₂ = 2 · (2, 0, 1) = (4, 0, 2)
  • w₂v₁ = −1 · (1, −1, 3) = (−1, 1, −3)
  • v₁ × v₂ = ((−1)(1) − (3)(0), (3)(2) − (1)(1), (1)(0) − (−1)(2)) = (−1, 5, 2)

Adding those three componentwise: (4 − 1 − 1, 0 + 1 + 5, 2 − 3 + 2) = (2, 6, 1). So

\[ q\,p \;=\; -7 + 2i + 6j + k. \]

Run the same arithmetic the other way round — only the cross product changes, to (1, −5, −2) — and you get p q = −7 + 4i − 4j − 3k. The scalar parts agree, as §4.2 said they must, and the difference is (0, −2, 10, 4) = 2 · (−1, 5, 2), which is twice the cross product exactly. §B.1's 0.000e+00 is worth a sentence of its own. It is not a tolerance being met; it is exact. The two expressions are the same sequence of floating-point operations in a different order of writing, and there is nothing for rounding to do.

4.4 The control

§B.3's identity was measured on one example, and one example is a coincidence waiting to be discovered. §B.5 is the control that turns it into a claim: if the cross term really is the only thing that changes under a swap, then a pair of quaternions whose vector parts are parallel must commute exactly, since their cross product is zero. It does: 

   worst relative |qp|-|q||p|:        2.550e-07
  [PASS] B.4  the norm is multiplicative
  [PASS] B.5  CONTROL: parallel vector parts commute

B.4 is quietly one of the most important lines in the file. |qp| = |q||p| means the unit quaternions are closed under multiplication: compose two rotations and you get a rotation, with no renormalisation step in between and no drift beyond rounding. It is also the same demand as §3.1 in different clothes, which is why they force the same table.

5. Mirrors in space: the sandwich and the half-angle, together

§2 promised that a rotation is two reflections. This section does that in algebra, and three things fall out of one calculation that are normally presented as three separate facts you have to accept.

5.1 A reflection, as a quaternion product

Take a plane through the origin with unit normal n, and a vector v. Treat both as pure quaternions and form the product n v n. Expand it with §4.1's formula, twice.

First n v. Both are pure, so nv = −(n·v) + (n×v). Now multiply that on the right by n, which is a general quaternion times a pure one:

\[ \begin{aligned} (nv)n &= \big(-(n\cdot v) + (n\times v)\big)\,n \\ &= -(n\cdot v)\,n \;+\; \big(-\!(n\times v)\cdot n \;+\; (n\times v)\times n\big) \end{aligned} \]

The term (n×v)·n is zero — a cross product is perpendicular to both its arguments, so it cannot have a component along n. And (n×v)×n is the standard triple product, which expands to v(n·n) − n(n·v) = v − (n·v)n since n is unit. Adding up:

\[ n\,v\,n \;=\; v \;-\; 2(n\cdot v)\,n \]
You have had this function since Lesson 1.8

v − 2(n·v)n is engine::reflect(v, n), in math/vec3.hpp, derived in Module 1 for a ball bouncing off a wall and used ever since for mirror directions in gfx/light.hpp. It is the Householder reflection, and it is what n v n computes. Two routes to one answer is a check; one route is a hope, so §C.1 measures the engine's function against the quaternion spelling across 20,000 random normals and vectors: 

   worst |n v n - reflect(v, n)|:     1.046e-06
  [PASS] C.1  n v n is the Householder reflection

So a reflection is one multiplication on each side. Compare complex.hpp's reflect_in_line, which is m² v̄ — the shapes differ because a mirror in the plane is named by its direction and a mirror in space by its normal, and because conjugating a pure quaternion negates it. The content is the same.

5.2 Do it twice, and watch what appears

Reflect in the plane with normal n₀, then in the plane with normal n₁. That is n v n applied twice, reading left to right through the sequence:

\[ v \;\longmapsto\; n_1\,(n_0\,v\,n_0)\,n_1 \;=\; (n_1n_0)\,v\,(n_0n_1) \]

using nothing but associativity to move the brackets. Now look at the two outer factors. They are the same two things in opposite orders, and for pure quaternions there is a name for that relationship:

\[ \overline{n_1 n_0} \;=\; \overline{n_0}\,\overline{n_1} \;=\; (-n_0)(-n_1) \;=\; n_0 n_1 \]

Conjugation reverses a product — that is a one-line consequence of the definition, true in any such algebra — and conjugating a pure quaternion just negates it, so the two minus signs cancel. Therefore, writing q = n₁n₀:

\[ v \;\longmapsto\; q\,v\,\bar q \]
The sandwich was not chosen

It fell out of composing two reflections. Nobody decided that a rotation should be applied from both sides; that is simply what "do two reflections" looks like once you write a reflection as n v n, and the right-hand factor is a conjugate because reversing a product of pure quaternions is exactly what conjugation does.

5.3 And q itself is the half-angle object

We have a formula for the rotation in terms of q = n₁n₀. Work out what that quaternion actually is, using §4.1 once more, and let φ be the angle between the two normals:

\[ q \;=\; n_1 n_0 \;=\; -(n_1 \cdot n_0) \;+\; (n_1 \times n_0) \;=\; -\cos\varphi \;-\; \sin\varphi\;\hat n \]

where n̂ is the unit vector along n₀ × n₁ — the line where the two planes meet, which is the axis, exactly as §2's two hands predicted. (The sign on the second term is because n₁ × n₀ = −(n₀ × n₁), and |n₀ × n₁| = sin φ.)

So q = −(cos φ + sin φ · n̂). There is an overall minus sign sitting in front of the thing we want, and nothing in the derivation determines it — reflecting in −n₀ instead of n₀ gives the same mirror plane and flips it. Negate, which §8 will justify properly and which you can take on trust for two paragraphs, and write θ = 2φ for the rotation angle the two mirrors generate:

\[ \boxed{\;q \;=\; \cos(\theta/2) \;+\; \sin(\theta/2)\,\hat n\;} \]

In prose: the quaternion for a rotation of θ about the unit axis n̂ has real part cos(θ/2) and vector part sin(θ/2) times the axis.

Prediction 3 discharged — and prediction 4 arrived uninvited

The θ/2 is not a convention. It is there because the mirrors are φ apart and the rotation is 2φ, which is a fact about reflections that Lesson 7.3 demonstrated in the plane with a protractor. And the double cover appeared inside the derivation, as a sign nobody could pin down, before anybody stated it as a property. That is the honest order of discovery and it is worth noticing: q and −q are not two conventions for one object, they are two genuinely different pairs of mirrors that generate the same turn.

5.4 Checked against Lesson 7.2, which knows nothing about any of this

§5 has produced a completely independent construction of a rotation: an axis, an angle, a half-angle object, and a two-sided product. Lesson 7.2 produced another one — Rodrigues' formula, derived by splitting a vector into its component along the axis and its component across it. If both are right they must agree everywhere, and this is the single most load-bearing measurement in the lesson:

Look at the second line of that transcript. rotor_from_mirrors genuinely returns (−0.866, 0, 0, −0.5) — the negated branch — and reading its angle without negating gives 300°, which is the same rotation as 60° and is not a bug in either. 5.5 The control, and it is the sharpest one in the lesson

§C.5 says composing quaternions matches composing matrices in the same order — M(qp) = M(q)M(p). That is a claim about the sandwich's orientation, and here is why a naive test of it is worthless.

The other sandwich, q̄ v q, is also a rotation. It sends the basis vectors somewhere perfectly sensible, its matrix is orthonormal, its determinant is 1, and if you checked it on a single rotation you would see nothing wrong. What it gets wrong is the order: it is an anti-homomorphism, so every composition comes out backwards. §C.6 builds it deliberately and demands both halves of that statement: 

   conj(q) v q:  |W(qp) - W(p)W(q)| = 2.980e-07
                 |W(qp) - W(q)W(p)| = 1.975e+00
  [PASS] C.6  CONTROL: the other sandwich reverses order

The first number must be tiny and the second must be large, and requiring both is what makes this a control rather than a tautology. A test that only checked "the wrong sandwich differs from the right one" would pass on a typo.

This also settles a question that is easy to get wrong in your own code and painful to debug: q * p means "do p, then do q", the same right-to-left reading as A * B * v. That reading is not free — it is a consequence of choosing q v q̄ over q̄ v q, and the other choice reverses every composition in the engine.

6. Implementation: the type

Everything above is now code. math/quat.hpp is written to be read as a diff against math/complex.hpp, function for function, and that is not a stylistic conceit — Lesson 7.3 chose the names in that file with this mapping in mind, so that the harder header could be read as the easier one with one more imaginary unit and one crucial loss.

6.1 The type, and why the storage is the derivation

struct quat
{
    float w{1.0f};   ///< cos(θ/2) for a unit rotation
    vec3 v{};        ///< sin(θ/2)·n̂ for a unit rotation

    static constexpr quat identity() { return {}; }
    static constexpr quat i() { return {0.0f, {1, 0, 0}}; }
    static constexpr quat j() { return {0.0f, {0, 1, 0}}; }
    static constexpr quat k() { return {0.0f, {0, 0, 1}}; }
    static constexpr quat pure(vec3 p) { return {0.0f, p}; }
};

Four contiguous floats in the order w, x, y, z. A good deal of published code — and most GPU-side packing — uses x, y, z, w instead. Neither is more correct; they are incompatible, and code that memcpys a quaternion across that boundary without saying which it means has a bug that looks like a 180° error about a diagonal axis. It is on the Conventions page §8e, which is where this course puts things that cannot be deduced.

The three imaginary units are worth typing into a test the first time you meet them, because quat::i() is a half turn, not a quarter turn, and that catches everyone arriving from complex numbers. complex::i() is a quarter turn of the plane; quat::i() has w = cos(θ/2) = 0, so θ = π. Multiply it by itself and you get −1, which is the same rotation as +1 — the double cover, in the smallest example there is.

6.2 The product

Two lines, and they are §4.1's formula read straight across: 

[[nodiscard]] constexpr quat operator*(quat a, quat b)
{
    return {a.w * b.w - dot(a.v, b.v),
            b.v * a.w + a.v * b.w + cross(a.v, b.v)};
}

Sixteen multiplies and twelve adds. A mat3 product is twenty-seven and eighteen. §10 measures whether that operation count survives contact with a real processor, and the answer is a useful lesson in itself.

6.3 Conjugate, norm, inverse — the block that did not change

Every function in this block is complex.hpp's function with im widened to a vec3. Not "analogous to" — the same algebra and the same proofs, because none of those proofs used commutativity. That is worth noticing while reading: the things that broke on the way up are exactly the things that mentioned the order of a product, and nothing here does. 

constexpr quat conjugate(quat q) { return {q.w, -q.v}; }

constexpr float length_squared(quat q)
{
    return q.w * q.w + dot(q.v, q.v);
}

inline quat inverse(quat q)
{
    const float len_sq = length_squared(q);
    if (len_sq <= 0.0f) { return quat::identity(); }
    return conjugate(q) / len_sq;
}

One detail deserves a sentence because it looks as though it ought to be a problem and is not. inverse is a genuine two-sided inverse even though the algebra does not commute: q·q̄ and q̄·q are both |q|², because the cross product of v with itself is zero. The one place non-commutativity would have hurt most is the one place it cannot reach.

6.4 Applying: the sandwich, optimised — and a correction

Computing q v q̄ literally is two quaternion products: 24 multiplies and 17 adds, with a known-zero scalar part being carried through both. Expanding it symbolically for unit q and collecting gives a much better form:

\[ v' \;=\; v \;+\; 2w\,(u \times v) \;+\; 2\,\big(u \times (u \times v)\big), \qquad u = q.v \]
[[nodiscard]] constexpr vec3 rotate(quat q, vec3 v)
{
    const vec3 t = cross(q.v, v) * 2.0f;
    return v + t * q.w + cross(q.v, t);
}

Two crosses, two scales, two adds: 18 multiplies and 12 adds. It is Rodrigues' formula in disguise, which it must be, since §C.4 measured that they describe the same turn.

A correction, and the harness is what caught it

The first draft of this function's documentation said "a non-unit q scales the result by |q|², exactly as a non-unit z does in the plane". That is true of the literal q v q̄ and false of the form above. Getting from (w² − |u|²)v + 2w(u×v) + 2(u·v)u to the two crosses substitutes u×(u×v) = (u·v)u − |u|²v, and the two expressions agree only when w² + |u|² = 1. Off the unit sphere this is a different linear map — not a rotation, not a scaled rotation, not a similarity at all. §F.6 measures both: 

   |q|    literal/|q|^2 err   rotate() err
   1.000    3.332e-08           0.000e+00
   1.100    1.032e-07           1.352e-01
   1.500    7.451e-08           8.046e-01
  [PASS] F.6  CONTROL: the literal form scales, rotate() does not

This matters far more than a documentation slip usually does, and §10.5 is where it bites.

6.5 Axes and angles, in and out

inline quat quat_from_axis_angle(vec3 unit_axis, float angle)
{
    const float half = angle * 0.5f;
    return {std::cos(half), unit_axis * std::sin(half)};

That is the only trigonometry in the file, together with its inverse and the Euler bridge that calls it — and noticing that is the point of the whole representation. Trig appears exactly at the boundary where a human angle enters or leaves. Composing, inverting, applying and (in Lesson 7.5) interpolating are arithmetic. 7. The one price, made visible

"Quaternions do not commute" is a sentence. gimbal's new C mode turns it into a picture and a number, and the picture is better than the number.

Take two turns of the same size φ, one about +Y and one about +X, and perform them in both orders. There is exactly one parameter, so the whole family is one slider — and it is the case everybody already knows by heart, because it is the book you turn twice on a desk.

7.1 Derived first, so the measurement has something to disagree with

With c = cos(φ/2) and s = sin(φ/2), §4.1's formula gives the two composites directly, and they are startlingly close together:

\[ \begin{aligned} q_y(\varphi)\,q_x(\varphi) &= \big(c^2,\;\; cs\,\hat x + cs\,\hat y - s^2 \hat z\big) \\ q_x(\varphi)\,q_y(\varphi) &= \big(c^2,\;\; cs\,\hat x + cs\,\hat y + s^2 \hat z\big) \end{aligned} \]

Identical but for the sign of the z component — which is the cross product, and nothing else, exactly as §4.2 said. Their four-component dot product is therefore c⁴ + 2c²s² − s⁴, and since the angle between two unit quaternions is twice the arccosine of that (§8.2 explains the factor of two), the gap is

\[ \text{gap}(\varphi) \;=\; 2\arccos\big|\,c^4 + 2c^2s^2 - s^4\,\big| \]

At φ = 90°, c = s = 1/\sqrt2, so c² = s² = 1/2 and the dot product is 1/4 + 1/2 − 1/4 = 1/2. So

\[ \text{gap}(90°) \;=\; 2\arccos(1/2) \;=\; 2 \times 60° \;=\; 120° \]

Two perpendicular quarter-turns, performed in the two orders, leave you 120° apart. Exactly 120°, with no rounding anywhere in the derivation.

7.2 Then measured, three ways

gimbal: commute 90.00 deg -> gap 120.0000 deg
gimbal:   matrix 120.0000, closed form 120.0000

Three routes to one number: the quaternion metric angle_between, the matrix metric angle_between_rotations that Lesson 7.1 wrote and that has never heard of a quaternion, and the closed form above. They share no code. A single route reporting 120° is a hope; three agreeing is a fact.

7.3 And the picture is the better half

The first draft of this mode drew only the two destinations — two aircraft and an arc between them. That is an honest picture of a fact nobody disputes, and it cannot show why they differ. Drawing each order as the two arcs it actually is fixes that for free:

The four arcs form a quadrilateral that fails to close. Same two turns, same two axes, same starting nose; the only difference is which one you do first, and the journeys end 120° apart. It is the same picture as walking a mile south, a mile east, a mile north and a mile west on a sphere and not arriving home — and it is the same phenomenon, which is why people who have met differential geometry call the gap holonomy and everyone else calls it "rotations don't commute".

The sweep runs φ from 0 to 180° and back, and the reason it goes back as well as out is that the interesting part is not the 120° at the far end — it is that the gap is present at every angle but zero, growing like φ² at first. Non-commutativity is not a threshold effect waiting at large angles. A pair of 5° turns do not commute either; they miss by 0.44°, which is small and is not nothing, and over a few thousand frames of integrating angular velocity it is Module 8's problem.

8. The double cover

§5.3 produced a sign that the derivation could not determine. This section is that observation, promoted to a property, measured, and then turned into the one API decision it forces.

8.1 Why q and −q are the same rotation, exactly

Look at the sandwich: q v q̄. Replace q with −q. The conjugate of −q is −q̄, so the expression becomes (−q) v (−q̄), and the two minus signs multiply to +1.

\[ (-q)\,v\,\overline{(-q)} \;=\; (-1)(-1)\,q\,v\,\bar q \;=\; q\,v\,\bar q \]

The sandwich is quadratic in q — every term carries one factor from each side — so a global sign cannot survive it. This is not "the same to within floating point"; it is the same sequence of operations on the same magnitudes, and the measurement says so: 

D. HALF ANGLES AND THE DOUBLE COVER
   worst |M(q) - M(-q)| over 20000:   0.000e+00
  [PASS] D.1  q and -q are the same rotation, bitwise

0.000e+00, not 1e-7. Over 20,000 random rotations, the two matrices are bit-identical.

So the map from unit quaternions to rotations is two-to-one. Every rotation has exactly two quaternions, differing by a sign, and no implementation can recover which one you started from. That is what "double cover" names, and Lesson 7.3 predicted it three lessons early from the mirrors: R and −R are different pairs of mirrors that generate the same turn, because a rotation remembers the angle between its mirrors and not which mirrors.

8.2 The bug you get if you forget

Here is the one place it must be handled, and it is the single most common quaternion bug in shipped code. The angle between two orientations is 

[[nodiscard]] inline float angle_between(quat a, quat b)
{
    const float d = a.w * b.w + dot(a.v, b.v);
    return 2.0f * std::acos(std::clamp(std::fabs(d), 0.0f, 1.0f));
}

Two things in that body deserve attention. The first is that the real part of q̄ₐ q_b is exactly the four-component dot product of a and b — one of the small miracles of this algebra, and the reason every slerp implementation you will ever read starts with a dot product. The second is the std::fabs, and it is the double cover: 

   angle_between(q, -q):    0.0000 deg
   with the fabs removed: 360.0000 deg
  [PASS] D.2  CONTROL: dropping the fabs reports 360 deg
Symptom → cause → fix

Symptom: a character occasionally spins all the way round between two keyframes that look adjacent in the authoring tool.
Cause: a metric or a blend that treats q and −q as far apart. The two poses are the same orientation; the two quaternions are antipodal.
Fix: take the absolute value of the dot product, or negate one input when the dot product is negative. Lesson 7.5 pays for this properly — the same sign choice, made once, is what makes slerp take the short way round.

Almost never, and it is occasionally a feature. §D's other two checks make the point:

9. Extraction: four candidates, and no bad case

Going from a quaternion to a matrix is three calls to rotate — column 0 is rotate(q, x̂) and so on, the definition of a matrix this course has used since Lesson 2.5. Going the other way is the interesting direction, and it is where the fourth component pays a dividend nobody advertises.

9.1 The naive route, and where it dies

The trace of a rotation matrix built from a unit quaternion is 4w² − 1, so

\[ w \;=\; \tfrac12\sqrt{1 + \operatorname{tr} R}, \qquad x = \frac{r_{21} - r_{12}}{4w}, \quad y = \frac{r_{02} - r_{20}}{4w}, \quad z = \frac{r_{10} - r_{01}}{4w} \]

Four lines, correct, and it is what almost every first implementation looks like. It divides by 4w, and w = cos(θ/2), so as the rotation approaches a half-turn it divides by nearly nothing, and at exactly 180° it divides by zero and loses the axis entirely.

9.2 The fix is Lesson 7.2's fix, which is why they are taught in this order

Lesson 7.2 hit exactly this shape and solved it by pivoting on the largest of three squared components, so that it never divided by something near zero. The three squares sum to 1, so the largest is at least 1/3 and the divisor at least 1/\sqrt3. That was good but not unconditional, and 7.2 had to derive a crossover at tan(θ/2) = \sqrt3, i.e. 120°, between two routes.

Here there are four squares to pivot on, and each is a trace-like combination of the diagonal:

\[ \begin{aligned} 4w^2 &= 1 + r_{00} + r_{11} + r_{22} \qquad & 4x^2 &= 1 + r_{00} - r_{11} - r_{22} \\ 4y^2 &= 1 - r_{00} + r_{11} - r_{22} \qquad & 4z^2 &= 1 - r_{00} - r_{11} + r_{22} \end{aligned} \]

Add the four. The diagonal terms cancel in pairs — r₀₀ appears twice with a plus and twice with a minus, and so does each of the others — leaving

\[ 4w^2 + 4x^2 + 4y^2 + 4z^2 \;=\; 4 \]

which is just |q| = 1 restated, and is the whole argument:

Four numbers summing to 4 leave the largest at least 1

So the divisor 2\sqrt{\text{largest}} is always at least 2, and this routine has no bad case anywhere on the range. No crossover to derive. No threshold to tune. No second route. Four candidates instead of three is what buys that — and four candidates is precisely what having a fourth component means.

9.4 A prediction the measurement refused

§G.3 was written to make a claim that turned out to be false, and the false version is more instructive than the true one would have been.

The prediction: extracting an axis from a quaternion should be better conditioned at small turns than extracting it from a matrix, because |v| = sin(θ/2) is a quantity of the size of the answer, whereas the matrix route divides by 2 sin θ formed as a difference of near-equal entries. Lesson 7.2 measured that difference costing an axis half a degree at θ = 10⁻⁵.

Why the prediction was wrong, and it is worth more than the prediction

quat_from_rotation's input is a matrix. At a small turn it pivots on w, and the three vector components then come off exactly the same antisymmetric differences the matrix route uses. The information was already gone before either route ran. A quaternion's better conditioning is a property of holding one, not of extracting one, and Lesson 7.2's hole at θ = 0 is a hole in the matrix that no extraction can patch.

So what the four-candidate pivot actually buys is not accuracy. It is the absence of a decision — no crossover, no threshold, no second route, and no failure at exactly π, which the naive quaternion route does have and §E.2 measures at 180° of error. That is a real and different benefit, and stating it correctly is worth more than the plausible claim the check was written to confirm.

10. What it costs, measured

Lesson 7.3 made a prediction about this section, in the plane, where the numbers were mild: a complex number was cheaper to store and cheaper to combine and exactly as expensive to use, because z × v and M × v are the same four multiplies. It then said the same trade reappears one dimension up with the same shape and different numbers. It does, and the difference is that here the third column is not a wash — it is a loss.

10.1 Composing: 1.57× cheaper

F. COST
   compose  quat*quat   1.666 ns  (16 mul, 12 add)
   compose  mat3*mat3   2.612 ns  (27 mul, 18 add)
   ratio                 1.57x
  [PASS] F.1  composing is cheaper as a quaternion

The operation counts predict 1.69×; the measurement says 1.57×, which is close enough that nothing surprising is happening — unlike Lesson 7.2's closed-form Rodrigues, where a 3× count produced a 1.3× measurement because most of the time was going on a sin that both spellings paid for.

One bias in that row is worth stating rather than hiding. The rule for a timed loop in this course is consume the whole result, and consuming a quaternion is 4 adds while consuming a matrix is 9. The matrix row is therefore carrying five extra adds it would not pay in a real caller, which overstates the quaternion's win slightly. The apply row below has no such bias — both sides accumulate three floats — and it is the one to trust as a calibration.

The first draft of this measurement read 0.000 ns

It wrote acc = qs[i] * qs[j] inside the loop, which overwrites acc every iteration, so only the last product is needed and the compiler knows it. That is a third distinct way to lose a timing in this course's harnesses, after 7.2's "read one element of nine" and "hoist the whole loop". A measurement of exactly zero is at least loud; the dangerous version of this mistake is the one that leaves a plausible small number behind.

10.2 Applying: 1.57× dearer

   apply    quat*vec3   1.679 ns  (18 mul, 12 add)
   apply    mat3*vec3   1.070 ns  ( 9 mul,  6 add)
   ratio                 1.57x  (quaternion slower)
  [PASS] F.2  applying is cheaper as a matrix

This is the honest row, and it is the one most treatments of quaternions omit. Rotating a vector with a quaternion costs 18 multiplies; with a matrix it costs 9. The quaternion is better at everything except the operation a renderer performs most.

10.3 So there is a crossover, and it is a number you can design with

Converting costs something, and each vector rotated saves something, so there is a break-even count: 

   convert  mat3_from_quat   4.619 ns
   crossover: 7.58 vectors per rotation
  [PASS] F.3  the crossover is a small number of vectors
\[ N^{*} \;=\; \frac{t_{\text{convert}}}{t_{\text{quat}\cdot v} - t_{\text{mat}\cdot v}} \;=\; \frac{4.619}{1.679 - 1.070} \;=\; 7.6 \]

In prose: building the matrix costs 4.619 ns and each vector then saves 0.609 ns, so the matrix pays for itself after about eight vectors.

The rule that falls out of one number

Rotate a point, use the sandwich. Rotate a mesh, build the matrix. Eight is a small number — a single skinned vertex influenced by four bones is already past it if each bone's quaternion is used once — which is why every renderer that is handed quaternions converts them before it draws anything, and why mat3_from_quat is not an afterthought in this header. Note also which way this cuts against the folklore: "quaternions are faster than matrices" is true of storing and composing and false of the thing a frame spends its time on.

10.4 Drift, and an answer that is the opposite of the plane's

Lesson 7.3 ran this experiment in two dimensions and the complex number won by 6.15×. Here is the same experiment in three, with both representations asked the same question — max |MᵀM − I|, because "how big is the norm error" and "how non-orthonormal is the matrix" are not comparable numbers: 

   step    quat raw   mat3 raw   quat tidied
    0.37   8.774e-02  1.780e-02  5.960e-08
    1.00   2.940e-02  2.674e-02  1.192e-07
    2.50   1.824e-01  5.681e-02  1.192e-07
   (tidied = renormalised_fast every step, no sqrt)
  [PASS] F.4  a tidied quaternion beats both raw walks

The raw quaternion loses, at every step size. That is not the result this section was expecting, and the reason is better than the result would have been. An unrepaired quaternion's norm error compounds as a power: if |q_step| = 1 + ε, then after n compositions |q| ≈ 1 + nε, and the sandwich then squares it. Three steps were measured rather than one precisely because the answer is set by how far one particular step's norm happens to sit from 1 — a property of an input, not of a representation.

And the third column is what actually matters: 

   repair   renormalised_fast   1.059 ns
   repair   Gram-Schmidt        3.437 ns
   ratio                         3.25x
  [PASS] F.4b repairing a quaternion is much cheaper

One renormalised_fast per step — no square root, no divide, one subtract and five multiplies — brings the walk to 6×10⁻⁸, five orders of magnitude better than either raw walk, at a third the cost of the Gram-Schmidt a matrix needs. That is the real advantage, and "quaternions drift less" is not it. Quaternions are cheap to repair, which means you can afford to repair them every frame, which means they never drift.

10.5 And 7.3's sharpest warning inverts

Lesson 7.3 found that renormalised_fast at |z| = 2 returns −z: a perfect modulus wearing a 180° error, so a unit test on |z| certified a function that turned the object the wrong way round. Its conclusion was "choose the quantity before the threshold — test the rotation, not the modulus".

Carry the function up a dimension — it generalises without a character changing, being a Taylor series about |q|² = 1 that knows nothing about dimension — and run the same experiment: 

   |q|    norm after fix   pose error
   1.000    5.960e-08        0.0000 deg
   1.050    3.812e-03        0.3349 deg
   1.250    1.016e-01        9.0135 deg
   1.500    4.375e-01       34.7161 deg
   1.750    9.453e-01       49.8686 deg
   2.000    2.980e-07        0.0000 deg
  [PASS] F.5  exact at |q| = 1 AND 2, wrong in between
The failure is not monotonic, and that is worse

At |q| = 2 the function returns −q, and −q is q as a rotation (§8.1). So the worst input in two dimensions is a perfect input in three: exactly unit, exactly the right turn, both bit for bit. A test that sampled the two obvious points — 1.0 and 2.0 — would certify this function completely. The damage is in the middle, where nobody thinks to look.

And the damage lands on the pose rather than on the scale, because of §6.4's correction: rotate's two-cross form equals q v q̄ only on the unit sphere, so a norm error becomes an orientation error. At |q| = 1.5 the object is 34.7° wrong, which will not look like a normalisation problem when you meet it in a scene.

The transferable lesson is one level up from 7.3's. That lesson said choose the observable. This one says choose the sample points, and it says that a failure mode carried from one dimension to another is a hypothesis about the new dimension, not a fact about it.

11. What a quaternion cannot hold

math/transform.hpp has carried this sentence since Module 2:

Written in Lesson 2.8, and wrong by Module 5

"Everything downstream asks for a matrix, so the swap touches one line of parent_from_local() and nothing else."

The plan was always that this lesson would perform the swap: transform::rotation stops being a mat3 and becomes a quat. It does not, and the reason is a finding rather than a schedule.

11.1 The field is called rotation and one caller does not put one in it

gfx/renderable.cpp builds a transform out of an ECS world_transform, which is the matrix the hierarchy (Lesson 5.9) produced by composing an object's local transform with all of its ancestors': 

.xform = {.position = translation_of(w.matrix),
          .rotation = linear_of(w.matrix),
          .scale    = {1.0f, 1.0f, 1.0f}},

Its comment says — correctly, today — that the recomposition "reproduces the original affine matrix to the bit". And it does. It reproduces it because a mat3 will hold anything, including the scale that came down the hierarchy from a parent, which is then silently carried in a field named rotation while the field named scale says 1.

A quaternion will not hold that. Narrowing the type turns this line into a compile error — which is the type doing exactly the job it is here to do — and the repair is a decomposition rather than a rename.

11.2 What actually happens if you ignore it, measured

The first draft of this section assumed the failure would be "the scale is silently dropped", which would at least be an interpretable answer. The measurement refused it: 

H. WHAT A QUATERNION CANNOT HOLD
   det before 8.0000, det after 1.6531
   |q| of the extraction: 1.333721
   pose error vs the true rotation: 28.1519 deg
  [PASS] H.1  a scaled rotation extracts to neither answer

Hand quat_from_rotation a matrix 2R and it does not return R with the 2 discarded. §9.2's four candidates are built from the diagonal, a uniform scale of s moves them to 1 + s·(…), and they no longer sum to 4. What comes back has norm 1.334, its matrix has determinant 1.65, and the pose is 28.15° from the true rotation. That is neither 8 nor 1, neither the rotation nor a scaled rotation — a failure with no clean interpretation, which is exactly why it must not be reachable by accident. 

   non-uniform scale: pose moves 1.2089 deg
  [PASS] H.2  a non-uniform scale also ROTATES the result

11.3 The repair, and its honest limit

Six lines. Take the scale off as the three column lengths — each column of the matrix is where a basis vector landed, and T·R·S scales column k by scale[k] (Lesson 2.8) — then extract from what is left: 

auto decompose = [](const mat3& m, vec3& out_scale) {
    out_scale = {length(m.c0), length(m.c1), length(m.c2)};
    return quat_from_rotation(mat3{m.c0 / out_scale.x,
                                   m.c1 / out_scale.y,
                                   m.c2 / out_scale.z});
};
   after decomposing: scale (1.000, 0.400, 1.000)
   pose error now 0.000e+00 deg
  [PASS] H.3  taking the scale off first recovers the rotation

Exact, for uniform and non-uniform scale alike. And then the control, which states the limit rather than letting a reader assume there isn't one: 

   shear: column lengths (1.000, 1.000, 1.000)
   worst entry error after repair: 0.1500
  [PASS] H.4  CONTROL: a shear defeats the repair too

A shear survives neither route. Column lengths cannot see one, because a shear does not change the lengths of the columns — it changes the angles between them. The honest statement is that a position + quat + vec3 transform can represent translation, rotation and axis-aligned scale, and cannot represent shear, and that this is a limitation essentially every engine accepts because artists do not author shear and hierarchies with non-uniform scale in them are already a known source of grief.

11.4 So the swap is Lesson 7.5's, and here is the trade

What was decided, and why

The swap touches roughly twenty-five call sites across ten files, and has the design decision of §11.3 sitting inside one of them. Performing it here would have meant listing ten files in full — this course does not ship a listing with // ... in it — and the derivation that is this lesson's actual content would have been the smaller half of the page.

Lesson 7.5 makes it, with quat_slerp in hand, so that the call sites that move get something in return rather than merely a different spelling. What 7.4 leaves behind is the type, fully derived and fully measured, and the finding above written into transform.hpp where the next person to read that promise will meet it.

There is a general lesson in §11.1 worth taking away from the specific one. A narrower type does not just prevent future mistakes; it finds existing ones. That assignment has been correct, tested, and shipping since Lesson 5.11. It became visible the moment something refused to accept a value it was never supposed to receive — and the "to the bit" guarantee in its comment, which reads like a strength, was the symptom.

12. Four representations, side by side

Module 7 has now built four ways to write down a rotation in four lessons. Here they are together, which is the clearest statement of why the arc had to go in this order:

Read the quat column down. It loses exactly one row — applying to a point — and that row has a workaround costing 4.6 ns which is worth taking after eight vectors. Everything else is either a win or a tie.

And read across the rows, because the table is not an argument for deleting three things. All four are still in the engine and all four are still right for something:

  • Euler angles are an interface — what a human types and what somebody else's file format stores. Lesson 7.1 built math/euler.hpp and deliberately did not put a euler_angles in any struct.
  • Axis-angle is a reading — the answer to "what single turn is this pose?", which is how you debug an orientation and how Euler's rotation theorem becomes something you can print.
  • A matrix is what the renderer multiplies by, and the crossover in §10.3 is the reason.
  • A quaternion is what the engine will store, from Lesson 7.5.

That is four representations doing four different jobs, with explicit conversions between them, and it is the normal state of affairs in a real engine rather than a failure to pick a winner.

15. Common pitfalls & debugging

15.1 The object spins all the way round between two adjacent keyframes

Cause: a metric or a blend that has not handled the double cover. The two poses are the same orientation and the two quaternions are antipodal, so the naive angle between them is 360° rather than 0°.
Fix: std::fabs on the dot product, or negate one input when the dot product is negative. §8.2, and Lesson 7.5 in full.

15.2 Everything is rotated backwards, and consistently

Cause: the sandwich is q̄ v q rather than q v q̄, or the product is spelled with the operands swapped. Both produce genuine rotations, so a single-object test passes.
Symptom that separates them: composition. M(qp) comes out equal to M(p)M(q) instead of M(q)M(p), so a parent-child hierarchy applies its transforms in the wrong order while each individual object looks fine. §C.6 is that test.

15.3 The scene slowly inflates

Cause: quaternions accumulating without repair. mat3_from_quat scales by |q|², so a norm that has drifted to 1.01 produces a matrix with determinant 1.01⁶ ≈ 1.06, and every object is 6% too big.
Fix: renormalised_fast wherever a rotation is stepped incrementally — it costs 1.06 ns. §10.4.

15.4 An object is wrong by a large angle and normalising does not help

Cause: rotate called on a non-unit quaternion. §6.4: the two-cross form equals q v q̄ only on the unit sphere, so off it you get neither a rotation nor a scaled one. At |q| = 1.5 the error is 34.7°.
Why it is hard to spot: it does not look like a scale problem, so nobody checks the norm.

15.5 quat::i() does not do what you expected

Cause: arriving from complex numbers, where i is a quarter turn. quat::i() has w = cos(θ/2) = 0, so it is a half turn. The quarter turn about x is (0.707107, 0.707107, 0, 0).

15.6 A quaternion from another library is a 180° error about a diagonal

Cause: component order. This engine stores w, x, y, z; a great deal of published code and most GPU packing uses x, y, z, w. A blind memcpy across that boundary rotates w into x and produces exactly that symptom. Conventions §8e.

15.7 An extraction is fine in testing and wrong in the field

Cause: the naive trace route (§9.1). It is indistinguishable from Shepperd's up to about 120°, which is where a hand-written test suite lives, and it is off by 180° at 179.99°. Rotations near a half-turn are not exotic — a character facing away from the camera is one.

15.8 A process pitfall, from writing this lesson

A figure that measures an angle with acos reports exactly zero. §9.4's first draft compared two recovered axes with acos(|a·b|) and printed 0.000e+00 in every row — not a pass, a blind instrument. An axis error of 5.7×10⁻⁵ rad puts the dot product at 1 − 1.6×10⁻⁹, which rounds to exactly 1.0f, and acos of exactly 1 is exactly 0. That is Lesson 7.1 §6.4's finding arriving from a third direction. Measure the chord |a − b|, which is of the size of the answer, and turn it back into an angle with 2 asin(chord/2).

15.9 And one about drawing

A render figure on the page's own pale panel loses the colours the program chose. Every screenshot figure in this course before now sat on .fill-soft, which is near-white in light mode. That is fine for a lit solid and destroys a thin bright line: §8.3's gold dial hand — the entire content of that figure — was at 1.8:1 against it. The fix is one CSS rule, .fill-shot, giving a render panel the program's own background. The alternative, changing the demo's palette to suit the page, would have been the page telling a lie about the program.

16. Exercises

Exercise 7.4.1 — The other convention guided

Write quat_from_xyzw(const float* p) and quat_to_xyzw(quat q, float* p), and a round-trip test. Then deliberately break it: read a w,x,y,z buffer as x,y,z,w and measure, in degrees, how far the resulting orientation is from the intended one for a 30° turn about ŷ.

Hint. Use angle_between_rotations on the two matrices. The answer is large and it is not 180° — work out why before you run it.

Exercise 7.4.2 — is_rotation, finally guided

Lesson 7.1's Exercise 5 asked for a predicate that says whether a mat3 is a rotation, and it has been open ever since — §11 is the fourth place in this course to note its absence. Write it: bool is_rotation(const mat3& m, float tol), checking both MᵀM = I and det M = +1. Then answer the design question with a measurement rather than an opinion: what tolerance passes every matrix mat3_from_quat produces from a unit quaternion, over 20,000 random ones, and does it reject the 1.65-determinant matrix of §11.2?

Hint. Both halves are needed. MᵀM = I alone accepts a reflection, whose determinant is −1 — and a reflection composed into a scene graph turns a model inside out while every normal still looks unit.

Exercise 7.4.3 — The crossover on your machine measurement

§10.3 measured the break-even at 7.6 vectors. Reproduce it, then change the experiment: write the closed-form mat3_from_quat (the nine entries are in the header's doc comment) and measure it against the three-calls-to-rotate version. Does the crossover move? By how much, and is the closed form worth the loss of checkability?

Hint. Lesson 7.2 ran the identical experiment on rotation_from_axis_angle and found the closed form 1.3–1.6× faster, never the 3× its multiply count predicted, because both spellings paid for one sin. There is no trigonometry here at all, so predict the answer first.

Exercise 7.4.4 — Where does the gap grow fastest? derivation

§7.1 derived the gap between the two composition orders as 2 arccos|c⁴ + 2c²s² − s⁴| for two equal perpendicular turns of φ. Expand it for small φ and show the gap goes as φ². Then find the φ at which the gap is largest, and check it against the demo.

Hint. c⁴ + 2c²s² − s⁴ = (c² + s²)² − 2s⁴ = 1 − 2s⁴, which makes both questions easy. The gap is maximal where 1 − 2s⁴ is most negative, and s ≤ 1 bounds that.

Exercise 7.4.5 — A rotation from one vector to another engine challenge

Write quat quat_from_to(vec3 from, vec3 to): the shortest rotation carrying one unit vector onto another. The obvious construction is the axis from × to and the angle acos(from · to), which needs two transcendentals and dies when the vectors are nearly parallel or nearly opposite. There is a construction with no trigonometry at all that handles the parallel case gracefully — find it, and then handle the opposite case explicitly, because it genuinely has no shortest rotation and any perpendicular axis is correct.

Hint. §5 says a quaternion is built from two mirrors. What are the two mirror planes whose composite carries from to to? One of them is the plane bisecting the two vectors, which you can write down with an addition and a normalise. For the opposite case, from and any vector not parallel to it give you a perpendicular by one cross product — and choosing "any vector" robustly means choosing the coordinate axis from is least aligned with.

Exercise 7.4.6 — Integrate an angular velocity engine challenge

Module 8 will need this. Given an orientation q and an angular velocity ω in radians per second (a vector whose direction is the axis and whose length is the rate), advance the orientation by h seconds. Do it two ways — the exact way, with quat_from_axis_angle(normalised(ω), |ω|h), and the cheap way, using the derivative q̇ = ½ ω q as a Euler step followed by renormalised_fast — and measure how far apart they drift over ten seconds at 60 Hz for a few rates.

Hint. Derive q̇ = ½ ω q first, and the ½ should now be unsurprising: differentiate cos(θ/2) + sin(θ/2) n̂ with respect to time at θ = 0. The cheap way is what a physics engine actually does, and finding the rate at which it stops being acceptable is the point of the exercise. Guard |ω| = 0 in the exact version — normalised of a zero vector is the reason normalised_or exists.

17. Recap & next

The engine can now write down a rotation in four floats that compose in sixteen multiplies with no trigonometry, have no singularity anywhere, extract from a matrix with no bad case, and repair in one Taylor step. More to the point, nothing in this lesson was asserted.

  • The multiplication table was forced by isotropy. One demand — that space has no preferred direction, so every unit imaginary squares to −1 — gives ji = −ij in three lines, and associativity gives the other eleven entries. ijk = −1 arrives as a consequence.
  • The dot product and the cross product are inside the quaternion product, and the cross product is exactly the term that changes sign under a swap. "Quaternions do not commute" and "rotations of space do not commute" are the same sentence.
  • The sandwich and the half-angle came out of the same calculation — composing two reflections in planes — and so did the double cover, as a sign nobody could determine. Three facts usually presented separately, from one derivation, and Lesson 7.3 had already drawn all of it in the plane with a protractor.
  • Four candidates that sum to 4 leave the largest at least 1, so extraction has no threshold, no crossover and no bad case — where Lesson 7.2's three candidates needed a derived crossover at 120°.
  • 1.57× cheaper to compose, 1.57× dearer to apply, crossing over at 7.6 vectors. That single number is why renderers convert quaternions to matrices before drawing.
  • Two measurements refused the claims they were written to confirm. A quaternion's better conditioning turns out to be a property of holding one, not of extracting one; and renormalised_fast is exact at |q| = 1 and at |q| = 2 and catastrophic between, so a test at the two obvious points certifies a broken function.

Next: Lesson 7.5 — Slerp. The formula is already written: Lesson 7.3's a·(a⁻¹b)^t is not specific to the plane and not specific to complex numbers — it is the definition of a geodesic on any group with those three operations, which is why 7.3's three-line complex_slerp and 7.2's rotation_slerp for mat3 turned out to be the same three steps in different notation. What is genuinely new is the sign: §8 proved there are two quaternions per rotation, so there are two arcs between any two poses, and one of them is the long way round. 7.5 also performs the storage swap §11 declined, with the decomposition of §11.3 in the one place that needs it.
