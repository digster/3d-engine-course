Back-Face Culling

You cannot see the far side of a solid object. That is so obvious it sounds like nothing, and it is worth about a third of the rasterizer’s time. Better still: the test is a sign the rasterizer was already computing, and switching it on settles a twenty-nine-pixel debt Lesson 3.1 measured, named, and could not explain away.

1The Problem

This lesson has two problems, and the interesting thing about it is that they turn out to be the same problem.

The first is waste. The demo’s solids scene submits 44 triangles to the rasterizer every frame. Twenty-one of them face the camera. The other twenty-three are on the far side of a cube or an icosahedron — solid, opaque objects — and are therefore completely hidden behind their own front faces. The z-buffer discovers this the expensive way: it interpolates their depth across every pixel they cover and then throws almost all of that work away.

The second is a debt. Lesson 3.1 built a counter that renders the scene twice — once with the painter’s algorithm, once with the z-buffer — and reports how many pixels the two disagree about. On the deliberately-broken scenes it reads in the hundreds, which was the point. On the ordinary solids scene, where sorting should be perfectly adequate, it reads 29, and no amount of improving the sort moves it. Lesson 3.1 tracked that number down, offered an explanation, and — unusually — tested it:

So we already know the fix works. What this lesson owes you is the why, in enough detail that you could have predicted it — and the honest accounting of what culling does and does not change, which turns out to be more interesting than either of the two tidy answers.

2Building Intuition

2.1 The far side of a closed thing

Hold a coffee mug. You can see the near side of it and not the far side, and you do not need a depth buffer to know that — you know it because the mug is a closed opaque surface, so any ray leaving your eye that reaches the far wall must have gone through the near wall first. The near wall is opaque. Done.

That is the entire theorem, and it is worth noticing how strong the assumption is. It needs the surface to be closed — no holes, no open edges — and it needs every face to know which of its two sides is “out”. Break either and the argument collapses instantly: a sheet of paper has a front and a back, and you can see both, one at a time.

So we are looking for a per-triangle test that answers “is this triangle’s outward side the side facing me?” — and, since we are going to run it on every triangle of every object every frame, we would very much like it to be cheap.

2.2 Winding is which way you are looking at it

Draw a triangle on a piece of glass, going round its corners in order: 0, 1, 2. From one side of the glass that path runs counter-clockwise. Walk round the glass and look from the other side, and the same path, in the same order, now runs clockwise. Nothing moved. The order of the vertices did not change. The only thing that changed is which side you are on.

Every mesh in this course was authored with a convention: the vertices of each triangle are listed counter-clockwise as seen from outside the object. Lesson 2.12 stated it, mesh.hpp repeats it above every array, and Lesson 2.12’s validator checked it. So a triangle appears counter-clockwise on screen exactly when we are looking at its outside — and clockwise when we have got round the back of it.

Which means the whole of back-face culling is: measure the winding of the projected triangle, and throw it away if it is the wrong one. No normals, no dot products, no lighting-style geometry. Just the direction three points go round.

3The Theory

3.1 The test we are already computing

Lesson 2.2 built edge_function, and Lesson 2.3 pointed out that it has two readings: its sign says which side of a directed line a point is on, and its magnitude is twice the area of a triangle. Applied to a triangle’s three corners it gives twice the signed area:

\[ 2A \;=\; (b_x - a_x)(c_y - a_y) \;-\; (b_y - a_y)(c_x - a_x) \] 2*area = (bx-ax)*(cy-ay) - (by-ay)*(cx-ax)

Signed, because it changes sign when you reverse the winding — which is exactly §2.2’s observation, in arithmetic. And fill_triangle has been computing it since Lesson 2.2, on every triangle, for a completely different reason:

Look at what those five lines do. They measure the facing, and then they destroy it — deliberately, because the top-left rule (Lesson 2.2 §3.6) is stated for a positively-oriented triangle and a fill that vanished on “backwards” input would have been indistinguishable from a bug. That was the right call at the time and Lesson 2.2’s comment says so.

Culling is therefore not a new computation. It is one comparison inserted between those two statements: read the sign before the swap throws it away. Everything else in this lesson is about getting that comparison the right way round, and about knowing when you are allowed to use it at all.

3.2 Which sign? The y-flip decides

Here is the trap, and it is worth walking into deliberately rather than discovering it by trying both signs and keeping the one that looked right.

Our winding convention is stated in NDC ( conventions §7): counter-clockwise is front-facing, in a space where +y points up. But edge_function is evaluated on framebuffer coordinates, where +y points down — and Lesson 2.11’s viewport transform is what puts it there.

Negating one coordinate is a reflection, and a reflection reverses the sign of every signed area. So the same triangle, front-facing, has a positive signed area in NDC and a negative one in framebuffer coordinates:

Numbers — pushed through the real viewport

Take the NDC triangle (0,0), (0.6, 0), (0, 0.6). Its signed area in a +y-up frame is ½[(0.6)(0.6) − (0)(0)] = +0.18, so it is counter-clockwise, so it is front-facing by our convention.

Now apply Lesson 2.11’s viewport::to_screen with a full 320×180 framebuffer:

(0.0, 0.0) → (160, 90)
(0.6, 0.0) → (256, 90)
(0.0, 0.6) → (160, 36)   ← smaller y, because +y flipped

edge_function = (256−160)(36−90) − (90−90)(160−160)
            = (96)(−54) − 0 = −5184

verified: scratch/verify_34.cpp §A

Do not guess this by experiment

Getting the sign backwards produces a picture that is wrong in a plausible way: you see the inside of every object, which at a glance can read as “the lighting is odd” or “the model is inside-out”. And flipping the comparison makes it look right again, so the trial-and-error fix appears to work — until the day something else also flips (a mirrored transform, a negative scale, an imported mesh with the other convention) and now two wrongs are fighting. Derive it once, write down which space the convention lives in, and put the rule in a named function.

3.3 Where to ask: three tests, one of them wrong

There is more than one way to ask “is this facing me?”, and the differences matter. Here are the three you will meet, in the order people usually write them.

Test 1 — the axis test. Compute the triangle’s normal, dot it with the camera’s forward direction, and call it front-facing if the result is negative. It is the first thing everyone writes, it reads like the definition of “facing the camera”, and it is wrong.

Test 2 — the ray test. Dot the normal with the vector from the eye to the triangle. Front-facing if negative. This one is correct.

Test 3 — the area test. Project the triangle and read the sign of its signed area. Also correct — and, as §3.4 shows, exactly the same test as 2, with the projection doing the arithmetic for free.

The difference between 1 and 2 is the whole point. “Facing away” is not relative to the direction the camera is pointing; it is relative to the direction the light would have to travel to reach the camera. Under perspective those are different for every triangle except the one dead ahead, and they diverge by more the further off-axis you look.

How often does that actually bite? The harness fills the frustum with randomly oriented triangles and counts:

At the demo’s own field of view the axis test gets one triangle in six wrong. That is not a subtle numerical issue; it is a different question producing a different answer. And the last row is the one to remember, because a 120° field of view is not exotic — it is an ordinary first-person game.

Why the axis test feels right — and when it is

Run the same experiment under an orthographic projection and the axis test is wrong 0 times out of 200,000. That is not luck: orthographic projection is the statement that every ray to the eye is the camera axis. The axis test is not a sloppy approximation of the ray test — it is the ray test, for a camera we are not using.

Which is also why it survives so long in codebases. It is exactly right in the orthographic views a level editor uses, and it is subtly wrong in the game.

Two things to try. Drag the face toward the centre of the frustum: the angle between the two arrows shrinks, the wedge narrows, and near the axis the two tests become almost impossible to tell apart — which is exactly why this bug survives casual testing. Then push the field of view slider wider. The angle at any given position does not change — it is a property of where the face is, not of the lens — but the frustum grows, so more of your scene now sits at angles where the wedge is wide. That is the whole content of the table above, in one control.

3.4 Why the screen test is the view test

Tests 2 and 3 look like different animals — one is a dot product in 3-D, the other is the orientation of a projected triangle. They are the same number wearing different clothes, and the derivation is short enough to do here.

Start with the normal. For a triangle with corners a, b, c in view space, n = (b − a) × (c − a). Dot it with a and expand:

\[ \mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{a} = \big[(\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{a})\times(\mathbf{c}-\mathbf{a})\big]\cdot\mathbf{a} = \mathbf{a}\cdot(\mathbf{b}\times\mathbf{c}) = \det[\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b},\mathbf{c}] \] dot(n, a) = a · (b × c) = det[a, b, c]

Every cross term dies because a × a = 0 and a triple product with a repeated vector is zero. So the ray test — dot(n, a) < 0, which is the same as dot(n, centroid) < 0 because all three corners lie in the plane — is really the determinant of the three corners. Geometrically that is six times the signed volume of the tetrahedron formed by the eye and the triangle, and it is negative exactly when the eye is on the outward side.

Now project. With w = −z, the NDC coordinates are u = kx x/w and v = ky y/w for positive constants. Grind the signed area of the projected triangle through and everything collapses:

\[ 2A_{\text{ndc}} \;=\; k_x k_y \cdot \frac{-\det[\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b},\mathbf{c}]}{w_a\,w_b\,w_c} \] 2 * area_ndc = kx*ky * ( -det[a,b,c] ) / (w_a * w_b * w_c)

Read that carefully, because it settles three things at once.

  • The divisor is strictly positive. Every vertex reaching this point has w ≥ near > 0 — which is precisely what Lesson 3.3’s clipper guarantees. So the divisor cannot change the sign, and the projected orientation is decided by det[a,b,c] alone. The two tests are one test.
  • Clipping is load-bearing for culling. Let a vertex through with w < 0 and one factor of the divisor goes negative, flipping the projected orientation of a triangle whose geometry never changed. Culling and clipping are not independent features; the second is what makes the first meaningful.
  • The minus sign is §3.2’s y-flip, arriving from the algebra instead of from a diagram. Front-facing means det < 0, hence 2Andc > 0 (counter-clockwise, as the convention says), hence a negative edge function once the viewport flips y a second time.
Numbers — one triangle, all three tests

Take a = (0,0,−1), b = (1,0,−1), c = (0,1,−1) — a triangle one unit in front of the eye.

n = (b−a) × (c−a) = (1,0,0) × (0,1,0) = (0,0,1)
dot(n, a) = −1  → NEGATIVE → front-facing
a · (b × c) = (0,0,−1) · (1,1,1) = −1   ✓ the same number
u,v = (0,0), (1,0), (0,1)  → 2A_ndc = +1 → counter-clockwise ✓
w_a w_b w_c = 1, so −det / 1 = +1 ✓ the identity, exactly

Over 399,935 random triangles the harness finds the identity holds to a relative error of 1.06×10⁻⁶, and the ray test and the (unrounded) screen-area test disagree on zero of them.

verified: scratch/verify_34.cpp §B

The one caveat, and it is about pixels, not maths

Against the rounded integer pixel coordinates the two tests disagree on 768 of those 399,935 triangles — 0.19%. Every one is a sliver: the widest is 1.71 pixels across, measured as area over extent. Rounding three corners to integers moves each by up to half a pixel, which is enough to turn a one-pixel-wide shape inside out. Restrict to triangles at least four pixels wide and the disagreements go to zero.

This is worth knowing rather than worrying about: a triangle that thin covers almost nothing, and whichever way it is classified the picture barely moves. But it is also why real hardware rasterises with sub-pixel coordinates (typically 4 or 8 fractional bits) rather than whole pixels — and this is one of the reasons.

3.5 “It removes half your triangles” — measured

Everyone says half. It sounds right: a closed surface has an inside and an outside, and you can only see one of them. So let us check, by rotating a cube and an icosahedron through thousands of random orientations and counting.

MeshTrianglesFront-facingMeanExactly half
cube122 … 6 5.55 (46.2%)4,492 of 6,000
icosahedron207 … 10 8.80 (44.0%)1,619 of 6,000

Never more than half, and usually fewer. The icosahedron shows exactly half its faces in barely a quarter of orientations. So “half” is not a rule, it is a ceiling — and the reason is a nice piece of geometry worth a minute.

Pair up faces whose planes are parallel. The eye is in front of face +n when it is beyond that plane, and in front of face −n when it is beyond the other one. It cannot be beyond both — they face opposite ways. But it can easily be beyond neither, by sitting in the slab between them, and then both faces of that pair are back-facing.

And the saving in time is smaller still. Timing the demo’s solids scene, 44 triangles, a full frame of fills:

Numbers — what culling actually costs and saves

cull none: 19.95 µs
cull back: 13.64 µs   — 68.4% of the time

54.8% of triangles skipped buys 31.6% of the time back. The gap is not a measurement error and it is worth understanding: the back faces of a closed mesh are, by construction, the triangles the z-buffer was already rejecting on their very first depth comparison. They were never the expensive ones. What culling removes is their set-up — the bounding box, the edge-function increments, the attribute pre-division — plus one cheap failed test per covered pixel.

Which is still a third of the frame, for one comparison. But state it as 31.6%, not 50%, and state that you measured it.

3.6 The precondition: closed geometry

§2.1’s argument needed the surface to be closed. That was not a throwaway word. Culling is valid exactly when “this face points away from you” implies “something else of this object is in the way”, and for an open surface that implication is simply false.

The demo has both kinds. The cube and the icosahedron are closed solids. The ground plane, the three planks in the cycle scene, and the intersecting quads are sheets: infinitely thin, with two sides you can genuinely look at. Turn back-face culling on and walk round to the far side of one, and it is gone.

Numbers — a single quad, from four elevations

elevation   front-facing   culled px   unculled px
  +0.60          0          0        156   ← invisible
  +0.20          2       132        132
 −0.20          2       420        420
 −0.60          2       642        642

Below the plane the quad is drawn identically with and without culling. Cross to the other side and it stops existing. That is not a bug in the culler — the culler is answering the question it was asked, correctly. It is the culler being applied to geometry that does not satisfy its precondition.

verified: scratch/verify_34.cpp §G

So cull mode is not a global switch you turn on once. It is a property of the thing being drawn — which is why SDL_GPU keeps it in SDL_GPURasterizerState, part of a pipeline object, and why a real engine attaches it to a material. Two objects with different cull modes are two pipelines and therefore two draw batches, and Module 6 will care about that a great deal.

The third case: geometry that is closed but lies about it

A mesh can be closed and still cull wrongly, if its triangles are wound inconsistently. Half the faces vanish and the object looks like it has been bitten. Hand-authored meshes drift into this constantly, which is why Lesson 2.12 wrote a validator rather than trusting the arrays — and why Lesson 3.5’s OBJ loader will have to face the same question about geometry it did not author.

A negative determinant in the model matrix does it too. Mirroring an object by scaling one axis by −1 reverses the winding of every triangle in it, so a correctly-wound mesh becomes uniformly inside-out. Real engines detect this and flip the cull mode to compensate; ours does not, and Exercise 3.4.3 is where you find out what that looks like.

3.7 The debt: why 29 becomes 0

Now we can pay Lesson 3.1 back properly.

Consider the silhouette of a closed convex object — the outline where it turns away from you. Along that outline a front face and a back face meet at a shared mesh edge, and on that edge they are not merely close in depth, they are at exactly the same depth: it is one edge, with one pair of endpoints, belonging to both triangles.

So on those edge pixels the picture depends on mesh order, and about half the time the winner is a back face — you are seeing the inside of a solid object, one pixel wide, along its outline. The painter’s algorithm sorts by average depth and resolves the same tie differently, and there is the 29-pixel disagreement: two renderers, both “correct”, both showing you a surface that should have been invisible.

Culling removes the back face entirely, so there is no tie to lose. Both algorithms then agree, and they agree on the right answer.

Numbers — the debt, paid

cull        tris   front   drawn    painter vs z-buffer
none         44     21      44     29 px
back         44     21      21     0 px

Exactly the number Lesson 3.1 predicted, from an explanation it could not otherwise confirm.

verified: scratch/verify_34.cpp §D

That leaves an uncomfortable question, and the honest answer is the best thing in this lesson. If culling changes the picture, is it really an optimisation?

Not entirely, and here is what was measured. Over 1,008 camera and rotation combinations on the solids scene, culled and unculled renders differ by up to 44 pixels out of a mean covered area of 788. And 100% of the differing pixels are pixels a back face had been drawn on — which is the strongest possible form of the claim, and needs no threshold to state: the only thing culling can touch is a place where you were seeing the inside of a solid.

Two mechanisms put a back face there, and both are ties:

  1. The depth tie of Figure 6. Draw the front faces first and this one goes away — the worst case falls from 44 px to 29 px, which is a rather satisfying way to rediscover 3.1’s number from the other direction.
  2. Rounding. The remaining 29 px are the two faces quantising to integer pixels independently, so the back face’s outline sticks out by a pixel where the front face’s does not quite reach. No draw order fixes that. Only removing the back face does.
The honest summary

Back-face culling is an optimisation that is also, marginally, a correctness improvement — not because the z-buffer was broken, but because a tie-break has to go somewhere, and “never show the inside of a solid” is a better rule than “whichever triangle the index buffer listed first”.

Lesson 3.3 drew a firm line between correctness and optimisation and said knowing which side you are on is what lets you skip things deliberately. This lesson is the case that shows the line is real but not always sharp. Both statements are worth holding at once.

4Implementation

Four small pieces: a named test, an enum, one branch in the rasterizer, and the demo instrumentation that lets you watch it work and watch it fail.

4.1 Name the rule

The test is edge_function(…) < 0. Writing that literally at the point of use would be shorter than what we are about to do, and it would be a mistake — because the rule now has two readers: the rasterizer, which acts on it, and the demo, which counts it for the HUD. Lesson 2.4 made exactly this move with is_top_left and gave the reason: a rule you cannot inspect is a rule you cannot check.

It takes screen-space vertices, and that signature is doing real work. There is no way to hand this function a view-space position, so the §3.3 bug — asking the question before the projection, against the camera axis — cannot be written by accident. The type says where the test belongs.

4.2 The knob, in the state object

The enumerators are in that order because SDL_GPUCullMode is NONE, FRONT, BACK — checked against SDL3/SDL_gpu.h, not remembered — so Module 4’s port is a rename. And it goes into fill_style rather than becoming a parameter, because that is where SDL_GPU keeps it too: SDL_GPURasterizerState carries cull_mode next to fill_mode and front_face. Cull mode is pipeline state.

The one default in fill_style that is not “correct”

Lesson 3.2 set a rule for this struct: every field defaults to the correct value, so the short call is the right call and each deliberately-wrong mode must be asked for by name. Culling breaks it, and the break is the point: there is no universally correct cull mode. back is right for a closed mesh and destroys a ground plane. So the default is the safe one — none never removes anything you can see — and choosing otherwise is a decision the caller has to make with knowledge the rasterizer does not have. SDL_GPU lands in the same place: a zero-initialised SDL_GPURasterizerState is SDL_GPU_CULLMODE_NONE.

4.3 One branch, in exactly one place

Now the branch, and the only interesting thing about it is where it goes:

The reorientation two lines later is what makes this position forced rather than chosen: it exists to give the top-left rule a positively-oriented triangle, and it works by destroying the facing. There is exactly one window between the sign being known and the sign being thrown away, and the cull test lives in it.

Note also what is not here: no normal, no dot product, no camera. The rasterizer does not know where the camera is and does not need to — §3.4 proved the projection already folded that information into the sign of the area. This is what “the test we were already computing” buys.

4.4 Why the rasterizer and not the caller

It would be tempting to cull earlier, in collect_triangles, and skip even building the raster_triangle. Real engines do exactly that at a coarser granularity (frustum culling per object, Module 6), so the instinct is sound. Here it is wrong, for two reasons worth separating.

The correctness reason. To cull before the projection you would have to ask the question in view space, and §3.3 measured what that costs: one triangle in six, at this field of view. To ask it correctly in view space you need dot(n, centroid) per triangle — a cross product and a dot product, roughly twenty operations — where the rasterizer gets the same answer from a subtraction it was already doing.

The structural reason. Culling is pipeline state, and the hardware runs it after clipping and the divide, immediately before rasterization. Putting it anywhere else in our software renderer would mean Module 4’s port has to move it, and the whole premise of Stage A is that the port is a rename.

4.5 The demo: four modes and two numbers

U cycles four settings. Three of them are engine::cull_mode; the fourth is the §3.3 bug, kept behind a key so it can be watched rather than described. That is the seventh time this engine has kept a wrong implementation on purpose, and it is now simply the house style.

back_by_forward maps to cull_mode::none at the rasterizer and does its culling up in collect_triangles, in view space — which is not just a convenient place to put it, it is exactly where this bug lives in the codebases that have it. It looks like a sensible early-out:

face_normal.z > 0 is dot(normal, forward) < 0 written out: the camera looks down −z in view space, so its forward axis is (0, 0, −1) and the dot product is just −normal.z. Compact, fast, and wrong.

The answer is stored on every triangle even when the mode is off, so the HUD can report the disagreement live:

That loop calls the same is_front_facing the rasterizer calls. It costs one extra edge function per triangle and it means the counter cannot drift away from the behaviour it is counting — which is the whole reason §4.1 gave the rule a name.

The second number is the one that keeps culling honest. Culling is an optimisation, so the claim to test is not “does it look better” but “does it look identical” — so the demo renders the scene a second time with culling off and counts the pixels that differ. On a closed mesh that reads at most a few dozen (§3.7); on the ground plane, with back selected, it reads in the thousands and the floor is missing.

4.6 Telling the demo which meshes are closed

The last piece is a single bool, and it is the beginning of a much later idea:

The demo only warns when culling is on and the scene contains an open surface — it does not switch modes per object. That restraint is deliberate and it is the honest lesson: the whole scene is drawn in one batch with one fill_style, and two cull modes would mean two batches. Which is precisely why a real engine hangs this flag on the material and sorts its draw calls by pipeline state. We will build that in Module 6; today it is a bool and a warning, and knowing why it cannot yet be more is the point.

Checkpoint — six things to verify
  1. The 29 goes to 0. On the solids scene with F anywhere except wireframe, the painter-versus-z-buffer counter reads 29 with culling off and 0 with U on back. This is Lesson 3.1’s debt, settled, and it is the single most satisfying keypress in Module 3.
  2. About half the triangles stop being drawn — 21 of 44 here — and the picture is essentially unchanged. “Essentially” is doing honest work: the vs no culling counter reads a few dozen pixels, all of them on silhouettes (§3.7). It should never read hundreds.
  3. U to front and you are inside the objects. The icosahedron becomes a hollow shell seen from within. Useful as a sanity check that the test is discriminating and not just deleting things.
  4. U to back-by-dot(n,fwd) and orbit. Faces near the edges of the view flicker in and out as the wrong test misjudges them. The dot(n,fwd) misjudges counter tells you how many are wrong right now, even when the mode is off.
  5. Press C to the floor with back selected. The ground is still there from above — and press down to drop the camera below it and it vanishes. That is §3.6’s precondition, violated on purpose. The HUD line turns amber whenever the current scene contains an open surface.
  6. Wireframe is unaffected. F to wireframe and the U line says so: there are no filled faces to cull. Culling is a rasterizer state, and lines do not go through the triangle rasterizer.

7Common Pitfalls & Debugging

Everything vanishes the moment culling is switched on

Cause. The sign is backwards: you are culling front faces and keeping back ones, and the back ones are hidden behind nothing, so you see the inside of every object — which, for a convex solid lit by a flat debug palette, often reads as “empty”.

Why trial-and-error is a trap here. Flipping the comparison fixes it and teaches you nothing, so the next time something else flips the winding — a mirrored transform, an imported mesh, a different viewport convention — you have two unexplained sign flips fighting each other.

Fix, and the test. Derive it (§3.2): the convention is stated in NDC where +y is up, the viewport flips y, and a reflection reverses signed area — so front-facing is edge_function < 0 in framebuffer space. Then check it with one triangle you construct by hand, as verify_34.cpp §A does, rather than by looking at the scene.

Faces flicker in and out near the edges of the screen

Cause. You are culling with dot(normal, camera_forward) in view space. That asks whether the face points against the camera’s axis; the question is whether it points against the ray from the eye to it (§3.3).

Why it survives review. It is exactly correct in the centre of the screen, exactly correct at every point under an orthographic projection, and gets worse toward the edges — which is also where you look least. At the demo’s 55° vertical field of view it misjudges 15.46% of triangles; at a first-person 120° it is 32.43%.

Fix. Cull after the projection, from the sign of the screen-space signed area. It is both correct and cheaper — the projection already did the work (§3.4). If you genuinely must cull in view space, the correct test is dot(n, centroid) < 0, not dot(n, forward) < 0.

Flat things disappear when you walk round them

Cause. Back-face culling applied to geometry that is not a closed surface. Ground planes, walls modelled as single quads, billboards, foliage cards, decals, cloth, paper — all have two sides you are meant to be able to see.

Why it is not a bug in the culler. The culler answered correctly; the precondition was false. “This face points away” only implies “something is in front of it” when the surface encloses a volume.

Fix. Cull mode belongs to the material, not to the renderer. Give open geometry cull_mode::none and accept that it is a separate draw batch, because it is a separate pipeline. The alternative that real content pipelines use is to stop having open geometry: give the wall thickness, and the problem is gone permanently.

One object is inside-out and the rest are fine

Cause. Its model matrix has a negative determinant — almost always a mirror, from scaling one axis by −1 to make a left-handed copy of something. A reflection reverses the winding of every triangle it touches, so a correctly-authored mesh arrives at the rasterizer uniformly backwards.

Why it is confusing. Nothing is wrong with the mesh, nothing is wrong with the culler, and the object looks perfect with culling off. Only the combination fails, so both halves test clean in isolation.

Fix. Detect it and compensate: if det(model) < 0, swap the cull mode for that draw. This is what SDL_GPUFrontFace exists for, and why it is a separate field from cull_mode — the hardware expects you to flip the convention, not the mode. Exercise 3.4.3 builds it.

A mesh looks bitten — scattered faces missing, no pattern

Cause. Inconsistent winding within the mesh. Some triangles are listed counter-clockwise from outside and some clockwise, so culling removes an arbitrary subset that happens to be the wrong way round.

Why hand-authored data drifts into this. Nothing checks it. Without culling and without lighting, a backwards triangle is completely indistinguishable from a correct one, so the error can sit in a mesh for months.

Fix, and the prevention. Validate the geometry rather than the picture — Lesson 2.12 wrote exactly this check, and this is the lesson where you find out why it was worth writing. Lesson 3.5’s OBJ loader will inherit the problem from files it did not author, and will need the same answer.

“Culling made it twice as fast” — or did it?

Cause. Assuming that removing half the triangles halves the time. Measured on the solids scene, culling removes 54.8% of triangles and buys back 31.6% of the frame — 19.95 µs to 13.64 µs.

Why the gap. Back faces of a closed mesh are precisely the triangles the z-buffer was already rejecting on their first depth comparison. They were the cheapest pixels in the frame, not the most expensive. What culling saves is their set-up and one failed test per covered pixel.

Fix. Report the number you measured, not the number you expected. And notice the corollary, which matters for Module 6: culling saves more when the fragment work is expensive, because then every rejected pixel was worth more. Under a real PBR shader the same 54.8% would buy far more than 31.6%.

8Exercises

3.4.1Predict the signwarm-up

Before running anything: our viewport flips y, and we concluded front-facing is edge_function < 0. Suppose you built a viewport that did not flip y — a renderer targeting a bottom-left origin, as OpenGL’s default framebuffer has. Which sign is front-facing then? Now suppose the mesh convention were clockwise-from-outside instead. And both at once?

Solution

Each change flips the answer independently, so: no flip + CCW convention → positive; flip + CCW → negative (ours); no flip + CW → negative; flip + CW → positive. Two independent binary choices, four combinations, and exactly two of them give each sign.

The point of doing this on paper is what it tells you about debugging. If you find yourself flipping a comparison until the picture looks right, you are choosing one of four cells in this table by experiment — and you will get it right for the wrong reason, then be defeated the first time a third factor (a mirrored transform) enters. Write down which space your convention is stated in and derive the rest.

3.4.2Make the wrong test fail on purposewarm-up

Set U to back-by-dot(n,fwd) and find a camera position where the artifact is obvious. Then widen the field of view — change k_scene_fovy from 55° to 110° — and find it again. Watch the misjudges counter while you orbit.

Hint

The wedge in Figure 4 is widest for triangles far from the view axis, so put the object at the edge of the frame rather than the middle. Objects that are close to the camera also sit at large angles even when they are near the centre of the screen.

Solution

At 55° you have to hunt a little — the misjudged triangles are typically 2–6 of 44 and often hidden behind correctly-classified ones. At 110° the count roughly doubles and faces visibly pop in and out along the edges of the frame as you orbit.

The lesson to take is about how bugs get shipped. This one is invisible at a narrow field of view, invisible in the middle of the screen, and completely absent from any orthographic view — which is what a level editor shows you. It becomes obvious only in the wide-FOV first-person gameplay camera, which is the one nobody is looking at while they write the culler.

3.4.3Mirror an objectmedium

Give one of the solids a negative scale on a single axis — out[1].xform.scale = {-1.8f, 0.35f, 0.9f} — and turn back-face culling on. Then fix it properly: compute the determinant of the model matrix and flip that object’s cull mode when it is negative.

Hint

mat3 already has determinant() (Lesson 2.5), and the model matrix’s upper-left 3×3 is what matters — translation cannot change orientation. Note the structural problem you will run into: fill_style is chosen once for the whole batch, so “that object’s cull mode” means splitting the draw.

Solution sketch

The mirrored slab disappears entirely under cull_mode::back, and reappears under front. The fix is one comparison — det < 0 ? front : back — but making it per object forces you to draw the scene as two batches, one per cull mode.

That friction is the real content of the exercise. It is the first time this engine has needed two pipeline states in one frame, and the answer is not to add a parameter but to group draws by state — which is what a material system is, and what Module 6 builds. Note also how the hardware frames it: SDL_GPU would have you flip SDL_GPUFrontFace rather than cull_mode, because the mesh’s convention is what the mirror changed, not your intent about which side to keep.

3.4.4Where does the saving actually come from?medium

§3.5 measured 54.8% of triangles removed and 31.6% of time saved. Find out where the missing 23% went. Instrument the rasterizer to count, separately: triangles set up, pixels visited, depth tests run, depth tests passed. Compare the four numbers with culling on and off, and explain the ratio you measured.

Hint

Predict first, then measure. Which of those four counts do you expect culling to halve, and which do you expect to barely move? The answer is different for the solids scene than it would be for a scene of large overlapping surfaces, and the reason is the same one §3.5 gives.

Solution sketch

Set-up count falls by ~55%; pixels visited falls by a similar amount; depth tests passed barely changes at all, because the back faces were failing almost every test they ran. The time saved tracks set-up and visits, not passes.

Then repeat the measurement with 7 (the uv-checker shading, Lesson 3.2), which makes each surviving pixel more expensive. The proportional saving should rise, because the work you avoided is now worth more per pixel. That is the general shape of it: culling is worth more the more expensive your fragments are, which is why it matters far more in Module 6 than it does today.

3.4.5Two-sided lighting, and why culling is not freeopen

Culling assumes you never want the back of a surface. That is false for a great deal of real content — leaves, grass, cloth, paper, glass. Add a cull_mode::none path that also flips the normal when a face is back-facing, so a single-sided quad can be lit correctly from both sides. You will need Lesson 3.6’s normals, so treat this as a design exercise now and implement it after 3.6.

Solution sketch

The mechanism is one line at the top of the fragment work: if the triangle is back-facing, negate the interpolated normal before shading. Hardware exposes exactly this — HLSL has SV_IsFrontFace, GLSL has gl_FrontFacing — and the reason it is a fragment input rather than something you precompute is that the rasterizer already knows the answer, from the same signed area this lesson has been reading all along.

The interesting part is the accounting. Two-sided rendering costs you the culling saving and a branch per fragment, so it is not something to switch on globally. Which puts you back at §3.6: it is a property of the material. Write down what the material system needs to express — cull mode, two-sidedness, and how they interact — and keep the note for Module 6.

9Recap & Next

A short lesson with an unusually high ratio of consequence to code — one comparison, in one place, and a great deal of care about which comparison and where.

  • Winding is a viewpoint-dependent property, which is exactly what makes it a facing test. It flips when you cross to the other side of a triangle, and nothing else about the triangle changes.
  • The rasterizer was already computing it. Culling reads the sign of the signed area that fill_triangle measures and then deliberately destroys. There is one window between those two events, and the test lives in it.
  • Front-facing is edge_function < 0 — derived from the viewport’s y-flip, not discovered by trying both. The convention is stated in NDC; the test runs in framebuffer space; a reflection separates them.
  • dot(n, camera_forward) is the wrong question, and is wrong on 15% of triangles at our field of view and a third of them at 120°. The right question involves the ray from the eye — and the projection answers it for free, because the projected signed area is −det[a,b,c] / (wawbwc) and Lesson 3.3’s clipping guarantees that divisor is positive.
  • “Half your triangles” is a ceiling, not a rule — a cube shows 2 to 6 of its 12, an icosahedron 7 to 10 of its 20 — and the time saved (31.6%) is smaller than the triangles removed (54.8%), because back faces were the cheap ones.
  • The precondition is closed geometry, it is a property of the object rather than the renderer, and violating it makes flat things vanish.
  • And the debt is paid. 29 px → 0, exactly as Lesson 3.1 predicted, for exactly the reason it guessed — a silhouette tie that culling removes rather than resolves.

The rasterizer is now essentially complete. It clips, it culls, it interpolates correctly, it resolves depth per pixel. What it does not have is anything interesting to draw: every mesh in this course so far has been typed in by hand, and there are exactly three of them.

Lesson 3.5 — A Hand-Rolled OBJ Loader fixes that, and it inherits two debts from this module by name. It brings geometry whose triangle count you cannot raise to paper over a bug (Lesson 3.3 §3.9), and geometry whose winding you did not author and therefore cannot assume (§3.6 above). Parsing is the easy part; trusting the result is the lesson.

10Further Reading

  • Real-Time Rendering, 4th edition — the culling chapter. Back-face culling in its proper place: one of a family of techniques (frustum, portal, occlusion, detail) that all answer “can I avoid drawing this?” at different granularities. Worth reading now for the taxonomy, which Module 6 will fill in.
  • Fabian Giesen, “A trip through the Graphics Pipeline 2011” — the primitive-assembly and clip/cull instalment. Where culling sits in real hardware and why it is fused with clipping into a single stage. Also good on why GPUs rasterise with sub-pixel precision, which is §3.4’s caveat seen from the silicon.
  • The SDL3 headers — SDL_GPURasterizerState. Read the struct. fill_mode, cull_mode, front_face and the depth-bias fields sit together because they are all decisions made once per pipeline, and our fill_style is converging on it deliberately.
  • Jim Blinn, “A Trip Down the Graphics Pipeline: Backface Culling”. Blinn works the same determinant identity §3.4 derives, and is characteristically thorough about the degenerate cases — what happens at exactly zero area, and why that matters more than it looks.
  • Scratchapixel, “Rasterization: a Practical Implementation”. The same ground, and a useful second voice on the relationship between the edge function’s sign and winding.
  • The OpenGL and Vulkan specifications on face culling. Worth skimming one of them purely to see a convention stated with total precision: which space the winding is measured in, what happens to zero-area triangles, and how the front-face setting composes with the cull mode. It is a good model for how to write down a convention you want other people to implement against.
