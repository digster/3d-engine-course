Module 8 — Physics · Lesson 8.13

A Character Controller

Twelve lessons built a simulation that is correct, and the player's character is the one body in a game for which that is the problem. A rigid capsule on 8.10's solver, rotation locked, friction 0.6, stands on a 30° ramp and hangs on a wall mid-jump at any stick speed above 27 cm/s — one friction coefficient, two wishes. It slides 2.91 m after the stick is released, climbs a 20 cm ledge only at 8 m/s, and passes through a 10 cm wall half the time at 42 m/s. So the character leaves the simulation. It becomes a capsule with a position and no mass that asks the world one question, how far can I move before I touch something?, and answers it with a cast. A cast is Newton's method on a convex distance, and it cannot overshoot because each step stops at a separating plane. That holds only if the step is taken from GJK's certified lower bound: stepped from its distance, 377 of 6,049 casts finished inside the skin. Everything else is policy, and each policy has a number. Clip the original motion against every surface, or walk back and forth in an obtuse corner at 58 cm/s. A round bottom climbs a 90.8 mm curb for free and stands 219 mm past an edge. A step-up must carry the capsule up to 90.8 mm across before it lands on a ledge, and with the curb it climbs 0.39 m when told 0.30. A rider carried by velocity spirals off a turntable at eπωh per revolution. A kinematic proxy, steered and never teleported, lets the character push a crate at its own speed. Teleported, the proxy sinks 138 mm into the crate, or walks straight through it once it is asleep. The controller tunnels through nothing at any speed up to 10,000 m/s. Module 8 ends here.

1. The problem: a character that obeys Newton

Every lesson in this module has made something more physically correct. Bodies keep their momentum (8.2), spin the way their mass is spread (8.3), meet at the right points (8.7), push each other exactly as hard as they must (8.9), settle into stacks (8.10), and hang from joints (8.11). A ragdoll falls down a staircase the way a body would, because nothing about it is authored (8.12). The obvious next step is to make the player's character one more body of the same kind: a capsule standing upright, which is what 8.5 said a capsule was for, with its rotation locked so that it cannot tip over. The stick sets its velocity, and the solver does the rest.

So build it, on exactly 8.10's solver: a dynamic capsule 1.8 m tall and 0.3 m in radius, 80 kg, a zero inverse inertia (every engine's "freeze rotation" box), friction 0.6. Then ask it for the five things any designer asks of a character, and measure what it does. §A of this lesson's harness does exactly that, and Figure 1 is the result.

Stand on a 30° ramp. At μ = 0.5 it slides 1.98 m in two seconds, against a predicted 1.97 from g(sin α − μ cos α). That is correct: 8.9 measured the critical slope at atan(μ), 26.6°, and 30° is steeper. Raise μ to 0.6 (tan 30° = 0.577) and it holds.

Jump at a wall and keep pushing into it. Now μ = 0.6 is the problem, because the capsule hangs there. Each step the stick sets its horizontal velocity to v_push and the contact cancels it, so the normal impulse is m·v_push. Coulomb friction may then hold up to μ·m·v_push of vertical momentum, and gravity only adds m·g·h a step. The capsule hangs whenever μ·v_push ≥ g·h: a stick speed of g·h/μ, 0.2725 m/s at 60 Hz. The harness bisects it at 0.2757. Any character pushing into a wall at walking pace sticks to it. Setting μ to zero fixes the wall (it falls at exactly −9.81 m/s after one second) and breaks the ramp. Friction is one number for a pair of materials, and the design wants two: grip on the floor, none on the wall. The same material is floor at one angle and wall at another, so no material table can hold both.

Let go of the stick at 6 m/s. It slides 2.91 m. The continuous v²/(2μg) is 3.06 m, and the sum of semi-implicit Euler's steps, taken from where the stick let go, is 2.9098 m to four decimals. Momentum is conserved, and the design wants a stop.

Walk into a ledge. The round bottom meets a ledge's edge with a tilted normal, and the solver turns part of the stick into lift. How much depends on how hard the capsule is driven. A 10 cm ledge goes up at 1 m/s but not at 0.5. A 20 cm ledge goes up at 8 m/s and at nothing slower, and 30 cm never does. Whether a staircase can be climbed depends on speed, which no level designer can work with.

Dash into a wall. 8.10 §6 measured that a body arriving at a surface is first sampled up to one step's travel deep, uniformly. At 42 m/s a step is 0.7 m, and a 10 cm wall is passed exactly half the time (§9 derives the odds and measures them).

None of those red cells is a bug in 8.10's solver; each is the solver being right. A physics engine exists to conserve momentum, to make friction one number, and to answer questions about instants, and those are the three properties the design needs to be false. The fix is not a better solver. It is to take the character out of the simulation entirely, and that is the rest of the lesson.

2. Building intuition: a question, not a body

A controller does not simulate a character. It keeps a position and, every step, asks the world one question: if this capsule moved by d, how far would it get before it came within a centimetre of something, and what would it be touching? That question has a name, a shape cast (a sweep), and a precise answer: a fraction t of the move, a surface normal, and a body. Everything else a controller does is policy, a decision about what to do with the answer.

  • If it hit a wall, keep the part of the move that runs along the wall (collide and slide, §4).
  • If it hit ground, which is a normal within 45° of up, lay the move along it rather than stopping; and never let a steeper surface lift the character (slopes, §5).
  • After the move, look a little way down. If there is ground, the character is grounded, and its normal and its body are worth keeping (§6).
  • If it was grounded last step and the ground has dropped away, snap down onto it rather than stepping off into the air (§7).
  • If a wall is shorter than a stair, step up it: up, across, down (§8).
  • If the ground is moving, move with it, by exactly what it did (§10).

Figure 2 is that list as move_character runs it: five stages, each labelled with the section that measures it, over the one query they all share. The move is cut into a sideways pass and a vertical pass because walls and floors want opposite treatment. A wall should stop a sideways move and never a fall. A floor should stop a fall and never a sideways move. Doing both in one collide-and-slide is the classic way a character ends up creeping downhill while standing still, and §5 measures the creep at g·h·sin α.

Who owns what. 8.12's rule was that every body has exactly one owner at a time. The controller owns the character's position, and the solver owns every other body. The two have to meet, or the character would walk through crates and crates would fall through the character, and they meet through a proxy. The proxy is a kinematic capsule in the body_world, placed where the character is and steered there by velocity, the chord of 8.12, never teleported. To the solver it is an infinitely heavy moving body. A crate it walks into is pushed at the speed the character is really moving, and keeps that speed when the character stops. Light dynamic bodies (40 kg or less) are pushable: the controller's sideways moves ignore them, and the proxy shoves them. Heavier ones are walls. §11 measures what teleporting the proxy does instead, and it is worse than the obvious answer.

When. Each fixed step runs in three parts, in this order. Steer the proxy to where the controller left the character. Run the physics step. Then move the character against the world as the step left it. Running the controller after the step means it sees every crate and platform where it now is. It also means the one thing it cannot see in the world, how far the platform under it just travelled, is exactly readable from that platform's velocity and the integrator that moved it (§10).

Design, not Newton. The displacement handed to move_character is the game's, and so is everything that produces it: acceleration curves, air control, and the rule that a grounded character does not fall. A jump is the clearest example. A designer names a height, not a force, and the launch speed is arithmetic: v₀ = √(2gH). Asked for 1.20 m, though, the character rises 1.1599 m. Semi-implicit Euler (8.1) moves each step at the velocity it ends with, so the first step already moves at v₀ − g·h, and the apex is the discrete sum v₀²/2g − v₀h/2 = 1.1596. Four centimetres is not a physics error. It is a design parameter measured in the units the integrator actually works in, and a game that cares aims for the height the steps give.

3. The one query: a cast is Newton's method

Every query Module 8 has built so far is about an instant. Are these two shapes touching now (8.4)? How far apart are they now (8.5)? How deep are they now (8.6)? A cast asks about an interval: the mover travels along d, and the answer is the first fraction t of that trip at which it comes within skin of the obstacle. Nothing is sampled at a step boundary, so nothing is skipped. That is exactly what Lesson 1.8's swept test did for a ball and a paddle. The question is how to answer it for any pair of convex shapes, when all we can compute is a distance at an instant.

Newton, on the right function

Write the question as a function. Let f(t) be the distance between the mover, displaced by t·d, and the obstacle. 8.5's GJK computes f at any t we like, and hands back something more: the unit direction n from the mover's closest point to the obstacle's. Nudge the mover a little further along d and the gap closes at the rate the motion points along that direction. Whatever part of the motion runs across n slides the closest points past each other without bringing them nearer:

\[ f'(t) \;=\; -\,d\cdot n \] f'(t) = -(d . n): the gap closes at the motion's component along the closest-point direction

We want the first root of f(t) − skin, and we have the function and its slope. The tool for that is Newton's method: stand at t, follow the tangent line down to the target, and step there.

\[ t \;\leftarrow\; t \;+\; \frac{f(t) - \text{skin}}{d\cdot n} \] t <- t + (f(t) - skin) / (d . n): the gap left, over the speed it is closing at

That is conservative advancement, the name the collision literature gives it. Read the fraction aloud and it is Lesson 1.8's swept test: the gap, over the speed at which it is closing. 1.8 computed it once, for a flat paddle face. Here we compute it again from wherever that lands, until it stops moving.

Worked, with real numbers. A ball of radius 0.5 at the origin moves along d = (4, 0) toward another of radius 0.5 at (3, 0.6). The gap between them is f(t) = |(4t − 3, −0.6)| − 1, and they touch at (4t − 3)² = 1 − 0.36, so 4t − 3 = −0.8 and t = 0.55 exactly. Start at t₀ = 0. The centres are √(9 + 0.36) = 3.0594 apart, so the gap is 2.0594. The direction between them is (3, 0.6)/3.0594 = (0.9806, 0.1961), and the motion closes it at d·n = 4 × 0.9806 = 3.9223. One step goes to t₁ = 2.0594/3.9223 = 0.5250. There the gap is 0.0815 and the closing speed 3.3280, so t₂ = 0.5250 + 0.0245 = 0.5495. Then t₃ = 0.5499998. The errors run 0.55, 0.025, 0.00046, 1.9 × 10⁻⁷, each roughly the square of the one before: quadratic convergence. The ratio errn+1/errn² tends to f''/2|f'| = 5.76/6.4 = 0.9 at the root. The harness computes the table by hand in double precision and reads 0.74, 0.8966, 0.8993.

Why it cannot overshoot, twice

Newton's method is usually a gamble: step along the tangent, and the true function may already have crossed zero before the tangent does. A cast that stepped through a wall would be worse than no cast at all. There are two reasons it cannot happen here. One says how fast the steps converge, and the other why each one is safe.

The distance is convex. Move the obstacle into the mover's frame: the gap is the distance from the point −t·d to 8.5's Minkowski difference, which is a fixed convex set. The distance to a convex set is a convex function of the point, and the point moves on a straight line, so f is convex in t. A convex function lies above every one of its tangent lines:

\[ f(t + \Delta) \;\ge\; f(t) + f'(t)\,\Delta \;=\; \text{skin} \quad\text{at the Newton step } \Delta \] f(t + step) >= f(t) + f'(t) step = skin: a convex function is above its tangent, so the step lands short

Every step therefore lands short of the target. The iterates climb monotonically toward the first contact from below, and never jump past it. Figure 3's right panel is that picture: the green tangents stop on the axis, and the blue curve has not reached it yet.

GJK's direction is a separating plane. The second argument is stronger, and it is the one the code relies on. Take any unit direction n and the two supporting planes normal to it, one touching the mover and one the obstacle. The slab between them is empty, so its width is a lower bound on the gap. That is 8.4's separating-axis theorem. Translate the mover by t·d and its supporting plane moves by exactly t·(d·n), so the slab narrows at exactly d·n per unit t, linearly, for any n. The Newton step goes to where that slab is skin wide, and the true gap cannot be smaller than the slab. So the step is safe even if GJK's n is not quite the true normal, which at a centimetre it often is not (§6). It is the separating axis, turned into a time. The same argument proves the cast's other exit: if d·n ≤ 0 the slab never narrows, and the path is clear.

That argument has one condition, and it is not optional: the step must be taken from the slab's width, not from GJK's distance. gjk_result::distance is the length between two witness points, two real points on two real shapes, and so it is an upper bound on the gap. 8.5 carried both bounds and called the difference slack. The first draft of cast.cpp stepped from distance, and §B's control keeps a private copy of it. Over ten thousand random casts, it finished past the reference crossing in 377 of 6,049 hits, up to 0.17 mm inside the skin. That is the Newton step overshooting by exactly GJK's slack. Stepped from 8.5's certify(...).lower, the same casts finish past the reference none of the time; the worst lands 9 × 10⁻⁷ m inside, which is the reference's own resolution. The convexity argument had promised no overshoot. It was true of the exact distance and false of the number the code was using.

How it behaves, over ten thousand casts

§B sweeps a capsule of random size and orientation against a random box, ball or capsule, in a random direction, 9,996 times. Each result is checked against a reference that uses no Newton at all: a golden-section search for the minimum of the convex f, then bisection to the skin, with GJK run at a thousandth of its tolerance. The cast misses no hit the reference finds and finishes after no crossing. It takes 2.75 Newton steps on average and 7 at worst, and never reaches its 32-step cap. Three passes that graze the skin within the tolerance count as hits, which is the safe side.

Sixty-seven hits stopped early by more than the tolerance, by up to 1.81 mm. Each one is explained by the cast's own reported bound, and each is the same thing: at a centimetre's gap between metre-sized shapes, GJK stalls (8.5 §F.5), and its lower bound can sit up to 2.08 mm below the truth. A tighter GJK tolerance does not help, because GJK stops on float rounding before the tolerance matters. It is the price of the rigour, and it is paid on the safe side: short of the skin, never past it.

1.8's swept test was always the first Newton step. Against a flat face f is a straight line, so the tangent is the function and the first step lands exactly. A ball falling at an angle onto a floor stops at t = 0.4966656 (exact 0.4966667) after two GJK calls, the second of which only confirms the first. Curved shapes take the other two or three.

One obstacle at a time

A world has more than one obstacle, and it is tempting to cast against all of them at once: take the nearest distance as f and step. That is wrong, and Figure 4 shows why. The minimum of two convex functions is not convex, and a separating plane separates one pair. Pass a ball at a grazing angle with a wall further on: the nearer ball's plane is almost parallel to the motion, so its step is long, and it says nothing at all about the wall. §B built 20,000 such passes and stepped on the union. It went through the wall 11,194 times. One cast per obstacle, keeping the smallest t, went through it none of the time. The controller's sweep is exactly that, after a box test that throws away anything the move's swept bounds cannot reach.

Why divide by the closing speed. There is another conservative step, and ray marchers use it on signed-distance fields: sphere tracing. It moves along d by the gap itself (Δt = gap/|d|), which is safe for any shape, because nothing is nearer than the gap. Dividing by |d| instead of by d·n is dividing by something larger, so every step is shorter than Newton's, and at a grazing angle much shorter. To reach a face at 1° it takes 237 steps where the cast takes 2. Sphere tracing pays for needing no convexity; a cast is always against one convex obstacle, so it does not have to.

Why a skin

The cast stops skin short of contact, and character_config::skin is 1 cm. So the character stands a centimetre above the floor, and a game lowers the drawn mesh by that much. Why not zero? The first answer is the obvious one. A cast that stops exactly at contact leaves two shapes touching, and the next question, asked from there, is a question GJK answers as "intersecting" whenever the gap is inside its own contact margin, with no direction to report. §B's last table measures it: cast, then slide from where the cast stopped, 4,000 times. With no skin, 2.1% of the slides start inside. That is the prediction, but it is not the worst of it.

With no skin, the cast often cannot finish. Against a flat face Newton lands on the contact in one step, exactly on it, and GJK calls that intersecting. The cast then has to fall back to the last iterate it could measure, which is often the start. With no skin, casts stopped 134 mm short on average and 2.3 m at worst. In the worked example it is 1.93 mm short, one Newton step. And a skin thinner than GJK's margin is worse than none. At 0.1 mm, 7.3% of slides start inside, because the cast now finishes reliably and it finishes inside the margin. At a millimetre or more, nothing starts inside, and the cast lands on the skin to the tolerance: t = 0.5468830 against an exact 0.5468837. The engine uses a centimetre, ten times the working minimum, because §6 will need GJK's direction to be good and GJK is most accurate when the gap is not tiny.

4. Collide and slide

A cast answers "how far?" The controller still has to decide what to do with the rest of the move. If a character runs diagonally into a wall, the part of the motion that runs into the wall is gone, and the part that runs along it should still happen. That is the projection off the surface, applied only if the motion is going into it:

\[ v' \;=\; v - \min(0,\; v\cdot n)\;n \] v' = v - min(0, v . n) n: remove the part of v that points into the surface

Then cast what is left, and repeat. One wall is easy. Two walls make a corner, and a corner is where every character controller's first draft breaks, this one's included.

Projecting onto one wall can point the motion into the other. The motion that respects both walls lies along their crease, the line both planes contain, n₁ × n₂, and three walls pin the character completely. That gives Quake's rule, clip_to_planes: try each surface alone, and use the result if it goes into none of the others. Otherwise try each pair's crease. Otherwise stop. The rule is well known. What it is fed turns out to matter just as much, and it is easy to get wrong.

Clip the original, not the remainder. Figure 5 follows one move into a 120° corner, with the stick 12° off the bisector. The move hits wall A first and slides along it, toward the corner. The obvious loop then clips what was left, a motion already bent by A, against the planes it knows. Against A alone that remainder is unchanged and goes into B; against B alone it points back out along B, and does not go into A. So the check passes, and the character walks back out of the corner. Next step it walks in again. Quake 1's SV_FlyMove clips the original velocity against every plane instead. The original, clipped against A, goes into B; clipped against B, it goes into A; their crease is vertical, and a horizontal move has no part along it. The character stops, which is what a player pushing into a corner expects.

§C measures all three rules in 60°, 90° and 120° corners, pressing in for five seconds and watching the last one. Clipping the remainder, against the last plane or every plane, walks back and forth in the obtuse corner at 572–575 mm/s. The remainder against the last plane also runs out of slides on every one of 60 moves in the acute corner, burning four casts to go nowhere. Clipping the original is still in every corner. The first draft of character.cpp clipped the remainder against every plane; the plane list alone was not the fix. character_config::clip_rule keeps all three, so §C can keep measuring them.

Parallel is not approaching. One more detail makes a controller slide at all. A character running along a wall is exactly skin from it, and its motion is exactly parallel to it. Rounding gives the cast a closing speed of a few parts in 10⁸, and the gap is already within tolerance of the skin, so the cast reports a hit at t = 0. §C ran a character along a wall at 3 m/s with that check at zero, and it moved at 0.0000 m/s, 120 stalled steps of 120. cast_config::min_approach treats a closing speed below 10⁻⁴ of the motion's length as not closing, and it runs at 3.0000 m/s. The separating-plane argument bounds what the threshold can cost: the gap can close by at most min_approach·|d| more, 5 µm on a 5 cm step.

5. Slopes

A controller decides what a surface is from one number, its normal's height. A surface whose normal is within max_slope of straight up is walkable, and anything steeper is a wall, whatever it is made of:

\[ \text{walkable}(n) \;\iff\; n_y \;\ge\; \cos\theta_{\max} \] walkable(n) if and only if n.y >= cos(max_slope); the default max_slope is 45 degrees

This is the split §1 could not make with friction. A walkable slope is walked at full speed and stood on without sliding, and a steeper one cannot be climbed at all. The two regimes meet at one angle the designer chose, 45° by default, instead of at atan(μ). Everything in this section is the two halves of that one decision.

Laying a step along the ground

The stick produces a horizontal step d. On a 30° ramp, the obvious thing is to slide it along the ramp as §4 would slide it along a wall, d − (d·n)n. But that projection keeps only cos²α of the horizontal speed, so a character walking at 3 m/s crosses the map at 2.25 m/s up any 30° ramp. The controller instead wants the vector that has d's horizontal part and lies in the ground plane. Add a vertical amount λ to d and demand that the result be perpendicular to n: (d + λŷ)·n = 0, so λ = −(d·n)/n_y:

\[ \operatorname{along\_ground}(d,\,n) \;=\; d \;-\; \hat{y}\,\frac{d\cdot n}{n_y} \] along_ground(d, n) = d - y-hat (d . n)/n.y: d's horizontal part, and the rise that keeps it in the plane

Worked, with real numbers. At 3 m/s and 60 Hz, d = (0.05, 0) per step. A 30° ramp rising toward +x has n = (−0.5, 0.8660), so d·n = −0.025 and λ = 0.025/0.8660 = 0.028868. The step is (0.05, 0.028868): rise over run is exactly tan 30° = 0.5774, and its length is 0.05/cos 30°, 3.4641 m/s along the ramp. §D walks ramps of 10° to 40° and reads 3.0000 m/s horizontally on every one, rising 0.1763, 0.3640, 0.5773 and 0.8391 per metre against their tangents. Keeping the speed along the slope instead, a Mario-style slow-down, is a one-line change and just as legitimate. Both are design, and neither is physics.

The sideways pass keeps laying the step along whatever walkable ground it meets: flat, then a ramp's foot, then the ramp, then a crest. It re-lays after every wall it clips against too, because a clip turns the horizontal direction and the rise must turn with it. Walls are clipped with their normals flattened, their vertical part removed and renormalised, so that sliding along something too steep to stand on can move a character sideways but never up.

That flattening turned out to matter less than it looks, and only in the air. On the ground, a slide up a 50° face is generated, but re-laying the step along the floor throws its climb away: pressed into the slope with and without flattening, the character's centre stays at its resting point at the foot, −0.145, either way. In the air nothing re-lays the step. A character hopping 0.5 m at a time into a 50° slope gets 0.638 m into it unflattened, against 0.257 flattened, the extra 0.38 m being slides up the face (60°: 0.328 against 0.099). flatten_walls is the fix for the one case the floor cannot catch.

Standing still

A grounded character still falls every step. The game zeroes its vertical velocity on landing, adds one step of gravity (−g·h), and hands the controller a drop of g·h², 2.7 mm at 60 Hz, which the vertical pass stops on the ground. The mistake is to slide that drop along the ground the way §4 slides a sideways move along a wall. A drop of g·h² slid along a slope of angle α has a downhill part g·h²·sin α, every step, so a character told to stand still creeps downhill at

\[ v_{\text{creep}} \;=\; g\,h\,\sin\alpha \] creep speed = g h sin(alpha): 8.2 cm/s on a 30-degree slope at 60 Hz

— 8.18 cm/s on 30°, five metres a minute. §D measures it by turning stop_on_ground off: 0.028389, 0.055916, 0.081746 and 0.105092 m/s on 10°, 20°, 30° and 40°, against predictions of 0.028391, 0.055920, 0.081750 and 0.105096. With it on, all four stand at 0.000000. That is why the vertical pass is separate from the sideways one. A floor stops a fall, and sliding is for walls.

Too steep to stand on. On a surface that is not walkable, the vertical pass does slide the fall along it. That is the intent: a character on a 60° slope should slide off it. And the projection turns out to be the physics. The game's vertical speed grows as g·t while the character is airborne, and a fall of v_y·h slid along the slope moves it |v_y|·h·sin α along the slope, so its speed along the slope is g·t·sin α. That is the frictionless incline, with no force anywhere in the code. After half a second on 60° the harness reads 4.3897 m/s against g·t·sin α = 4.3895.

6. Ground, and the round bottom

After every move, the controller looks down. It casts two skins' width below where it stands; if that finds walkable ground, the character is grounded. It keeps the ground's normal, its contact point and its body index, which the carry (§10) and the next step's along_ground both read. It is one more cast, and it is where the capsule's shape starts to matter.

A capsule has a round bottom. When it meets an edge, a curb or the lip of a stair, the contact normal is not the normal of any surface in the level. It is the direction from the edge to the centre of the capsule's bottom sphere, and it tilts as the capsule moves. Figure 7 draws the three consequences, and all three come from one right triangle whose hypotenuse is r + skin, the radius the casts actually see, because every cast stops a skin short.

A free curb. Walk into a curb of height s. The bottom sphere's centre is r + skin above the floor, and it meets the curb's edge s up, so the contact normal has n_y = (r + skin − s)/(r + skin). While that is walkable the edge is ground, a little ramp, and the sideways pass lays the step along it and rolls the capsule up and over with no step-up at all:

\[ s_{\text{free}} \;=\; (r + \text{skin})\,(1 - \cos\theta_{\max}) \] the tallest curb climbed with no step-up = (r + skin)(1 - cos max_slope) = 0.31 x 0.2929 = 90.8 mm

With r = 0.3, a centimetre of skin and 45°, that is 0.31 × 0.29289 = 90.80 mm. §E turns the step-up off and bisects the tallest curb the character walks onto: 91.83 mm.

An overhang. Walk off an edge slowly. Once the sphere's centre is past it, the only thing under the capsule is the edge, and the normal tilts by asin(x/(r + skin)) with the overhang x. The character stays grounded until that tilt passes 45°, at x = (r + skin)·sin θ = 219.20 mm: nearly a quarter of a metre of standing on nothing, which a player sees and which §7 has to handle. Placed on the edge and bisected, the harness reads 219.79 mm. Walking off at 5 mm a step, it loses the ground at 215.0 mm, which is the same number rounded to the step.

The normal it reports. Figure 7's right panel sets six overhangs and reads the tilt of the cast's normal against the arcsine. They agree to within 0.189°, and that residue is GJK's. A curved shape against an edge a centimetre away is exactly where 8.5's GJK stalls, and its direction is then good to about 0.2°. At the 45° threshold, 0.2° moves the free curb by 0.219 × 0.0035 ≈ 0.8 mm, which is the millimetre between 91.83 and 90.80.

A tighter tolerance is not the fix, and the reason is the size of the level. The natural response to "GJK's answer is loose at a centimetre" is to tighten GJK. §E4 does, to 10⁻⁷, and nothing changes in the fourth decimal, because GJK is stopping on float rounding long before its tolerance matters. What does change the answer is the size of the obstacle. §3's cast steps from the width of a slab along GJK's direction. If that direction is off by a small angle ε, the slab's far face is set by the obstacle's farthest corner, and the bound is short by about the obstacle's size times ε. Walking down a 30° ramp and landing by a vertical cast every step, the character stands 10.00–10.07 mm from a ramp 6 m long and 10.02–12.32 mm from one 60 m long, at either tolerance. That is 8.4 §11's finding for a third time. There the scale that spoiled an answer was the distance to the world's origin, in 8.5 it was the size of the shapes relative to the gap, and here it is the size of the level geometry. Author collision geometry in pieces of sensible size, and the controller is precise to a tenth of a millimetre.

7. Snapping to the ground

A character walking down anything that is not flat is continually walking off the ground. Every step the ground drops away, and the only question is whether the controller finds it again before the player sees a bounce. There are three ways the ground drops, and they fail at different speeds.

A uniform slope. Move the character horizontally and let gravity bring it down, the way a controller without along_ground does. In one step the slope drops v·h·tan α. The character drops g·h² in the vertical pass, and the ground probe reaches two skins further. It stays grounded only while the probe can still find the slope:

\[ v \;\le\; \frac{g\,h^2 + 2\,\text{skin}}{h\,\tan\alpha} \] it stays on a uniform slope while v <= (g h^2 + 2 skin)/(h tan alpha): 2.3617 m/s on 30 degrees at 60 Hz

That is 2.3617 m/s on 30° at 60 Hz. With no probe it would be g·h/tan α, 28 cm/s, which is slower than a walk. §F bisects the speed at which the character first leaves the slope: 2.3561 m/s on a ramp 8 m long. On a 60 m ramp it leaves at 2.2537, 4.6% early, because §6's looser certificate stands the character up to 2.3 mm higher and that comes out of the probe's reach. Above the onset it spends most of every second in the air, 57 frames of 60 at 3 m/s. Laying the step along the ground fixes a uniform slope completely: at every speed tested, up to 6 m/s, zero frames in the air.

A crest. Where the flat meets a downhill, the step was laid along the flat, and past the edge there is nothing under it. It turns out the round bottom rolls over a crest for free, up to a point. The first step past the edge carries the sphere's centre v·h beyond it, and it only has to drop (r+s) − √((r+s)² − (v·h)²) to rest on the edge again, with a walkable tilt. While that drop is within the probe's reach the character never leaves the ground:

\[ v\,h \;\le\; \sqrt{(r+s)^2 - (r + s - \text{reach})^2} \;=\; 0.1165\ \text{m} \] it rolls over a crest while v h <= sqrt((r+s)^2 - (r+s-reach)^2) = 0.1165 m, v < 6.99 m/s

which is below 6.99 m/s. The section was written to show a crest launching the character like a projectile at a jog. It did not: at 3 and 6 m/s, with no snap at all, zero frames in the air. At 7.5, 10 and 14 m/s it does fly: 0.833, 1.133 and 1.617 s against a projectile's 2v·tan α/g of 0.883, 1.177 and 1.648. Each flight is a step or three short, because the character leaves the edge a step past the crest, where the slope is already below it.

A stair, going down. This is the case the snap was designed for, and the round bottom makes it harder. Walk off a stair's edge and, for the first 219 mm of overhang, the capsule is still standing on that edge (§6). After that it is resting on it, not grounded, with a contact normal too steep to stand on. A snap that simply casts down finds the edge first, at t = 0, and gives up. So the snap rolls off first: sideways, away from the edge, by exactly (r + s) − overhang, which is less than (r + s)(1 − sin θ) = 90.8 mm whenever the edge was too steep to stand on. Then it casts straight down.

The first draft slid down the edge's tangent plane instead. That rolls off too, but a straight slide along a tangent overshoots a round edge badly: the character lurched 175 mm forward on some steps. Rolled off by the exact amount, eight 18 cm stairs are walked down at 2 m/s in nine snaps, with zero frames in the air, and the worst extra forward movement in one step is 77.7 mm. Without the snap the same staircase is eight small falls, 54 frames in the air. The same 90.8 mm reappears in §8, going up.

A snap is also a decision about ledges. snap_distance is how far down the controller will look for ground, and so it is also the height of the drop a character is pulled down in one step rather than falling. It defaults to 0.35 m. Walking off a 0.30 m ledge, the character never leaves the ground (the largest single snap is 0.196 m, the rest taken by the roll and the next step); off a 0.50 m one it falls for 16 frames. That is the right behaviour for stairs and kerbs, and a visible jerk for anything designed as a drop. Set it from the tallest stair the level has, not from a tolerance. The snap never runs while the character is rising, so a jump is never pulled back down.

8. Stepping up

A stair is a wall 18 cm tall, and walls stop the sideways pass. The step-up turns a short wall into a stair. When the sideways pass hits a wall while the character is grounded, it tries, once per move, three casts in a row: up by step_height (or as far as the ceiling allows), across by the rest of the move, and down by as much as it rose. The step is kept only if the down cast lands on walkable ground, higher than the start and no higher than step_height. Otherwise it never happened, and the pass slides along the wall as usual.

That much is in every character controller. The round bottom adds a condition that is easy to miss and fatal to leave out. At the riser, the bottom sphere's centre is short of the ledge's edge: by √((r+s)² − (r+s − riser)²) for a riser lower than r + skin, and by the full r + skin for anything taller. After rising and moving across by f, the down cast lands on the edge, with the tilted normal of §6. The landing is walkable only if the centre is now within (r+s)·sin θ of the edge. So f must be at least the distance short of the edge, less that, and for any riser at least as tall as r + skin:

\[ f_{\min} \;=\; (r + \text{skin})\,(1 - \sin\theta_{\max}) \;=\; 0.31 \times 0.2929 \;=\; 90.8\ \text{mm} \] the least forward move that lands walkable on a tall ledge = (r + skin)(1 - sin max_slope) = 90.8 mm

At 2 m/s a step is 33.3 mm across. A step-up that moved across only by the rest of the move would land on the edge at a tilt of more than 45° every time, be refused every time, and leave the character standing at the foot of the staircase forever. §G bisects the least forward move that lands walkable at three risers and finds 46.15, 84.67 and 90.89 mm, against a prediction of 46.32, 84.94 and 90.80 from the distance short of the edge less (r+s)·sin θ. The step-up therefore moves across by max(rest of move, f_min). That is a lurch forward on the frame the step is taken, and it is the price of a round bottom; the same 90.8 mm rolled the capsule off a stair edge in §7, going down. Up eight 18 cm stairs at 2 m/s: eight step-ups, zero frames in the air, at the top in 1.267 s, and the worst extra forward movement in one step is 75.9 mm.

The tallest ledge is not step_height. §G bisects the tallest ledge the character climbs with step_height = 0.30 and gets 0.39092 m. After rising 0.30, the capsule's round bottom rolls over whatever of the ledge is left, exactly as it rolled over §6's curb. The two add: 0.30 + 90.80 mm = 0.39080 m. If a level designer's number is the ledge, author step_height as that number less the free curb.

A step that lands on a slope is refused, or a staircase of small steps would climb anything. Pressed into a 60° slope for two seconds with the step-up on, the character takes zero steps and rises zero: every down cast lands on the slope itself, with its own 60° normal. Set that against §1's rigid capsule, which climbed a 20 cm ledge only at 8 m/s. The controller climbs 18 cm, 30 cm and anything up to 39 cm at walking pace, at a jog, at a run, and at a crawl. What climbs is decided by the ledge, and not by the speed.

9. Tunnelling, revisited

Lesson 1.8's ball passed straight through its paddle at speed, and the swept test was the fix. Eight modules later the rigid capsule of §1 has the same problem, and now we can say exactly how often. 8.10 §6 found that a body is first sampled overlapping a surface at a depth d spread uniformly on [0, v·h], because it crossed the surface at an unknown moment during the step. Against a wall of thickness w, EPA's minimum translation pushes the capsule back out the near side only while its centre has not passed the wall's middle, d < w/2 + r. Past that, the shortest way out is forwards. So the probability of going through is

\[ P_{\text{through}} \;=\; \max\!\left(0,\; 1 - \frac{w/2 + r}{v\,h}\right) \] P(through) = max(0, 1 - (w/2 + r)/(v h)): zero below v = (w/2 + r)/h, 21 m/s for a 10 cm wall

— zero below (w/2 + r)/h, 21 m/s for a 10 cm wall and a 30 cm capsule. §H samples the phase, 200 evenly spaced starting offsets across one step of travel, not the speed. That is 8.11's lesson: the quantity depends on where the event falls inside a step, so that is what must be uniform. It measures 15.5%, 30.0%, 50.0%, 65.0% and 79.0% at 25, 30, 42, 60 and 100 m/s, against 16.0, 30.0, 50.0, 65.0 and 79.0.

The controller never samples an instant. Its one question is about the whole interval, so a wall is either in the swept volume or it is not. §H dashes it at a 1 cm wall at 10, 100, 1,000 and 10,000 m/s, which is 167 m in one step. It passes none of them, and stops 10.011, 10.051, 10.004 and 10.004 mm from the face: the skin, to the tolerance. The character does not need continuous collision detection added on. The cast is continuous collision detection, for the one body that needed it most. The crates it kicks still tunnel at 8.10's odds; speculative contacts remain open defect 3 of the module.

10. Standing on things that move

A lift rises, a turntable turns, a boat rocks. A character standing on one must move with it, and a rigid body would do that for free, through friction, which is exactly the property §1 threw away. So the controller carries the character itself. It knows which body it is standing on (the ground probe kept the index), and it runs after the physics step. So the question is exact: what did that body just do?

A kinematic body is moved by integrate_positions, and 8.12's lesson was that anything reading a body's motion must invert the integrator the body was actually moved with. Its centre travelled v·h. It turned about its old centre by the rotation advance_orientation performs, which for the default linearised rule is normalise(1, ½hω) and not the axis-angle |ω|h. step_motion reads both back, and the carry applies them to the character as a transform:

\[ p' \;=\; c + v\,h + R_{\Delta}\,(p - c) \] p' = c + v h + R(p - c): the platform's step, applied to the rider — c the platform's old centre, R its step's rotation

The carry is swept like any other move (a platform can carry you into a wall), and its rotation is handed back so the game can turn the character's facing with the platform. On a turntable at 1 and 2 rad/s, a rider 1.5 m out is at 1.50000 m after one revolution, lagging the plate by 0.000°, and facing exactly where the plate has turned it.

The tempting carry is by velocity: add the point velocity of the ground under the rider, v + ω × r, times h. It is exact for a lift that only translates. Both rules keep a rider on a lift moving (2, 1.5, 0) m/s within 10 µm for two seconds, and not carrying at all leaves it 5.1 m behind. On a turntable it is wrong, and 8.11 already said why. A point velocity is tangent to the circle the rider is on, so each step moves the rider along a tangent, off the circle:

\[ |r|^2 \;\leftarrow\; |r|^2\,\bigl(1 + (\omega h)^2\bigr) \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \text{growth per revolution} \;=\; \bigl(1 + (\omega h)^2\bigr)^{N/2} \;\approx\; e^{\pi\omega h} \] |r|^2 grows by 1 + (w h)^2 a step, so the radius grows by about e^(pi w h) a revolution: 5.4% at 1 rad/s, 11.1% at 2

Worked, with real numbers. At ω = 2 rad/s and 60 Hz, ωh = 1/30, so each step multiplies the radius by √(1 + 1/900) = 1.000555. One linearised step turns the plate by 2·atan(1/60) = 0.033330 rad, so a revolution is 2π/0.033330 = 188.51 steps, and 1.000555188.51 = 1.11035. The harness turns the plate for 189 whole steps and measures 1.11065, which is that number times one half-step more. At 1 rad/s, 1.05375 measured and 1.05375 predicted. A rider 1.5 m out gains 17 cm a revolution and is thrown off the plate in a few turns. It is 8.11's velocity joint again: a velocity is a tangent, and a tangent leaves a circle.

11. One owner: pushing, and being pushed

The character has left the simulation, and the crates have not. They meet through the proxy of §2: a kinematic capsule in the body_world, which steer_proxy gives the chord velocity from where it is to where the controller left the character. The next physics step lands it there exactly, and that is 8.12's steer, for one body. A dynamic body of 40 kg or less is pushable: the controller's sideways moves ignore it, the character walks into it, and the proxy, a moving body of infinite mass, shoves it out of the way. §J pushes a 20 kg crate at 2 m/s for a second and a half and then lets it go.

Steered, the crate moves at 2.0000 m/s with a velocity of 2.0000 m/s: the number anything reading it (friction, sound, damage, a network) will see is the truth. The proxy overlaps it by exactly 5.00 mm, which is 8.10's penetration slop. Released, it coasts 0.3562 m. That is the discrete v²/(2μg) of 0.3233, plus one step at 2 m/s, because the proxy follows the character a step late and so lets go a step late. Together they predict 0.3566.

Teleported, the proxy's position is written and its velocity left at zero, the obvious way to put a body where you want it. The obvious prediction is a crate that moves with no velocity and stops dead when the character stops. The first run refused even that. The crate had fallen asleep before the character arrived, and 8.12's wake_touched_by_kinematic wakes what a kinematic body touches only if that body is moving. A teleported proxy's velocity says it is not, so the crate never woke, and the character walked straight through it. That is the exact bug 8.12 fixed, reintroduced by a lie about a velocity.

Kept awake, the teleported crate does move at 1.9941 m/s, with a velocity of −0.0000. Only split impulse's position pass moves it, by β·(d − slop) a step. To move it v·h a step the proxy must start each step v·h/β + slop deep in it, and end it v·h shallower: 0.005 + 0.0333/0.2 − 0.0333 = 138.33 mm predicted, 138.20 measured. The character is visibly inside the crate, sunk to its elbows. When the character stops, the crate is pushed out of the excess, about v·h/β = 0.167 m (0.165 measured), and has no velocity to keep. Under Baumgarte the same bias is velocity, so the crate gets its 2 m/s back and coasts 0.3565 m, while the character is still sunk 138 mm in.

Too heavy to push. A 100 kg crate is over the limit and is a wall to the sideways pass. The character stops a skin from it, the proxy never touches it, and it moves at 0.00000 m/s. Standing on a pushable crate still works, because the vertical pass and the ground probe include pushable bodies: dropped onto a 20 kg crate the character lands grounded, on the crate, 0.0100 m above its top. The crate carries none of the character's weight, because nothing touches it. Exercise 4 adds it.

Being pushed. A 200 kg boulder rolled at 5 m/s into a standing character is moving at 0.0688 m/s a second and a half later, and the character has not moved at all. The proxy is kinematic, infinitely heavy, and the boulder hits it like a wall. That is the one-way coupling every kinematic controller has. The first draft of recover, which pushes the character out of whatever has moved into it, included heavy dynamic bodies too. With it, the same boulder carried the character 2.94 m and kept 3.57 m/s. That looked like a feature until it was named: the solver was moving the controller's character, which is two owners for one position. recover now answers only to fixed and kinematic bodies, the ones gameplay moves by intent. A game that wants boulders to shove its hero reads the proxy's contact impulses back as a knockback, deliberately, and exercise 4 does that as well.

13. Writing it

Two new pairs of files and one small change. phys/cast.hpp is the query. Like gjk.cpp it knows nothing about bodies or shapes, only about convex, because a projectile, a camera boom or a line of sight needs a cast as much as a character does. phys/character.hpp is the policy. It reaches down to rigid_body.hpp for where the obstacles are and how a platform moved, and to collide.hpp once, for 8.6's EPA. It does not include solver.hpp: the controller is outside the simulation on purpose, and the only thing it hands the solver is a proxy's velocity. convex.hpp gains a small struct, and that is a debt being paid, not a feature.

Step 1: the cast

§3's loop, and every line is one of four questions: how far apart are the two shapes now (GJK), how fast is the motion closing that gap (a dot product), how far can the mover go before the gap is the skin (a division), and has it arrived (a comparison). The mover is moved by changing one vector, origin, because 8.5 made every support function answer relative to it. The line that matters most is the one that takes the gap from certify(...).lower and not from g.distance: 

cast_result cast(const convex& mover, vec3 displacement, const convex& obstacle, const cast_config& cfg)
{
    cast_result out;

    const float len = length(displacement);

    // The mover, placed at the current iterate. `convex::support` answers
    // RELATIVE to `origin` (8.5's split), so moving the whole shape is moving
    // one vector — the adapter's pointer still views the untouched shape.
    convex at = mover;
    gjk_config gcfg = cfg.gjk;

    float t = 0.0f;
    for (int i = 0; i < cfg.max_iterations; ++i)
    {
        at.origin = mover.origin + displacement * t;
        const gjk_result g = gjk_distance(at, obstacle, gcfg);
        out.gjk_iterations += g.iterations;
        out.iterations = i + 1;

        if (g.status != gjk_status::separated)
        {
            // Overlapping (or within GJK's own contact margin, which is the
            // same thing to a cast). At the start that is the caller's problem
            // and EPA's question. Later it means the step landed ON the contact
            // — which, with no skin, is exactly where a flat face sends it —
            // and GJK cannot tell touching from overlapping there, so report the
            // last iterate it could still measure. `cast_config::skin` is the
            // measured case for never getting here.
            if (i == 0)
            {
                out.status = cast_status::started_inside;
                out.t = 0.0f;
                return out;
            }
            out.status = cast_status::hit;
            return out;   // `t`, `distance`, normal and point are the previous iterate's
        }

        // `direction` points from the mover toward the obstacle; the closing
        // rate of the gap is the motion's component along it. Per unit of `t`,
        // so it carries the displacement's length.
        const float approach = dot(displacement, g.direction);

        // THE GAP IS THE LOWER BOUND, NOT GJK'S `distance`. `distance` is the
        // length between two witness points — an UPPER bound, short of proof by
        // up to GJK's tolerance — and a step sized from an upper bound can end
        // inside the skin by exactly that slack (8.13 §3: 377 of 6,049 hits, by
        // up to 0.17 mm). The
        // lower bound is the width of the slab between the two supporting planes
        // normal to `direction`, and under a translation that slab narrows at
        // exactly `approach` per unit `t`: stepping to where IT reaches `skin`
        // cannot cross the skin, whatever `direction` is. That is the separating
        // plane of 8.4, turned into a time.
        const float gap = certify(at, obstacle, g).lower;

        out.t = t;
        out.distance = gap;   // the certified lower bound
        out.surface_normal = -g.direction;
        out.point = g.point_b;

        // Not closing, or closing so slowly that the whole of what is left of
        // the motion cannot bring the gap below `skin` by more than
        // `min_approach · len`. The slab normal to `direction` narrows at
        // exactly `approach` per unit `t` and not a hair faster, so this is a
        // proof of "clear" rather than a heuristic.
        if (approach <= cfg.min_approach * len)
        {
            out.status = cast_status::clear;
            out.t = 1.0f;
            return out;
        }

        // Arrived: within tolerance of the skin, and still closing.
        const float excess = gap - cfg.skin;
        if (excess <= cfg.tolerance * len)
        {
            out.status = cast_status::hit;
            return out;
        }

        // The Newton step toward `f = skin`: to where the slab is `skin` wide.
        // It cannot overshoot — see the header — so the only way out of the
        // motion is past its end.
        t += excess / approach;
        if (t >= 1.0f)
        {
            out.status = cast_status::clear;
            out.t = 1.0f;
            return out;
        }

        // Warm start the next GJK from this one's answer: the closest features
        // barely move between Newton steps, which is the case 8.5 §G measured.
        gcfg.initial_direction = g.direction;
    }

    // Out of steps. The iterate we stopped at was never evaluated, but it is
    // still safe — it is where the last separating slab narrowed to `skin` — so
    // report it; `distance` and the normal describe the iterate before it.
    out.status = cast_status::iteration_limit;
    out.t = t;
    return out;
}

Note the two ways out besides a hit. If the motion is not closing the gap (approach at or below min_approach·|d|), the slab will never narrow and the path is clear, which is a proof and not a guess. If GJK finds the shapes intersecting after the first step, the step landed on the contact, and the cast returns the last iterate it could still measure. §3's skin measurement is the case for never getting there.

Step 2: a placed shape, by value

Every scene since 8.10 has needed to turn "body i's shape at body i's pose" into a convex. as_convex needs an lvalue of the placed primitive to point at, so each of them wrote the same small struct: a sphere, a box and a capsule held by value, and a switch to fill the right one. That makes five copies, in three demos and two harnesses, each named as a debt by the lesson that wrote it. The controller is the first code inside the engine that needs it, and a sixth copy there would be the first a student could not see was a copy. So it moves into convex.hpp, with the deleted rvalue overload that makes place(...).view(), a view of a temporary, a compile error: 

struct placed_shape
{
    shape_kind kind = shape_kind::sphere;
    engine::sphere s{};
    obb b{};
    capsule c{};

    /// A `convex` view of the live member. **It points INTO this object**, so
    /// the `placed_shape` must outlive it — and the deleted rvalue overload
    /// below turns the one-liner that would not, `place(...).view()`, into a
    /// compile error, for the same reason the `as_convex` overloads above are
    /// deleted.
    [[nodiscard]] convex view() const&
    {
        switch (kind)
        {
        case shape_kind::sphere:  return as_convex(s);
        case shape_kind::box:     return as_convex(b);
        case shape_kind::capsule: return as_convex(c);
        }
        return as_convex(s);
    }

    convex view() const&& = delete;
};

Step 3: the one query

A sweep is §3's rule made into a function. Test the move's swept bounds against every body, cast against each survivor separately, and keep the smallest t. The solid filter leaves out pushable bodies, which is how the sideways pass walks into a crate and lets the proxy move it. An obstacle the capsule already overlaps is skipped, because overlap is recover's job, and a sweep that refused to move because of it would pin the character inside whatever pushed into it: 

character_hit sweep(vec3 position, vec3 d, const character_world& w, const character_config& cfg,
                    sweep_filter filter, move_report* report)
{
    character_hit best;
    if (report != nullptr) { ++report->sweeps; }
    if (length_squared(d) <= 0.0f) { return best; }

    const capsule here = character_capsule(position, cfg);
    const capsule there = character_capsule(position + d, cfg);
    const convex mover = as_convex(here);

    // The swept volume's box: both ends, plus the skin. A cast can only hit
    // what overlaps it, and a box test is a dozen comparisons against a cast's
    // several GJK calls.
    aabb swept = bounds_of(here);
    swept.expand(bounds_of(there));
    swept.grow(cfg.skin);

    cast_config cc = cfg.cast;
    cc.skin = cfg.skin;

    const std::size_t n = std::min(w.bodies.size(), w.shapes.size());
    for (std::size_t i = 0; i < n; ++i)
    {
        const auto index = static_cast<std::uint32_t>(i);
        if (index == w.self) { continue; }
        const rigid_body& b = w.bodies[i];
        if (filter == sweep_filter::solid && pushable(b, cfg)) { continue; }
        const shape& s = w.shapes[i];
        if (!overlaps(swept, bounds_of(s, b.state.position, b.orientation))) { continue; }

        const placed_shape obstacle = place(s, b.state.position, b.orientation);
        const cast_result hit = cast(mover, d, obstacle.view(), cc);
        if (report != nullptr)
        {
            ++report->candidates;
            ++report->casts;
            report->cast_iterations += hit.iterations;
            report->gjk_iterations += hit.gjk_iterations;
        }
        if (!hit.hit()) { continue; }
        if (!best.hit || hit.t < best.t)
        {
            best.hit = true;
            best.t = hit.t;
            best.normal = hit.surface_normal;
            best.point = hit.point;
            best.body = index;
        }
    }
    return best;
}

Step 4: what the rest of a move is clipped from

Quake's rule is clip_to_planes: one plane at a time, then each pair's crease, then stop. Its one subtle line is the slack. A vector clipped exactly onto plane i reads a hair either side of it after rounding, and without the slack every corner would be a stop. next_move is §4's finding, with the other two rules kept so that §C can measure them. The default clips the original motion, scaled to the time left: 

[[nodiscard]] vec3 next_move(const character_config& cfg, vec3 intent, float time_left, vec3 left,
                             const vec3* planes, int count)
{
    const auto all = std::span<const vec3>{planes, static_cast<std::size_t>(count)};
    switch (cfg.clip)
    {
    case character_config::clip_rule::original:   return clip_to_planes(intent * time_left, all);
    case character_config::clip_rule::remainder:  return clip_to_planes(left, all);
    case character_config::clip_rule::last_plane: return clip_to_planes(left, all.subspan(all.size() - 1));
    }
    return vec3{};
}
vec3 clip_to_planes(vec3 v, std::span<const vec3> planes)
{
    // "Goes into a plane" with a relative tolerance: the clipped vector lies IN
    // the planes it was clipped against, and rounding leaves it a hair either
    // side. Without the slack a vector clipped exactly onto plane i reads as
    // entering plane i again, and every corner becomes a stop.
    if (planes.empty()) { return v; }
    const float slack = 1e-5f * length(v);
    auto enters = [&](vec3 u, std::size_t k) { return dot(u, planes[k]) < -slack; };

    // One plane at a time.
    for (std::size_t i = 0; i < planes.size(); ++i)
    {
        const vec3 u = slide_along(v, planes[i]);
        bool ok = true;
        for (std::size_t j = 0; j < planes.size() && ok; ++j)
        {
            if (j != i && enters(u, j)) { ok = false; }
        }
        if (ok) { return u; }
    }

    // Two at a time: along the crease both planes contain.
    for (std::size_t i = 0; i < planes.size(); ++i)
    {
        for (std::size_t j = i + 1; j < planes.size(); ++j)
        {
            const vec3 crease = cross(planes[i], planes[j]);
            const float len2 = length_squared(crease);
            if (len2 <= 1e-10f) { continue; }   // parallel planes have no crease
            const vec3 dir = crease * (1.0f / std::sqrt(len2));
            const vec3 u = dir * dot(dir, v);
            bool ok = true;
            for (std::size_t k = 0; k < planes.size() && ok; ++k)
            {
                if (k != i && k != j && enters(u, k)) { ok = false; }
            }
            if (ok) { return u; }
        }
    }

    // Three planes pin it. Stop.
    return vec3{};
}

Step 5: the sideways pass

The pass carries three things through its loop: the intent (the whole step's motion, laid along whatever ground it is on), the time_left, and the list of walls. A walkable hit re-lays the intent along the new ground and carries on. A wall gets one step-up attempt, then a plane in the list with its normal flattened. The clipped result is laid back along the ground, because the clip turned it: 

void sideways(character& c, vec3 d, bool grounded, const character_world& w, const character_config& cfg,
              move_report& r)
{
    vec3 planes[k_max_planes];
    int count = 0;
    const int limit = std::min(cfg.max_slides, k_max_planes);
    bool tried_step = false;
    const bool follow = grounded && cfg.lay_along_ground;

    vec3 ground_n = c.ground_normal;
    vec3 intent = d;           // the whole step's motion, laid along whatever ground we are on
    float time_left = 1.0f;
    vec3 remaining = d;
    for (int i = 0; i <= limit; ++i)
    {
        if (length_squared(remaining) <= k_tiny_move2) { return; }
        if (i == limit)
        {
            r.blocked = true;
            return;
        }

        const character_hit hit = sweep(c.position, remaining, w, cfg, sweep_filter::solid, &r);
        c.position += remaining * hit.t;
        if (!hit.hit) { return; }
        time_left *= 1.0f - hit.t;
        const vec3 left = remaining * (1.0f - hit.t);

        // Walkable ground rising or falling under a grounded character — the
        // foot of a ramp, the edge of a curb low enough for the round bottom to
        // roll over. Not a wall: lay the intent along it and carry on.
        if (follow && walkable(hit.normal, cfg))
        {
            ground_n = hit.normal;
            intent = along_ground(horizontal(intent), ground_n);
            remaining = count == 0 ? intent * time_left
                                   : along_ground(horizontal(next_move(cfg, intent, time_left, left, planes, count)),
                                                  ground_n);
            continue;
        }

        ++r.slides;

        // A wall while standing: try to step up it, once per move.
        if (grounded && !tried_step && cfg.step_height > 0.0f)
        {
            tried_step = true;
            if (try_step_up(c, horizontal(left), w, cfg, r)) { return; }
        }

        planes[count++] = (cfg.flatten_walls && !walkable(hit.normal, cfg)) ? wall_normal(hit.normal) : hit.normal;
        vec3 next = next_move(cfg, intent, time_left, left, planes, count);

        // Clipping turned the motion; turn its climb with it, so that it still
        // lies on the ground it is crossing rather than lifting off or digging in.
        // A flattened wall normal is horizontal, so this cannot undo the clip.
        if (follow && walkable(ground_n, cfg)) { next = along_ground(horizontal(next), ground_n); }
        remaining = next;
    }
}

Step 6: the step-up

Three sweeps and four ways to refuse: no headroom, still a wall at the top, no floor to land on, or a landing that is not higher than the start. The across move is the larger of what was left and §8's step_forward_min, called with the radius the casts see: 

bool try_step_up(character& c, vec3 across, const character_world& w, const character_config& cfg,
                 move_report& r)
{
    const float len = length(across);
    if (len <= 0.0f || cfg.step_height <= 0.0f) { return false; }

    const vec3 start = c.position;
    vec3 p = start;

    // UP, as far as the ceiling allows.
    const character_hit up = sweep(p, k_up * cfg.step_height, w, cfg, sweep_filter::everything, &r);
    const float rise = cfg.step_height * up.t;
    p.y += rise;
    if (rise <= cfg.skin) { return false; }   // a roof right overhead: nothing to step into

    // ACROSS, by the rest of the move or the least that lands walkable.
    const float reach = std::max(len, step_forward_min(cfg.radius + cfg.skin, cfg.max_slope));
    const vec3 dir = across / len;
    const character_hit ahead = sweep(p, dir * reach, w, cfg, sweep_filter::solid, &r);
    const float moved = reach * ahead.t;
    if (moved <= cfg.skin) { return false; }  // still a wall at the top: taller than a step
    p += dir * moved;

    // DOWN, as far as it rose. Anything lower is a drop, not a step.
    const character_hit down = sweep(p, k_up * -rise, w, cfg, sweep_filter::everything, &r);
    if (!down.hit || !walkable(down.normal, cfg)) { return false; }
    p.y -= rise * down.t;

    const float gained = p.y - start.y;
    if (gained <= 0.0f || gained > cfg.step_height) { return false; }

    c.position = p;
    stand_on(c, down);
    r.stepped = true;
    r.step_rise = gained;
    return true;
}

Step 7: the vertical pass, and the snap

Rising, one sweep; the first thing overhead is a ceiling, and the game zeroes its upward velocity when hit_ceiling says so. Falling, land on the first walkable surface, or slide along a steep one. stop_on_ground is §5's creep, kept as a knob. The snap is §7: roll off an edge the character is resting on, by exactly what clears it, then cast straight down and keep the result only if it is floor: 

void vertical(character& c, float dy, const character_world& w, const character_config& cfg, move_report& r)
{
    if (dy > 0.0f)
    {
        const character_hit hit = sweep(c.position, k_up * dy, w, cfg, sweep_filter::everything, &r);
        c.position.y += dy * hit.t;
        r.hit_ceiling = hit.hit;
        return;
    }
    if (dy == 0.0f) { return; }

    vec3 planes[k_max_planes];
    int count = 0;
    const int limit = std::min(cfg.max_slides, k_max_planes);

    const vec3 intent = k_up * dy;
    float time_left = 1.0f;
    vec3 remaining = intent;
    for (int i = 0; i < limit; ++i)
    {
        if (length_squared(remaining) <= k_tiny_move2) { return; }
        const character_hit hit = sweep(c.position, remaining, w, cfg, sweep_filter::everything, &r);
        c.position += remaining * hit.t;
        if (!hit.hit) { return; }
        time_left *= 1.0f - hit.t;

        if (walkable(hit.normal, cfg))
        {
            stand_on(c, hit);
            if (cfg.stop_on_ground) { return; }
        }

        // Steep: gravity slides the character down it. Not flattened — sliding
        // DOWN a slope too steep to stand on is exactly what the design wants.
        const vec3 left = remaining * (1.0f - hit.t);
        planes[count++] = hit.normal;
        remaining = next_move(cfg, intent, time_left, left, planes, count);
    }
}
void snap_down(character& c, const character_world& w, const character_config& cfg, move_report& r)
{
    vec3 p = c.position;
    const vec3 down = k_up * -cfg.snap_distance;
    character_hit hit = sweep(p, down, w, cfg, sweep_filter::everything, &r);

    if (hit.hit && !walkable(hit.normal, cfg))
    {
        const vec3 foot = p - k_up * cfg.half_height;        // the bottom sphere's centre
        const vec3 away = horizontal(foot - hit.point);
        const float overhang = length(away);
        const float clear = cfg.radius + cfg.skin - overhang + 0.1f * cfg.skin;
        if (overhang > 1e-6f && clear > 0.0f)
        {
            const vec3 roll = away * (clear / overhang);
            const character_hit side = sweep(p, roll, w, cfg, sweep_filter::solid, &r);
            p += roll * side.t;
            hit = sweep(p, down, w, cfg, sweep_filter::everything, &r);
        }
    }
    if (!hit.hit || !walkable(hit.normal, cfg)) { return; }

    p.y -= cfg.snap_distance * hit.t;
    r.snapped = true;
    r.snap_drop = c.position.y - p.y;
    r.snap_shift = length(horizontal(p - c.position));
    c.position = p;
    stand_on(c, hit);
}

Step 8: recovering from what moved in

Overlap arrives from outside the controller: a door closes on the character, or a platform rises faster than the carry. It is 8.6's question, not a cast's. recover pushes out of anything fixed or kinematic that overlaps, by EPA's minimum translation plus the skin, and back to a full skin from anything nearer than half of one. The dynamic line is §11's refusal, one line long: 

int recover(character& c, const character_world& w, const character_config& cfg)
{
    int pushes = 0;
    const std::size_t n = std::min(w.bodies.size(), w.shapes.size());

    // A few passes, because pushing out of one thing can push into another; a
    // corner is two walls. Four is plenty for anything that is not a vice.
    for (int pass = 0; pass < 4; ++pass)
    {
        bool moved = false;
        for (std::size_t i = 0; i < n; ++i)
        {
            const auto index = static_cast<std::uint32_t>(i);
            if (index == w.self) { continue; }
            const rigid_body& b = w.bodies[i];
            if (b.kind == body_kind::dynamic) { continue; }   // the solver's: the proxy meets them

            const capsule here = character_capsule(c.position, cfg);
            aabb box = bounds_of(here);
            box.grow(cfg.skin);
            const shape& s = w.shapes[i];
            if (!overlaps(box, bounds_of(s, b.state.position, b.orientation))) { continue; }

            const placed_shape obstacle = place(s, b.state.position, b.orientation);
            const convex me = as_convex(here);
            const gjk_result g = gjk_distance(me, obstacle.view());
            vec3 push{};
            if (g.status == gjk_status::separated)
            {
                // Apart, but nearer than half a skin: casts from here would start
                // inside GJK's contact margin. Back off to a full skin.
                if (g.distance >= 0.5f * cfg.skin) { continue; }
                push = g.direction * -(cfg.skin - g.distance);
            }
            else
            {
                // Overlapping: 8.6's minimum translation, plus the skin. The axis
                // points from us toward it, so we move the other way.
                const separation s2 = collide(me, obstacle.view());
                push = s2.axis * -(s2.depth + cfg.skin);
            }
            c.position += push;
            ++pushes;
            moved = true;
        }
        if (!moved) { break; }
    }
    return pushes;
}

Step 9: what a platform just did

advance_orientation(q) is Δ·q for both spin rules. For the linearised one that is because normalising (1 + ½hω)·q is normalising (1 + ½hω) when q is unit. So the step's rotation is what the step does to the identity: one line, and exactly the integrator's: 

body_step step_motion(const rigid_body& b, float h, spin_rule rule)
{
    body_step m;
    if (b.kind == body_kind::fixed)
    {
        m.pivot = b.state.position;
        return m;
    }
    m.translation = b.state.velocity * h;
    m.pivot = b.state.position - m.translation;
    // `advance_orientation(q)` is `Δ·q` for both rules — the linearised one
    // because normalising `(1 + ½hω)·q` is normalising `(1 + ½hω)` when `q` is
    // unit — so the step's rotation is what it does to the identity.
    m.rotation = advance_orientation(quat{}, b.angular_velocity, h, rule);
    return m;
}
vec3 carry_by_transform(vec3 p, const body_step& m)
{
    return m.pivot + m.translation + rotate(m.rotation, p - m.pivot);
}

Step 10: the move, in order

Five stages, each there because a section needed it: carry (§10), recover (§11), sideways (§4, §5, §8), vertical (§5), and then the ground, probed and snapped (§6, §7). Two details are worth reading slowly. The sideways pass is only grounded if the character was grounded and is not rising, so a jump does not follow the floor. And c.velocity is (end − start)/h, what the move achieved, never what was asked for. It is the velocity to show a camera, an animation blend, or the proxy: 

move_report move_character(character& c, vec3 displacement, float h, const character_world& w,
                           const character_config& cfg)
{
    move_report r;
    const vec3 start = c.position;
    const bool was_grounded = c.grounded;

    // 1. CARRY. The ground moved during the physics step that just ran; move
    //    with it, swept, so that a platform cannot carry us through a wall.
    if (was_grounded && c.ground_body < w.bodies.size() && cfg.carry != character_config::carry_rule::none)
    {
        const rigid_body& g = w.bodies[c.ground_body];
        if (g.kind != body_kind::fixed)
        {
            const body_step m = step_motion(g, h, w.spin);
            const vec3 target = cfg.carry == character_config::carry_rule::transform
                                    ? carry_by_transform(c.position, m)
                                    : carry_by_velocity(c.position, g, h);
            const vec3 before = c.position;
            slide_move(c.position, target - c.position, w, cfg, sweep_filter::solid, r);
            r.carried = c.position - before;
            r.carried_turn = m.rotation;
        }
    }

    // 2. RECOVER from whatever moved into us.
    r.recovered = recover(c, w, cfg);

    // 3. SIDEWAYS.
    c.grounded = false;
    vec3 lateral = horizontal(displacement);
    if (was_grounded && cfg.lay_along_ground && displacement.y <= 0.0f && walkable(c.ground_normal, cfg))
    {
        lateral = along_ground(lateral, c.ground_normal);
    }
    sideways(c, lateral, was_grounded && displacement.y <= 0.0f, w, cfg, r);
    const bool stepped = r.stepped;

    // 4. VERTICAL.
    vertical(c, displacement.y, w, cfg, r);

    // 5. GROUND. A rising character is not standing on anything, whatever is
    //    under it. Otherwise probe a skin's width below the skin.
    if (displacement.y <= 0.0f && !c.grounded)
    {
        const character_hit probe = sweep(c.position, k_up * -(2.0f * cfg.skin), w, cfg,
                                          sweep_filter::everything, &r);
        if (probe.hit && walkable(probe.normal, cfg))
        {
            c.position.y -= 2.0f * cfg.skin * probe.t;
            stand_on(c, probe);
        }
    }
    if (stepped) { c.grounded = true; }

    // ...and SNAP: it stood last step, it is not rising, and the ground has
    // gone. Kept only if it ends on floor.
    if (!c.grounded && was_grounded && displacement.y <= 0.0f && cfg.snap_distance > 0.0f)
    {
        snap_down(c, w, cfg, r);
    }

    r.landed = c.grounded && !was_grounded;
    if (!c.grounded) { c.ground_body = k_no_body; }
    c.velocity = (c.position - start) * (1.0f / h);
    return r;
}

Step 11: the frame, from outside

The demo's fixed step is §2's three parts in order: steer the proxies (or teleport them, on P), run 8.10's physics step unchanged, then move each character against the world the step left. The game's own rules are the few lines around move_character. A grounded character does not fall, a jump is jump_speed(g, H), and a landing or a ceiling zeroes the vertical velocity: 

void advance(float h)
{
    // Gameplay moves the kinematic bodies by velocity, never by position.
    if (lift_ != k_no_body)
    {
        // Up and down between 0.1 and 1.5, a 5 s period: v = dy/dt.
        const float w = 2.0f * k_pi / 5.0f;
        body(lift_).state.velocity = vec3{0.0f, 0.7f * w * std::cos(w * t_), 0.0f};
    }

    // 1. The proxies catch up to where each character was left.
    for (player& p : players_)
    {
        rigid_body& proxy = body(p.proxy);
        if (steer_) { steer_proxy(proxy, p.ch.position, h); }
        else
        {
            proxy.state.position = p.ch.position;
            proxy.state.velocity = vec3{};
        }
    }

    // The rigid character in [2] is driven the way a naive game drives one.
    if (rigid_ != k_no_body)
    {
        const auto [wish, jump] = newton_script();
        rigid_body& b = body(rigid_);
        b.state.velocity.x = wish.x;
        b.state.velocity.z = wish.z;
        if (jump) { b.state.velocity.y = jump_speed(k_g, 1.2f); }
    }

    // 2. The physics step.
    physics_step(h);

    // 3. The controllers, against the world as the step left it.
    for (std::size_t i = 0; i < players_.size(); ++i)
    {
        player& p = players_[i];
        p.cfg.clip = scene_ == scene::corners ? p.cfg.clip : clip_;
        p.cfg.snap_distance = snap_ ? 0.35f : 0.0f;
        p.cfg.step_height = (step_ && scene_ != scene::corners) ? 0.3f : 0.0f;
        p.cfg.carry = carry_;

        const vec3 wish = wish_for(i);
        bool jump = (scene_ == scene::newton) ? newton_script().second : jump_;
        if (p.ch.grounded)
        {
            p.vel.y = 0.0f;
            if (jump) { p.vel.y = jump_speed(k_g, 1.2f); }
        }
        p.vel.x = wish.x;
        p.vel.z = wish.z;
        p.vel.y -= k_g * h;

        character_world view;
        view.bodies = world_.bodies();
        view.shapes = std::span<const shape>{shapes_};
        view.self = p.proxy;
        view.spin = world_.spin();

        const auto t0 = std::chrono::steady_clock::now();
        p.last = move_character(p.ch, p.vel * h, h, view, p.cfg);
        p.us = std::chrono::duration<double>(std::chrono::steady_clock::now() - t0).count() * 1e6;

        p.facing = engine::normalised(p.last.carried_turn * p.facing);
        if (p.ch.grounded && p.vel.y < 0.0f) { p.vel.y = 0.0f; }
        if (p.last.hit_ceiling && p.vel.y > 0.0f) { p.vel.y = 0.0f; }
    }
    jump_ = false;
}

16. Common pitfalls & debugging

The character creeps downhill while standing still.
Symptom: on a gentle slope with the stick released, the character slides slowly and steadily down: centimetres a second, the same speed every time.
Cause: gravity's one-step drop is being slid along the ground instead of stopped by it, g·h·sin α a second (§5: 8.2 cm/s on 30°).
Fix: a downward move that lands on walkable ground stops there. Keep the vertical pass separate from the sideways one.
The character jitters in a corner.
Symptom: pushed into an obtuse corner, it walks back and forth along one wall and then the other, forever.
Cause: the rest of the move is clipped from what was left after the first wall, which that wall had already bent. The other wall's projection of it points out of the corner (§4: 575 mm/s).
Fix: clip the original motion, scaled to the time left, against every surface hit so far.
The character sticks to a wall it is running along.
Symptom: running parallel to a wall, touching it, the character stops dead or stutters.
Cause: the cast reads a closing speed of 10⁻⁸ from rounding and calls it a hit (§4: 0.0000 m/s).
Fix: a motion closing at less than a small fraction of its length is not approaching; the separating-plane argument bounds what that costs.
A cast ends inside something, or the next one starts inside.
Symptom: the character occasionally overlaps a wall by a fraction of a millimetre, or a slide reports "started inside" and the character stops.
Cause: the Newton step was taken from GJK's distance, an upper bound (§3: up to 0.17 mm inside); or the skin is thinner than GJK's contact margin (7.3% of slides start inside at 0.1 mm).
Fix: step from certify(...).lower, and keep the skin at a millimetre or more.
The character goes through a wall that a cast should have seen.
Symptom: fast motion past one obstacle toward another passes straight through the second.
Cause: one cast against the nearest of several obstacles, whose separating plane says nothing about the rest (§3: 11,194 of 20,000).
Fix: one cast per obstacle, keep the smallest t.
The step-up never climbs a stair.
Symptom: with step_height well above the riser, the character still stands at the foot of the stairs.
Cause: the step moves across only by this frame's motion, a few centimetres, and the round bottom lands on the stair's edge at a tilt too steep to count as ground.
Fix: move across by at least (r + skin)(1 − sin θ), 90.8 mm at the defaults (§8).
Walking down stairs, the character hops.
Symptom: each stair is a small fall, and the landing animation fires on every step.
Cause: the snap's cast down hits the edge the capsule is resting on, at a steep tilt, and gives up; or it slides down the edge's tangent and lurches forward.
Fix: roll off the edge sideways by (r + skin) − overhang, then cast straight down (§7).
The character drifts off a turntable.
Symptom: standing still on a rotating platform, the character slowly spirals outward.
Cause: it is carried by the point velocity under it, which is a tangent (§10: eπωh per revolution, 11% at 2 rad/s).
Fix: carry by the platform's step as a transform, read back with the integrator the platform was actually moved by.
Crates the character walks into do not move, or sink it in.
Symptom: the character walks through a crate that has been still for a while; or it pushes the crate while standing a hand's width inside it, and the crate stops the instant it does.
Cause: the proxy is being teleported. Its velocity is zero, so a sleeping crate is never woken, and an awake one is moved only by the position pass (§11: 138 mm deep).
Fix: steer_proxy, every step, and never write its position.
The character stands a couple of millimetres above a big floor.
Symptom: on a very large ramp or ground plane the feet float slightly, and by a different amount from frame to frame.
Cause: the certified lower bound is loose by about the obstacle's size times GJK's angular error (§6: up to 2.3 mm off a 60 m ramp, 0.07 off a 6 m one). A tighter GJK tolerance changes nothing.
Fix: author collision geometry in pieces of sensible size.

17. Exercises

  1. Warm-up: the round bottom, by hand. For a capsule of radius 0.4, a 2 cm skin and a 40° slope limit, compute the free curb, the overhang it can stand at, and the least forward move a step-up needs. Then change character_config in §E and §G and check all three.

    Hint

    Use r + skin, not r, in all three: every cast stops a skin short, so it is the inflated capsule that touches.

    Solution

    r + s = 0.42. Free curb 0.42(1 − cos 40°) = 0.42 × 0.23396 = 98.3 mm; overhang 0.42 sin 40° = 270.0 mm; least forward 0.42(1 − sin 40°) = 0.42 × 0.35721 = 150.0 mm. A shallower limit makes the capsule stand further past edges and lurch further up stairs, which is why 45° is the common default.

  2. Coyote time and a jump buffer. Players press jump a few frames after walking off a ledge, and a few frames before landing, and expect both to work. Add both, for 0.1 s each, to the demo. Decide, and justify, whether they belong in character.cpp or in the game.

    Hint

    The controller reports grounded and landed honestly. Both features are about what the player meant, so they are the game's, and they need two timers, not a change to the controller.

  3. The other slope policy. Change along_ground to keep the step's length (the speed along the slope) instead of its horizontal part. Predict, then measure with §D, the horizontal speed up a 30° ramp at 3 m/s, and the time to climb 10 m of height.

    Solution

    Horizontal speed 3 cos 30° = 2.598 m/s. Ten metres of height is 10/sin 30° = 20 m along the ramp: 6.67 s, against 5.77 s when the horizontal part is kept (17.3 m horizontally at 3 m/s). Neither is physics; the first slows a character on a hill, the second keeps map speed constant.

  4. Weight and knockback: close the coupling. (a) While grounded on a dynamic body, apply the character's weight, m·g down at ground_point, with add_force_at, and build a see-saw that tips when the character walks onto one end. (b) After each solve, sum the normal impulses the proxy's manifolds received (8.10's write_back leaves them in the manifolds) and give the character a knockback velocity of that impulse over a chosen character mass. Measure §J's boulder again.

    Hint

    For (b) the character's mass is a design number, not a physical one; 80 kg makes the boulder push the character, and a very large number reproduces today's wall. Feed the knockback into the game's velocity, not into character::position, so that move_character still sweeps it.

  5. Engine challenge: a region query. §12's cull tests every body on every sweep. Give 8.8's uniform_grid a query(const aabb&, std::vector<std::uint32_t>&) that returns the bodies in the cells a box covers, pass the grid to sweep through character_world, and re-measure the 10,000-box corridor.

    Hint

    The grid already hashes each proxy's cell range; a query is the same range walk for one box, with 8.8's owner-cell rule replaced by a "seen" mark, because a query wants each body once rather than each pair once. The cost should become flat in N, near the 10-box figure.

18. Recap & next — and Module 8's project tree

The engine has a character controller, and underneath it a shape cast that any gameplay code can use. The character left the simulation because the simulation is right: friction is one number, momentum is kept, and a step is an instant. In its place is a capsule that asks one question and makes decisions about the answer. It clips the original motion against every surface it meets. It lays a step along walkable ground and never climbs anything steeper. It stands on what a short probe finds, and snaps down to ground that dropped away, rolling off edges first. It steps up what is shorter than a stair, and moves with whatever it stands on by exactly what that thing just did. It meets the rest of the world through a proxy with one owner, steered and never teleported.

The query turned out to be the deep part. A cast is Newton's method on a convex distance, which is why it converges in two or three steps. It is safe because each step stops at a separating plane, which is why it must be stepped from GJK's certified lower bound and never from its distance. And the capsule's round bottom gave the lesson its recurring number, (r + skin) times a trigonometric function of the slope limit, four times: a free curb, an overhang, the least forward move up a stair, and the roll off one going down.

Eight claims refused their sections: that no skin is merely inconvenient; that Newton on a convex function cannot overshoot, which was true only of the exact distance; that the plane list alone fixes corners; that flattening a wall's normal matters on the ground; that a tighter GJK tolerance buys precision; that a crest launches a character at a jog; that step_height is the tallest ledge; and that a teleported proxy's crate merely stops dead. Each refusal is in the lesson in place of the claim, as it has been all module.

19. Further reading

  • Kasper Fauerby, “Improved Collision Detection and Response” (2003). The classic collide-and-slide for an ellipsoid against triangles, with the swept-sphere tests written out; the reference most hobby controllers start from, and the reason "collide and slide" is the name.
  • id Software, Quake (1996) and Quake III Arena (1999) source, SV_FlyMove and PM_SlideMove. The clip-plane list of §4, and in Quake 1 the rule that clips the original velocity; Quake III's version adds the crease and the overbounce.
  • Brian Mirtich, “Timewarp Rigid Body Simulation” (SIGGRAPH 2000), and Impulse-based Dynamic Simulation of Rigid Body Systems (PhD thesis, 1996). Conservative advancement's origin, with the bound on rotational motion this lesson avoids by never turning the capsule.
  • Gino van den Bergen, “Ray Casting against General Convex Objects with Application to Continuous Collision Detection” (2004). GJK turned into a ray and shape cast directly: the other road to §3's query, trading conservative advancement's simplicity for fewer support calls.
  • Erwin Coumans, “Continuous Collision Detection and Physics” (2005). Where conservative advancement sits among the continuous methods, and why rotation is the hard part.
  • Jorrit Rouwé, Jolt Physics, CharacterVirtual. A production kinematic controller: stair walking with a forward test probe (the alternative to §8's minimum forward move), a mass for pushing dynamic bodies, and ground snapping. Read it after writing your own, not instead.
  • NVIDIA PhysX documentation, “Character Controllers”. The step offset, the slope limit and the contact offset (PhysX's skin) as one vendor names and documents them, including the same round-bottom caveat about steps.
  • John C. Hart, “Sphere Tracing: A Geometric Method for the Antialiased Ray Tracing of Implicit Surfaces” (1996). The other conservative step of §3, and why it needs no convexity.
  • Glenn Fiedler, “Fix Your Timestep!” (Gaffer on Games). Why §2's controller runs in the fixed step and not per frame, and what to interpolate when it draws.
