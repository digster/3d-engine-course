Microfacet Theory

Lesson 6.2 ended on a number: 1.1386 — a white surface with a white highlight reflecting more light than arrives. That number is the door into this lesson, and the question it forces is not "which constant is wrong?" but a much better one: if that light bounced off the surface, how can it also have gone into it? Answering it means replacing shininess — a number that makes the highlight the right size and measures nothing — with a statistical claim about what a surface is. Along the way, Blinn-Phong turns out to have been a microfacet distribution all along, and the constant this engine has shipped since Lesson 3.7 turns out to be wrong by exactly 17×.

1The Problem

Lesson 6.2 gave the shading equation units and then used them to measure the model the engine actually ships. Two of those measurements are worth putting back on the table, because this lesson is the answer to both:

What 6.2 measured, and could not explain

A white surface with a white highlight reflects 1.1386 of the light that hits it — diffuse and specular are added with no coupling, so the same photon is counted once as having bounced off the surface and once as having gone into it.

And the same surface reflects 0.1386 toward an eye 37° off the normal and 0.0259 toward one at 75° — a swing of 5.36× that no parameter controls and nothing in the model asked for.

It would be possible to patch both. A normalising constant would pull the first under 1; some ad-hoc falloff would flatten the second. Both patches would be wrong, and the reason they would be wrong is the actual subject of this lesson.

The model has no story about what a surface is. shininess is a number you turn until the highlight looks the right size. Consider what that means:

  • It is not comparable between models. Lesson 3.7 measured that Blinn's exponent must be roughly 4× Phong's for the same visual tightness — fitted at 4.38× falling to 4.01×. A parameter whose meaning depends on which formula consumes it is not describing the surface.
  • It is not measurable. There is no experiment you could perform on a piece of brushed aluminium to determine its shininess. There is no instrument, because there is no physical quantity.
  • It is not transferable. A shininess copied from one renderer to another means nothing, which is why every asset pipeline that has tried has produced materials that look different in every engine.

So the two symptoms in the callout are not two bugs. They are one absence, showing up twice. A model that cannot say what a surface is cannot say how much of it is visible, and cannot say how the light that bounced off it relates to the light that went in.

Microfacet theory supplies the missing story, and it supplies it in a form that can be checked — which is the difference that matters. By the end of §3 you will have an equation whose right-hand side is the number 1, and a twenty-line test that says whether any distribution satisfies it. That test is what finds everything else in this lesson, including a bug in the arithmetic that every reference on the subject prints.

2A Landscape of Tiny Mirrors

Here is the whole idea, and it fits in a sentence: a surface is not smooth, it is a landscape of microscopic mirrors, and each one is perfect.

Take that literally for a moment, because the payoff comes from taking it literally. Not "a surface is a bit rough". Every microfacet is a flawless mirror, obeying the reflection law exactly. The facets are far smaller than a pixel — so you never see one — and far larger than a wavelength — so ordinary geometric reflection applies to each and we can ignore diffraction. Between those two scales, a rough surface is a huge number of tiny perfect mirrors pointing in slightly different directions.

Now ask the shading question. Light arrives from l and your eye is at v. A given microfacet sends light from l to v only if it is a mirror oriented to do exactly that — and Lesson 3.7 already worked out which orientation that is. The halfway vector was derived there as "the normal this surface would need in order to bounce the light straight into the eye". In a microfacet model that sentence stops being a way of putting it and becomes the mechanism:

A facet contributes if and only if its own normal is h

Which turns the whole of specular shading into a counting problem. How many of the surface's microfacets happen to face h? That is the only question the model asks, and everything that follows is bookkeeping on the answer.

Look at what happened to the parameter. A surface where the facets nearly all face the same way is smooth: almost all of them line up with h at once, when h is near the surface normal, so the highlight is small and intense. A surface where the facets point every which way is rough: only a few line up with any particular h, but that stays true as h moves, so the highlight is broad and dim.

Roughness is the width of a histogram of surface slopes. That is a physical property. You can measure it with a profilometer. It transfers between renderers. It means the same thing in every model that uses it — which is exactly the list of things shininess was not.

The ninety-percent picture

Two simplifications are baked in here and are worth naming rather than discovering. First, the facets are treated as a statistical ensemble with no spatial arrangement — the model knows how many face each way but not where they are, which is why it cannot produce the glinty sparkle of real snow or car paint. That needs an actual normal map at microfacet scale, and it is a live research area.

Second, light is allowed to bounce off one facet and then leave. Real light in a valley bounces several times before escaping. §8 measures exactly what that costs, and it turns out to cost a great deal.

3The Distribution, and What Makes It One

"How many microfacets face this way" is a distribution over directions, and it gets a name and a letter: the normal distribution function, D(h). Feed it a direction, get back how much of the surface faces that way.

3.1 The identity, and why the cosine is in it

Here is where a distribution stops being a curve someone drew. If D reports what fraction of the surface faces each way, then the fractions have to add up to the whole surface. Integrate over every direction and you must get the surface back:

\[ \int_{\Omega} D(\mathbf{h}) \cos\theta_h \, d\omega = 1 \]

In prose, so this survives the CDN being unreachable: the integral over the hemisphere of D of h, times the cosine of the angle between h and the surface normal, is exactly 1.

The cosine is the part worth stopping on, because it is easy to read as a convention and it is not one. A microfacet tilted away from the macroscopic surface presents less of itself to that surface — its own area, projected down, is cos θh times its true area. It is Lesson 6.2 §3's projected-area factor again, in its third appearance this module, and it is the same factor for the same reason.

Which gives the identity a much better reading than "the distribution is normalised":

Read it backwards

The microfacets' projected areas add up to the area of the flat surface they stand on.

Nothing else could possibly be true. If they summed to more, the surface would have area it does not have; if less, part of it would be missing. Stated that way the identity is not a normalisation rule imposed from outside — it is the definition of the facets being on the surface.

3.2 What it buys: a test with a known answer

This is the practical difference between this lesson and everything before it in the shading arc, and it is worth being explicit about.

shininess could not be wrong. Whatever value it had produced whatever highlight it produced, and the only test available was whether someone liked the picture. D can be wrong, in a way a machine can detect, because the identity above has a right-hand side and the right-hand side is 1.

verify_63 §A integrates all three distributions on a 400,000-step grid and reports a worst error of 3.74e−07. That is quadrature noise, and the way you know it is quadrature noise rather than a wrong constant is that it shrinks when the grid refines — §9 turns that observation into a bug hunt.

Worked example — the constant a lobe is missing

Take the bare coss(θh) that Lesson 3.7 shipped, with no constant at all, and put it through the identity at s = 32. The integral separates: the azimuth contributes 2π, and

∫₀^(π/2) cos^32(θ) · cos(θ) · sin(θ) dθ = ∫₀¹ u^33 du = 1/34

substituting u = cos θ. So the whole integral is

2π / 34 = 0.1848

and verify_63 §A measures exactly that. The lobe is not a distribution; it is a shape, off by a factor of 5.41. Generalising, the integral is 2π/(s+2), so the constant that fixes it is its reciprocal — (s+2)/2π. That is the number §4 is about.

4Blinn-Phong Was a Microfacet Model

The worked example above did something worth noticing. It put Lesson 3.7's specular lobe through the NDF identity — and the integral came out finite, and a single constant fixes it. That is not a coincidence and it is not a curiosity:

Blinn-Phong's lobe is a microfacet distribution

Give coss(θh) the constant (s+2)/2π and it satisfies the identity exactly, for every exponent. It has always been a distribution over microfacet normals. What it has never had is its normalisation.

This is the spiral paying off, and it is worth being precise about what is being claimed. Lesson 3.7 introduced dot(n, h) raised to a power as a shape that looked right, and said so — it called the halfway vector "the normal this surface would need", and noted that "Blinn-Phong is the first, crudest microfacet model". Three modules later that sentence turns out to have been literally true. 3.7 was not wrong. It was un-normalised, which is a much more specific and much more fixable complaint.

So: what constant is the engine using? Lesson 6.2 divided the specular lobe by π, so that both lobes shared the light's units — and was careful to say that this was a scale choice and explicitly not a normalisation. Now we can price that choice.

Worked example — the ratio, and there is no π in it

The engine multiplies the lobe by 1/π. A distribution requires (s+2)/2π. The ratio is

((s + 2) / 2π) ÷ (1 / π) = (s + 2) / 2

Both constants carry a π and it cancels, so the answer is a plain integer arithmetic in the exponent. At the demo's default shininess of 32:

(32 + 2) / 2 = 17

verify_63 §B checks the identity across five exponents and finds the ratio is exactly (s+2)/2 to 1e−4 — which is float noise, because the relation is algebraic.

So this engine's highlights have been seventeen times dimmer than the distribution says, at the default material, since Lesson 3.7. And nothing looked wrong — which is the part worth dwelling on, because it is how this class of error survives.

The reason is that specular::colour absorbed it. An artist setting up a shiny surface turns the specular colour up until the highlight looks right, and the value they land on is seventeen times what a physically plausible reflectance would be. The picture is fine. The parameter is nonsense, and it is nonsense in a way that only shows up when you try to author against physical values, or load a material from another renderer, or ask whether the surface conserves energy — which is exactly what Lesson 6.2 tried to do and why it found 1.1386.

Reading the old materials into the new vocabulary

The mapping runs both ways, and the reverse direction is the useful one right now. Inverting s = 2/α² − 2 gives α = √(2/(s+2)), so the demo's shininess of 32 means α = 0.2425 — a perceptual roughness of 0.49.

Almost exactly half way, which is a perfectly reasonable thing for a default material to be, and was arrived at by nobody choosing it. verify_63 §B also checks that shininess → α → shininess round-trips to 1e−5 relative, because a material that changes when you convert it to the new vocabulary and back is not a conversion, it is a bug.

5Beckmann, GGX, and the Tail

Blinn-Phong's exponent lobe is a distribution. It is not one that any surface actually has, and the reason is that it was chosen for being cheap to evaluate rather than for describing a slope histogram. Two distributions that were chosen for the second reason:

Beckmann's assumes the microfacet slopes are Gaussian — the natural assumption for a surface produced by a large number of small independent processes, which is what grinding, weathering and casting are. It is the distribution physical-optics derivations arrive at, and for thirty years it was what "physically based" meant.

\[ D_{\text{Beckmann}}(\mathbf{h}) = \frac{\exp\!\left(-\tan^2\theta_h / \alpha^2\right)} {\pi \alpha^2 \cos^4\theta_h} \]

In prose: e to the minus tangent-squared of θₕ over α², all divided by π α² cos⁴θₕ.

GGX — also called Trowbridge–Reitz, after the 1975 paper that got there first and was forgotten for thirty years — replaces the exponential with a rational function:

\[ D_{\text{GGX}}(\mathbf{h}) = \frac{\alpha^2}{\pi \left( \cos^2\theta_h (\alpha^2 - 1) + 1 \right)^2} \]

In prose: α² divided by π times the square of (cos²θₕ times (α²−1), plus 1). §9 is about why the implementation does not compute that denominator the way it is written here.

Both integrate to 1. Both peak at the same height for the same α — verify_63 §C checks that, and gets 3.5368 for each at α = 0.3. So they are not "a bright one and a dim one". Every difference between them is a difference of shape.

And the shape difference is dramatic, in a way a linear plot completely hides — which is why the figure is on a log axis. Read the measured table:

θhBeckmannGGXGGX / Beckmann
10°2.66182.07710.78
20°1.04090.74230.71

45°2.11 × 10⁻⁴9.64 × 10⁻²456

GGX is lower near the peak — 0.71× Beckmann at 20° — and then crosses over and is 456× higher at 45°. It has taken energy out of the shoulder and put it in the tail. It has to: both integrate to 1, so anything one gains somewhere it loses elsewhere.

The mechanism is visible in the two formulas. Beckmann's exponential dies faster than any power, so by 60° it is 1.9 × 10⁻¹³ — numerically zero in a shader, and a hard edge to the highlight where a real surface has a soft one. GGX's denominator is a polynomial, so its tail is a power law: it falls off and never quite stops.

Why this won, and what it says about how graphics research goes

Real surfaces have a faint glow around a highlight, extending much further than a Gaussian slope distribution predicts. Everybody could see it; the standard answer for years was to add a second, broader lobe and tune the mix. Walter et al. pointed out in 2007 that a distribution with a heavier tail reproduces it with no extra lobe and no extra parameter — and that Trowbridge and Reitz had published exactly such a distribution in 1975 by fitting measured data rather than deriving from optics.

The lesson is not "GGX is better". It is that the distribution is where the physical claim lives, so when a model looks wrong, the distribution is the first place to look and the last place people looked.

6Matching the Peak Is Not Matching the Lobe

Every reference that mentions both gives you the same conversion from a GGX roughness to a Blinn exponent:

\[ s = \frac{2}{\alpha^2} - 2 \]

It is easy to derive, and deriving it shows immediately what it is and is not. Set the two distributions equal at the peak, where h = n and both cosines are 1:

\[ \frac{s+2}{2\pi} = \frac{\alpha^2}{\pi \left(\alpha^2\right)^2} = \frac{1}{\pi \alpha^2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad s + 2 = \frac{2}{\alpha^2} \]

In prose: Blinn's peak value is (s+2)/2π, GGX's is 1/(πα²), and setting them equal gives s + 2 = 2/α².

verify_63 §E confirms the mapping does exactly what it was derived to do: the two peaks agree to a relative 5.99e−08, which is one float ULP. So it is correct — for the question it answers, which is "which exponent gives the same peak height?"

Now ask a different question — "which exponent gives the most similar lobe?" — and fit it numerically, minimising the difference over the whole hemisphere. §E scans the exponent on a log grid and reports:

αpeak-match sfitted sratio
0.10198.0159.50.806
0.2048.038.60.803
0.3020.216.10.794
0.506.04.60.759

Consistently about 0.80×, drifting further apart as the surface roughens. And the fit is not converging on the "true" answer with the peak match as a bad approximation — both numbers are exactly right, for the two different questions. A lobe fit trades peak height for tail coverage, because GGX has a tail Blinn cannot reproduce at any exponent whatsoever, and the least-bad compromise is to sit slightly wider than the peak match.

This is what folklore is, and how to handle it

The formula is not wrong. What is missing, in every place it is quoted, is the criterion — and without the criterion you cannot know whether it is the right conversion for your problem. Matching a legacy asset's peak intensity? Use it. Matching how an old material looked? You want the 0.80×.

The transferable habit is small and worth keeping: when you meet a conversion formula, derive it far enough to see what it optimised. Two minutes of algebra here revealed that a widely-quoted mapping answers a narrower question than the one people use it for.

7Shadowing and Masking

D counts the facets that face h. It does not ask whether they can be seen, or whether the light can reach them — and on a landscape, both questions have real answers. A facet down in a valley may face exactly the right way and be completely hidden behind the ridge in front of it.

Two effects, and they are the same effect from the two ends of the light path. Shadowing is a facet the light cannot reach. Masking is a facet the eye cannot see. Together they are the geometry term, G, and the crucial thing about it is that it is not a correction bolted onto the model to make the numbers behave — it is a consequence of the picture in §2. Once you have said "landscape", you have said "some of it is hidden".

Smith's formulation is the standard one, and its idea is that the fraction of the surface visible from a direction can be derived from the same slope distribution that produced D — which is what makes it a consequence rather than a second model. For GGX the result is exact and compact:

\[ G_1(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{2 (\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{x})} {(\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{x}) + \sqrt{\alpha^2 + (1-\alpha^2)(\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{x})^2}} \]

In prose: twice the cosine, divided by the cosine plus the square root of (α² plus (1−α²) times the cosine squared).

It is 1 looking straight down — nothing hides from directly above — and falls toward 0 at grazing incidence. How fast it falls is entirely a question of roughness, and that is the whole content of the term:

Worked example — the term 6.2 was missing

At 75° from the normal, measured in verify_63 §D:

roughness 0.22 → G₁ = 0.9920
roughness 1.00 → G₁ = 0.4112

A near-smooth surface still shows you 99% of itself at a glancing angle; a fully rough one shows you 41%. That is what Lesson 6.2's unexplained 5.36× view-angle swing was. A rough surface genuinely does hide more of itself as you move round to the side — but Blinn-Phong has no term that can say so, so its own swing came out of the lobe's geometry by accident, in an amount nobody chose and no parameter controls.

Note what changed: the effect is still there, and it is still large. It has stopped being arbitrary.

7.1 The assumption in the multiplication

The obvious way to combine the two is to multiply: mask for the light, then mask for the eye. smith_g_separable does exactly that, and its name is a warning label.

Multiplying two probabilities assumes they are independent — that whether a facet is lit tells you nothing about whether it is visible. That is plainly false: both depend on the facet's height in the same landscape, so a facet down in a valley tends to be hidden from both directions at once, and the separable form counts it as hidden twice.

Heitz's height-correlated form resolves the two together over the shared height distribution. The usual presentation treats the choice as a matter of taste. verify_63 §D measures it instead:

Identical to four decimals on smooth surfaces and at steep angles — and 1.715× apart on a rough surface at grazing incidence, where the separable form throws away nearly half the light. Rough materials seen at glancing angles are sunsets, wet roads, worn metal and the rim of every object in every scene. "Either is fine" would have been a comfortable sentence to write, and measuring it made it an untrue one.

8The Energy Budget

Lesson 6.2 built a hemisphere integrator to answer "does this model conserve energy?" as a number. Point it at the new machinery — and point it at the same integrator, character for character, because a test rewritten for each new subject is a test of the test:

\[ R(\mathbf{v}) = \int_{\Omega} \frac{D\,G}{4(\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{l})(\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{v})} \cos\theta \, d\omega \]

In prose: the directional-hemispherical reflectance of the specular microfacet BRDF — D times G, over four times the two cosines — with Fresnel set to 1, so nothing is absorbed and any shortfall is light the model itself loses. (The 4 in the denominator is a Jacobian from changing variables between the light direction and the halfway vector; Lesson 6.4 derives it, because it belongs with the assembly rather than with the parts.)

The first result is the one this whole lesson was for. It never exceeds 1. Not at any roughness, not at any view angle. Compare Lesson 6.2's table, where Blinn-Phong went over at every shininess — 1.1386 at 32, 1.7333 at 2. The model can no longer emit more light than arrives, and it cannot because D is a distribution and G is a fraction, which are properties we can check rather than hope for.

The second result is a surprise, and it is large. Read the other end of the table:

roughnessR(v), eye at 0°light lost
0.220.99760.2%
0.500.91588%
0.710.683832%
0.870.458754%
1.000.306969%

A fully rough surface returns 30.7% of the light that hits it, with Fresnel set to 1 — with, that is, a surface that absorbs nothing. Where did the other 69% go?

Single scattering forgets the second bounce

G removes the light that hits a facet the eye cannot see. It does not then ask what happened to that light — and what happened is that it hit another facet and carried on. The model lets light bounce once and then loses interest in it.

On a smooth surface there is barely a second bounce to miss, which is why the error is 0.2% at roughness 0.22. On a rough one, most of the light that leaves has bounced several times, which is why it is 69% at roughness 1.0.

The visible symptom is specific and you will recognise it once named: rough metal rendered this way is too dark, and it gets darker the rougher it is, in a way no amount of turning the light up fixes because it is a fraction rather than an offset.

This is not a bug in the implementation and it is not quadrature error. It is what single-scattering microfacet theory is, and every engine that ships GGX ships this deficit unless it also ships a multiple-scattering compensation term. §11 says what those look like and why this course does not build one.

It is worth noticing what just happened, though: a model that is honest enough to be measured told us the size of its own remaining error. Blinn-Phong could not have done that, because there was no quantity to compare against.

9The Test Found a Bug in float

§3 claimed that having a test whose expected answer is exactly 1 is worth more than having a picture somebody likes. Here is that claim cashing out, on the first run.

The §A table, before any of the implementation below was written, looked like this — with tolerances set from the double-precision probe, which had reported clean 1.0000000s:

The obvious response is to loosen the tolerance: 1.8e−4 is small, the picture would be fine, and the number is smallest exactly where the surface is smooth and nobody is looking closely. Do not do this. The value of a test with a known answer is entirely in believing the known answer, and a tolerance widened to admit a failure is a test deleted slowly.

The right first question is which error this is. There are only two candidates — the quadrature, or the arithmetic — and they are trivially distinguishable:

Quadrature error shrinks when the grid refines. Arithmetic error does not.

Refining the integration 5× moved the answer from 0.9998169 to 0.9998154 — nothing. Meanwhile the identical formula evaluated in double, on the identical grid, gave 1.0000000.

Two runs, and the diagnosis is complete: the error is in float, and it is in the formula rather than in the integrator.

Look again at GGX's denominator as every reference prints it:

\[ d = \cos^2\theta_h \left(\alpha^2 - 1\right) + 1 \]

Near the peak, cos²θh is very close to 1 and (α²−1) is very close to −1. So this computes the difference of two numbers of size 1 that very nearly agree — which is catastrophic cancellation, the classic way to destroy precision. A float holds about seven significant digits, so a quantity of size 1 carries an absolute error around 1e−7. At α = 0.01 the true value of d at the peak is 1e−4. A thousandth of it is noise before we start. Then it is squared, which doubles the relative error.

The fix is one line of algebra and no extra operations:

\[ d = \cos^2\theta_h \left(\alpha^2 - 1\right) + 1 = \left(1 - \cos^2\theta_h\right) + \alpha^2\cos^2\theta_h = (1 - c)(1 + c) + \alpha^2 c^2 \]

In prose: the same expression, rearranged so the subtraction is (1 − c)(1 + c) rather than a near-cancelling sum.

Why (1 − c) is the win — Sterbenz's lemma

For any two floating-point numbers a and b with b/2 ≤ a ≤ 2b, the subtraction a − b is computed exactly, with no rounding at all, in any IEEE format. It is one of the very few operations that is free of error, and it is free precisely in the case that looks most dangerous.

Here a = 1 and b = c, so the condition is c ≥ 0.5 — which covers the entire peak region, exactly where the old form was worst. The near-cancellation is not avoided; it is made exact.

The habit worth keeping: when a formula subtracts two nearly-equal quantities, look for an algebraically identical form that subtracts them in one operation instead of accumulating them through several.

Worked example — what the rearrangement is worth

Both forms, integrated against the identity, in float:

α = 0.001   textbook 0.9891063   rearranged 1.0023035
α = 0.010   textbook 0.9998169   rearranged 1.0000004
α = 0.050   textbook 1.0000006   rearranged 1.0000000

At mirror-like roughness the textbook form loses 1.1% of the model's energy to rounding alone. At α = 0.01 the rearrangement is a 450× improvement in the error. Above α ≈ 0.05 there is nothing in it, which is why this is invisible in ordinary scenes and shows up on chrome and still water.

verify_63 §A asserts the comparison directly — computing the textbook form inline and requiring ours to beat it by 4× — because the rearranged version looks like a pointless shuffle, and the next person to tidy it back should be told by a failing test rather than by a code review.

10Implementation

One new header, engine/gfx/microfacet.hpp, header-only like light.hpp — so the public header count goes 56 → 57 and engine/CMakeLists.txt does not move.

10.1 Roughness, α, and a remap that is a choice

α = roughness² is Disney's remap, and it is a convention rather than physics. The distributions are written in α, which is the RMS slope — a real quantity you could measure. But α is a terrible thing to put on a slider: almost all the visible change happens in its bottom fifth, so four fifths of the travel does nothing. Squaring a [0,1] parameter pushes resolution toward the smooth end where the eye is.

That is the same argument Lesson 6.1 made about sRGB spending codes where the eye is, arriving in a completely different place — and it is worth flagging that a roughness value copied from a renderer that squares differently will not match. This is a real interoperability trap and it is entirely conventional.

10.2 The floor on α, which is a lie with a physical reading

A perfectly smooth surface is not a small number — it is a delta function. Every facet faces exactly n, so D is infinite at h = n and zero everywhere else. That is correct physics and unrepresentable: the denominator goes to zero, the answer arrives as inf, and the first multiplication by a zero turns it into NaN.

Clamping is standard, and the honest framing is that it is a lie which happens to be closer to the truth than the alternative: no real surface is smooth at the scale of a light wavelength, so a floor on roughness is more physical than zero is. At 1e−3 the peak of GGX is 1/(πα²) = 318,310 — large, and finite.

10.3 The three distributions behind one enum

ndf_model is pipeline state, not a material parameter — the same call Lesson 3.7 made for specular_model, for the same reason. A real engine bakes one choice into a shader; it is a runtime knob here so the three can be compared and the differences measured rather than asserted, which is what §5 and §6 did with it.

Blinn stays in, and not out of sentiment: showing that coss satisfies the same identity as the other two is the whole argument of §4, and an argument you can run is worth more than one you can read.

10.4 Two geometry terms, because the choice was measurable

The height-correlated form is the default and smith_g_separable is kept beside it, named for its assumption. Keeping both is only worth the lines because §7 measured the gap at 1.715× — without that number the second function would be ceremony, and with it the default is a decision rather than a preference.

10.5 Nothing is wired in, and that is the design

shade() is untouched. scene.frag.hlsl is untouched. The only edit outside the new header is a comment in light.hpp pointing at it and recording the 17× measurement next to the constant it is about.

Why the parts ship before the assembly

A microfacet BRDF is D, G and F over a denominator, and Fresnel is the term that couples the diffuse and specular lobes — which is the thing §1's 1.1386 actually needs. Shipping D and G live would leave the renderer running a model that is neither the old one nor the new one for the length of a lesson: worse pictures, and a golden image that has to be re-baselined twice.

So 6.3 builds the parts and tests each one against a property it must satisfy on its own. 6.4 assembles, derives the 4(n·l)(n·v) denominator, adds Fresnel, and replaces shade()'s specular half. The golden survives a fourteenth lesson here and will certainly not survive 6.4 — which is exactly the right place for it to break, because that is the lesson that changes the picture on purpose.

11What This Lesson Does Not Do

It does not compensate for multiple scattering. §8 measured the deficit at 69% for a fully rough surface, and the standard fixes — Kulla–Conty's tabulated energy compensation, or Turquin's cheaper analytic fit — both work by measuring R(v) offline and adding back a lobe carrying the missing energy. Both are a lesson's worth of work and both need the assembled BRDF from 6.4 to compensate. Named here so the darkness of rough metal is a known limitation rather than a mystery.

It does not do anisotropy. Brushed metal, hair and vinyl have slope distributions that are wider in one surface direction than the other, which needs two α values and a tangent frame to orient them. The tangent frame arrives in Lesson 6.7 for normal mapping; anisotropic GGX is a natural exercise once it exists.

It does not use the Smith height-correlated visibility form. Real shaders fold the 4(n·l)(n·v) denominator into G and evaluate V = G/(4(n·l)(n·v)) as one expression, which avoids a division by two numbers that both go to zero at grazing angles. That is an assembly concern, so it is 6.4's.

It does not model the facets' arrangement. The distribution says how many face each way and nothing about where they are, which is why this model produces a smooth highlight and real snow, car paint and the sea produce glints. Glinty appearance models are an active research area and well outside this course.

14Common Pitfalls & Debugging

The highlight vanishes as the surface gets smooth

Symptom: lowering roughness makes the highlight tighter and then it blinks out entirely, or the whole surface goes black or white.

Cause: α reached zero. D divides by α², so you get inf, and the first multiplication by a zero elsewhere turns it into NaN — which then propagates through every blend it touches.

Fix: the k_min_alpha floor. And when hunting it, remember that NaN compares false to everything, so if (x > 0) does not catch it — Lesson 3.10 hit the same shape of bug and the rule there was to write the test as !(x <= 0).

Your NDF integrates to 2, or 0.5, or 6.28

Symptom: the normalisation test fails by a clean-looking factor.

Cause and fix: the factor names it, exactly as in Lesson 6.2. 2.0 means the cos θh weight is missing. 0.5 means the sin θ in dω is. 2π means you integrated θ and forgot φ.

Establish the integrator first, on something with a known answer: integrate the constant 1 and check you get 2π, then cos θ and check you get π. Two lines, and they separate the instrument from the subject.

A failure that will not shrink

Symptom: a numerical test is off by a small amount and refining the grid does not help.

Cause: it is not a quadrature error. Quadrature error shrinks with the grid, by definition; anything that does not is arithmetic.

Fix: run the identical formula in double on the identical grid. If double is clean, the bug is in float and it is almost always cancellation — look for a subtraction of two nearly-equal quantities and rearrange it. This is §9's entire method and it took two runs.

Materials imported from another engine look wrong at the same numbers

Symptom: roughness 0.4 in one renderer does not look like roughness 0.4 in yours.

Cause: the roughness→α remap. Squaring is a convention, not physics, and not everyone squares. Some tools author α directly; some square twice.

Fix: convert at the import edge and write down which convention you store — exactly the two-conversions-at-the-edges discipline Lesson 6.1 arrived at for colour, and for the same reason.

Rough metal is too dark and turning the light up does not fix it

Symptom: rough metallic surfaces look dull and lifeless; raising the light blows out the smooth parts of the scene before the rough parts look right.

Cause: not a bug — §8's single-scattering deficit, which is 69% at full roughness. It is a fraction of the arriving light, so a brighter light scales the problem rather than fixing it.

Fix: multiple-scattering compensation (§11), or accept it and know what you are accepting. Recognising the shape of the error — proportional, worse with roughness, worst on metal — is most of the value here.

15Exercises

  1. 15.1 — Normalise the old model, and predict the picture first. Change specular_brdf in light.hpp to use (s+2)/2π instead of 1/π, and before rebuilding, write down what you expect to see and what you would have to do to the demo's materials to compensate.

    Hint

    17× at shininess 32, and §8's energy test now applies to the result. What does 17× a specular of 0.30 do to Lesson 6.2's 1.1386?

    Solution sketch

    The highlights blow out completely — and the honest reading is not "the constant is wrong" but that the material was authored against the old constant, so it is now a material with a specular reflectance of 5.1, which is not a surface. Divide the demo's specular colours by 17 and the picture comes back nearly unchanged, which is the proof that the parameter had absorbed the error. Then run 6.2's energy test: a white albedo with the corrected specular is still over 1, because normalising D does not supply the coupling. That is 6.4's job, and this exercise is the cleanest way to feel why a normalising constant was never going to be enough.

  2. 15.2 — Break the stable denominator on purpose. Replace ndf()'s GGX denominator with the textbook c2 * (a2 - 1) + 1, watch §A fail, and then find the roughness at which the failure disappears.

    Hint

    Somewhere between α = 0.02 and α = 0.05. The relevant comparison is the true value of d at the peak — which is α² — against a float's absolute resolution near 1, which is about 6e−8.

    Solution sketch

    It becomes invisible once α² is comfortably above float's ~1e−7 resolution at 1, so around α ≈ 0.05, which is roughness 0.22. Above that the cancellation still happens and simply does not matter. The transferable point is that the bug is bounded by the data: it lives entirely in the smooth end, which is why it survives in production renderers and shows up on chrome and still water rather than everywhere.

  3. 15.3 — Test your own distribution. Implement the original Phong lobe as an NDF — coss about the mirror direction rather than the halfway vector — and put it through §A's identity.

    Hint

    The identity is an integral over microfacet normals. What is the mirror direction the normal of?

    Solution sketch

    It is not an NDF at all, and the exercise is worth doing to find out why rather than to get a number. A distribution of microfacet normals is a function of h, and Phong's lobe is a function of the angle between the mirror direction and the eye — a quantity that depends on where you are standing, so it cannot be a property of the surface. That is a much sharper statement of Phong's defect than Lesson 3.7 could make, and it explains 3.7's measured hard cut-off: the model was never describing a surface, so there was nothing to stop it describing an impossible one.

  4. 15.4 — Anisotropy. Extend GGX to two roughness values, αx and αy, along two tangent directions, and check that the anisotropic form still integrates to 1.

    Hint

    The isotropic denominator is a function of cos²θh and sin²θh. Anisotropy splits the sine term into its two tangent components, each divided by its own α. You will need h in tangent space, which the engine does not have until Lesson 6.7 — so build the test on a synthetic frame.

    Solution sketch

    The normalisation becomes 1/(π α_x α_y …) and the identity still holds, which is the point of checking: an anisotropic distribution is still a distribution. Setting αx = αy must reproduce the isotropic form exactly — assert that, because it is the cheapest possible regression test and it catches most of the ways this goes wrong.

  5. 15.5 — Measure the deficit properly, and make a table. §8 sampled R(v) at two view angles and five roughnesses. Build the full 2-D table that a multiple-scattering compensation term needs — R as a function of roughness and n·v — and look at its shape.

    Hint

    32×32 is plenty. Watch the cost: each cell is its own hemisphere integral, so this is a few million evaluations and belongs in a one-off program rather than in verify_63.

    Solution sketch

    The surface is smooth and monotone in roughness, and — the thing worth noticing — it is not monotone in view angle at high roughness: §8 already shows 0.3069 at 0° but 0.5906 at 75°. That is why compensation terms are tabulated in two dimensions rather than fitted to roughness alone. Having built the table you have most of Kulla–Conty; the remaining step is adding back a lobe carrying 1 − R, which needs 6.4's assembled BRDF to attach to.

16Recap & Next

The engine now has a story about what a surface is, and it is a story that can be checked. A surface is a landscape of perfect mirrors; the shape of a highlight is a histogram of their slopes; and that histogram is a distribution, so it must satisfy

\[ \int_{\Omega} D(\mathbf{h}) \cos\theta_h \, d\omega = 1 \]

which is the first equation in this shading arc with a right-hand side you can hold a model to.

What the engine gained: gfx/microfacet.hpp, with three distributions, two forms of the Smith geometry term, and the roughness remap — 56 → 57 public headers, nothing wired in. What it learned about itself: Blinn-Phong was a microfacet distribution all along, missing only its constant, and the constant this engine ships is wrong by exactly (s+2)/2 — 17× at the default material, silently absorbed by every specular colour an artist ever tuned.

And three numbers worth carrying forward. GGX beats Beckmann by 456× in the tail at 45°, which is the entire reason it won. The height-correlated Smith term differs from the separable one by 1.715× at grazing on rough surfaces, which is why "either is fine" is not true. And single-scattering microfacet theory loses 69% of the light at full roughness, which is why rough metal renders dark in every engine that has not bought it back.

The test also found a bug that has nothing to do with microfacets: the textbook GGX denominator loses 1.1% of the model's energy to catastrophic cancellation in float, and one line of algebra plus Sterbenz's lemma removes it. That is what a test whose expected answer is a round number is for.

Next, Lesson 6.4: Cook–Torrance, derived. The parts are built and each is tested against a property it must satisfy alone. 6.4 assembles them — which means deriving the 4(n·l)(n·v) denominator rather than quoting it, and adding the term this lesson deliberately left out. Fresnel is the coupling. It says what fraction of the arriving light bounces off the interface, and only the remainder — 1 − F — is left to go into the surface and scatter back out diffusely. The two lobes stop being independent, and §1's 1.1386 stops being possible. That is the centrepiece of the module, and everything in this lesson is the apparatus it needs to make its argument in numbers rather than adjectives.
