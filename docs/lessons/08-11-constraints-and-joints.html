Constraints and Joints: Hinge and Ball-Socket

Lesson 8.10's solver holds a stack of crates up and cannot hold a lamp up. A contact may push and may not pull, and a lamp on a chain is nothing but pulling. This lesson takes 8.9's three lines — a relative velocity along a direction, divided by an effective mass, clamped — and writes them once, for any question of that shape, as a Jacobian row: twelve numbers saying which combination of two bodies' velocities a constraint cares about, and two bounds saying what it is allowed to do about it. A contact normal turns out to be exactly such a row, checked to 1.9 × 10⁻⁷ on two hundred real contacts — and the general form turns out to be 27% faster than the hand-written one, which refused the prediction this lesson was handed. Then the new constraints, each a choice of rows and bounds: a rod, a rope (a contact turned inside out, which lets go at exactly the height a rod starts to push), a ball-socket solved as one 3×3 block that is exact where three rows leave up to 92% of the error, and a hinge with a limit and a motor. Along the way the measurements refused three more claims, and each refusal was the better lesson. A joint solved at the velocity level drifts outward along a tangent, by a Pythagorean recurrence that tracks the simulation to 1.7 × 10⁻⁷, and loses ρ·(ω·h)² of its energy every step. Baumgarte, which 8.10 showed throwing crates out of floors, turns out to conserve a turning joint's energy — and to leave 0.35 J of spin in a limb pulled back into its socket. And one number kept arriving, three times, in three different costumes: the parallel-axis ratio ρ = m·d² / (Icm + m·d²). It sets a swinging body's period, the energy a velocity joint loses, and the rate at which a hinge limit converges against its pin — 0.7481 per sweep for a door, measured to four figures, which is why a door with no restitution still bounces off its stop.

1. The problem: a solver that cannot pull

Here is where Lesson 8.10 left the engine. It can drop a crate on a floor and have it land, stop, settle at the slop and go to sleep. It can stack five crates and hold them, partition a yard of towers into islands, and stop paying for any of them once they are still. Every part of that is built on one operation, which 8.9 wrote in three lines and 8.10 wrapped in a loop: take the relative velocity of two bodies at a point, along a direction; divide the error by an effective mass to get an impulse; and clamp the accumulated impulse at zero, because a contact may push and may not pull.

Now hang a lamp from the ceiling. There is no contact between the lamp and the ceiling — they are not touching — and if there were one it would be no use, because what holds a lamp up is a pull. The same is true of a door on its hinges, a trailer on its hitch, a pendulum, a chain, and every limb of the ragdoll 8.12 is going to build. None of it can be said to this solver.

There are three obvious things to try, and each fails in a way that is worth knowing:

  1. A spring. Pull the lamp toward the ceiling with a force proportional to how far it has sagged. That is a penalty method, and 8.9 §1 already priced it: the stiffness you need to hold a small error is a stiffness whose oscillation demands a frame rate you do not have, and a spring tuned to hold a resting lamp still lets a swinging one stretch by centimetres. Nothing about joints makes that argument weaker.
  2. A teleport. After every step, move the lamp back to where the chain says it must be. The position is then right and the velocity is not: gravity keeps adding to it, the teleport never removes any, and a lamp corrected that way is falling faster every frame while appearing to stand still. (Correct the velocity from the displacement and you have invented position-based dynamics, which is a real and good method — and a different engine from this one.)
  3. A contact with the clamp taken off. This one works, and it is the whole of the lesson. 8.9's normal solve with max(0, …) deleted is an impulse that may push or pull along a direction, and pointed along a rod it holds a rod. What it does not do on its own is hold a door: a door needs five things held at once — its hinge pin in three directions, and its axis in two — and the harness in §9 measures what happens to a door given only the first three. It falls over, 176°, in five seconds.

So the job is to take 8.9's normal solve, find out which parts of it were about contacts and which were about constraints in general, and write the general part once. That is a Jacobian row, and the rest of this lesson is choosing rows.

2. Building intuition: a constraint is a question about velocity

Start from what a joint is, stated as geometry. A ball-and-socket says: the point on the lamp that is screwed to the chain, and the point on the chain it is screwed to, are the same point. Write that as a function of where the two bodies are:

\[ C(x_a, q_a, x_b, q_b) \;=\; p_b - p_a \;=\; (x_b + R_b\,\ell_b) - (x_a + R_a\,\ell_a) \;=\; 0 \] C = p_b - p_a = (x_b + R_b l_b) - (x_a + R_a l_a) = 0, with l the anchor in each body's own frame

Here x is a body's centre, R its orientation as a matrix, and ℓ the anchor point in the body's own frame — the place the screw goes, which does not move relative to the body. A rod says the distance between two such points is L. A contact says the separation along the normal is at least zero. Every constraint in this lesson is a function C of the bodies' positions and orientations that the solver must keep at zero (an equality) or keep non-negative (an inequality).

But the solver does not move positions. It changes velocities, and positions change because velocities are not zero. So the useful question is not "what is C" but "how fast is C changing, given the velocities the bodies have right now". Differentiate: ẋ is the velocity v, and the derivative of R ℓ is ω × (R ℓ), which is the lever arm r swept round by the angular velocity — the same v + ω × r that 8.2 called point_velocity. So

\[ \dot C \;=\; (v_b + \omega_b \times r_b) - (v_a + \omega_a \times r_a) \] dC/dt = (v_b + w_b x r_b) - (v_a + w_a x r_a)

and the thing to notice is that this is linear in the twelve numbers V = (v_a, ω_a, v_b, ω_b). Double every velocity and Ċ doubles. A linear function of twelve numbers is a row of twelve coefficients, and that row has a name:

\[ \dot C \;=\; J\,V, \qquad J = \bigl[\;\text{linear}_a \;\big|\; \text{angular}_a \;\big|\; \text{linear}_b \;\big|\; \text{angular}_b\;\bigr] \] dC/dt = J V, with J a row of twelve numbers in four blocks of three

The Jacobian. For a scalar constraint — one number that must be zero — it is one row of twelve; for the ball-socket's three-component C it is three rows. To read off a row, take one component of Ċ along a direction d and turn each cross product round with the scalar triple product, d · (ω × r) = (r × d) · ω:

\[ d\cdot\dot C \;=\; (-d)\cdot v_a \;+\; (-\,r_a \times d)\cdot\omega_a \;+\; d\cdot v_b \;+\; (r_b \times d)\cdot\omega_b \] d . dC/dt = (-d).v_a + (-(r_a x d)).w_a + d.v_b + (r_b x d).w_b

so the row that constrains two material points along a direction d is J = [−d, −(r_a × d), d, r_b × d]. Hold on to that shape; it is going to be a contact normal, a rod, a rope and each axis of a ball-socket, and they will differ only in which d they use and what they are allowed to do.

The impulse is Jᵀλ, and that is not a choice

Now the solver has to push. It could apply any combination of impulses to the two bodies — a twelve-number vector P — so why should the answer be one number? Because of a property every real joint has: an ideal constraint does no work on any motion it allows. A hinge pin does not slow a door that is turning about the pin; a rod does not slow a pendulum that is swinging on its arc. The motions a constraint allows are exactly the V with J·V = 0, and the work an impulse does on a motion is P·V. So P must satisfy P·V = 0 for every V with J·V = 0. The vectors orthogonal to everything orthogonal to J are the multiples of J — that is the whole of the linear algebra — and so

\[ P \;=\; J^{\mathsf T}\lambda \] P = J^T * lambda: the impulse points along the row, and lambda is one scalar

An entire constraint's response is one scalar, λ, in a direction the row already names. For a contact that was obvious — the impulse is along the normal, at the contact point — and now it is a theorem.

The effective mass is 1/(J M⁻¹ Jᵀ)

Apply P = Jᵀλ and each body's velocity changes by M⁻¹P, where M⁻¹ is the block-diagonal inverse mass: 1/m_a on v_a, the world inverse inertia tensor I_a⁻¹ on ω_a, and the same for b. So the rate J·V changes by

\[ \Delta(J V) \;=\; J\,M^{-1} J^{\mathsf T}\,\lambda \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \lambda \;=\; \frac{\text{target} - J V}{J\,M^{-1} J^{\mathsf T}} \] change in JV = (J M^-1 J^T) lambda, so lambda = (target - JV) / (J M^-1 J^T)

J M⁻¹ Jᵀ is a 1×1 matrix — a number — and for the point row it expands to 1/m_a + 1/m_b + (r_a×d)·I_a⁻¹(r_a×d) + (r_b×d)·I_b⁻¹(r_b×d). That is 8.9's effective_mass exactly. 8.9's header called the scalar effective mass "the reason it is a scalar is the whole trick"; the trick, seen from here, is that a one-row Jacobian makes J M⁻¹ Jᵀ one by one. §7 will meet a three-row Jacobian, where it is a 3×3 matrix and the trick stops being free.

Worked, with real numbers. A 10 kg crate of half-extent 0.25 m rests on a fixed floor, and we take the contact at one bottom corner. The floor is body a, so its 1/m and I⁻¹ are zero and only b's halves matter. The normal is n = (0, 1, 0) and the lever arm is r_b = (0.25, −0.25, 0.25), so r_b × n = (−0.25, 0, 0.25), of squared length 0.125. A cube's inertia about any axis through its centre is m·(0.5² + 0.5²)/12 = 0.4167 kg·m², so I⁻¹ is 2.4 times the identity and J M⁻¹ Jᵀ = 1/10 + 2.4 × 0.125 = 0.1 + 0.3 = 0.4. The contact's effective mass is 1/0.4 = 2.5 kg — a quarter of the crate. Three quarters of a push at a corner goes into turning the crate rather than lifting it, and that is the same 0.3 against 0.1 that 8.10 §2 found coupling the four points of one manifold.

What a constraint is allowed to do is two numbers

Solving a row is then 8.9's three lines with the contact taken out: measure J·V, compute the λ that would move it onto its target, add it to the accumulated impulse, clamp the accumulated impulse into the row's bounds, and apply whatever change that leaves. Everything that distinguishes one kind of constraint from another is in the row's direction and in those bounds:

  • A contact may push and not pull: [0, ∞).
  • A rod may do either: (−∞, ∞) — a contact with the clamp taken off.
  • A rope may pull and not push: the contact's row with its direction reversed, so [0, ∞) again, measured the other way.
  • A motor may push or pull up to a limit it has the torque for: [−τh, τh], with a target that is its speed rather than zero.
  • And friction, which 8.9 wrote as a clip to μ times the normal impulse, is a motor whose target speed is zero.

And in between, three sections are about what happens when you solve any of these at the velocity level and then move the bodies — because a row keeps Ċ at its target and says nothing at all about C, and §5 is about what that costs.

3. A contact is a row

The claim of §2 is strong, and it is checkable: 8.9's normal solve is not like a Jacobian row, it is one, with d = n and bounds [0, ∞), and every number it computes should come out of point_row and prepare_row to rounding. The harness settles a yard of fifty crates in ten towers, takes the manifolds of one step, and prepares every contact twice — once through 8.9's prepare_contacts, once as rows built from the same normal and lever arms — at the state the solver actually sees: after the velocity half of a step, when every resting body carries this step's g·h.

     worst |J M^-1 J^T  vs  tangent_mass|      1.887e-07 (relative)
     worst |J V  vs  8.9's relative velocity|  3.448e-08 m/s

  EIGHT SWEEPS OF THE WHOLE YARD, BOTH WAYS, from identical copies.
  engine `solve_contacts` (friction off) against engine `solve_row`:
     worst |v difference|    2.608e-08 m/s   (largest |v| 1.100e-01)
     worst |w difference|    1.484e-07 rad/s

The effective masses agree to 1.9 × 10⁻⁷ — two different orders of the same arithmetic, rounded differently — and so do the friction rows, which are the same point_row along the two tangents. The velocities after eight full sweeps of the yard agree to 2.6 × 10⁻⁸ m/s on velocities of 0.11. A contact normal is a Jacobian row.

Two controls, one for each half of the row. First, delete the angular halves — treat a contact as a push on the centre of mass, which is the most common first mistake — and the effective mass comes out wrong by between 3.799× and 4.032× over the same 200 points. §2's worked example predicted 4.000 at a corner: 0.1 against 0.4. Second, flip the row's sign. The equation J·V = 0 does not care, because it is the same equation; the clamp does, because [0, ∞) is now on the pulling side. 

     J              lowest crate centre    fell by      rows with impulse
     as derived             0.2172 m        0.0269 m      198 of 200
     negated               -0.5395 m        0.7836 m      112 of 280
     free fall over the same 0.5 s is g t^2 / 2 = 1.2263 m.

As derived, the rows hold the yard up — to 27 mm of sink in half a second, which is the cold-start sink 8.10 §4 measured, because this loop has neither warm starting nor a position correction. Negated, the yard falls 784 mm through the floor, and every row that acts is pulling two separating surfaces together. The first draft of this control counted flipped rows that pushed, and could not be made to say anything: in a stack, gravity adds the same g·h to both crates of a crate-on-crate contact, so only the floor contacts are clearly approaching and the rest are noise either side of zero. A consequence was the honest instrument; a count was not.

What the general form costs, which refused its prediction

The lesson was handed a prediction: the hand-written contact is three dot products and the general row is twelve multiplies, so rewriting the contact solver as rows would make it slower, and should be measured before anyone tries. It was measured, both arms as harness-local copies behind the same noinline boundary — 8.8 §7 timed a strictly more expensive hash as three times faster because one arm crossed into the library and the other did not. 

     hand-written (8.9)   11.953 ns     76 bytes per point
     Jacobian row          8.750 ns    108 bytes per point
     ratio row / hand      0.732

The row is 27% faster. The prediction counted the wrong half of the work. Measuring J·V is indeed four dot products against 8.9's two cross products and a dot — twelve multiplies against fifteen, nearly a wash. The difference is in applying the impulse. 8.9 applies it through apply_impulse_pair, which computes I⁻¹(r × J) for each body on every visit: two cross products and two matrix-vector products. A prepared row stores I⁻¹ times its angular halves — the angular half of M⁻¹Jᵀ — so applying λ is four scaled adds. Figure 2 counts them: 54 multiplies against 26. The general form is faster not because it is general but because it makes a hoist 8.9 never made, and it pays 32 bytes a point for the privilege.

So why does solve_contacts stay hand-written? Because the saving belongs to the hoist, not to the rewrite, and the hoist can be made inside 8.9's own struct — one more vec3 per lever arm — without touching anything else. And either change moves, at the rounding level, every number 8.9 and 8.10 printed: every residual, every sink, every sleep time. Those pages are an archive of what shipped, and their numbers should stay reproducible by the lesson that owns them. The hoist is §18's third exercise, with this table as the target to beat.

4. Rods and ropes

A distance joint is one point row along the line between its two anchors, u = (p_b − p_a)/|p_b − p_a|, with C = |p_b − p_a| − L. If the joint's two lengths are equal it is a rod: the row is bilateral, bounds (−∞, ∞), and it is exactly a contact with the clamp taken off. If its minimum length is zero it is a rope, and the rope's row is the interesting one.

A contact keeps a separation from going below zero. A rope keeps a distance from going above its length. Write the rope's constraint the way a contact is written — a quantity that must stay non-negative — and it is C = L − |p_b − p_a| ≥ 0, whose rate is −u along the line. So the rope's row is point_row(−u) with bounds [0, ∞): a contact turned inside out, and the minus sign is the whole of the turn. The engine's code for it is the same one-sided row as a hinge limit, which is §10's subject, including the speculative target that makes a slack rope catch its load without overshooting.

The rope's behaviour has a sharp prediction attached, which is the reason for choosing this fixture. Swing a bob on a joint of length L from the bottom with v₀² = 3.5·g·L — enough to rise above the pivot, not enough to go over the top. At height y above the pivot, energy gives v² = v₀² − 2g(L + y), and the joint's tension is m(v²/L − g·y/L): the centripetal requirement minus the part of gravity along the rope. Tension reaches zero where v² = g·y, which is

\[ y^{*} \;=\; \frac{v_0^2 - 2gL}{3g} \;=\; \frac{3.5\,gL - 2\,gL}{3g} \;=\; \frac{L}{2} \] y* = (v0^2 - 2gL)/(3g) = L/2 above the pivot

A rope goes slack there — its row's accumulated impulse hits the [0, ∞) bound and stays — and the bob leaves the circle on a parabola. (That parabola has a small surprise in it: released from (√3/2, 1/2)·L with v² = gL/2 along the tangent, it reaches x = 0 at t = √3·√(L)/v, where y = L/2 + 3L/2 − 3L = −L — the bob lands exactly on the lowest point of the circle, under the pin, and the rope snaps taut again where the swing began.) A rod cannot go slack: its row's impulse passes through zero and changes sign, from tension to compression, at exactly the same height, and the bob carries on round the circle to y = 3L/4, where energy runs out. Two different instruments — one row hitting a bound, another row changing sign — should report one fact. 

     rate      rope lets go at    rod turns at     rod peaks at   energy left
       60 Hz          0.4134 m        0.4134 m        0.5574 m        89.87%
      240 Hz          0.4819 m        0.4819 m        0.6990 m        97.32%
      960 Hz          0.4956 m        0.4956 m        0.7371 m        99.32%
     exact             0.5000 m        0.5000 m        0.7500 m      100.00%

The two instruments agree with each other to four decimals at every rate: the rope lets go exactly where the rod starts to push. And both disagree with the closed form at 60 Hz, by 17%, which is the measurement refusing the section's claim — but it refuses in a very particular way. The error has a sign (always low), it shrinks as the step shrinks (3.6% at 240 Hz, 0.9% at 960), and the last column says why: the bob reaches the release point with 10% less energy than it started with at 60 Hz. Less energy, lower release. Nothing about ropes did that. Something about solving a joint at the velocity level did, and it is §5.

5. Why a joint drifts

A row keeps Ċ on its target. It says nothing about C. That was harmless for a contact at rest, because a resting contact's geometry does not change within a step. It is not harmless for anything that turns, and the reason is one picture.

Take a bob on a rod, turning about a fixed pin. The velocity solve makes the bob's velocity exactly perpendicular to the rod: the rod is neither lengthening nor shortening, at this instant. Then the position update moves the bob by v·h along that velocity for the whole step — along a straight line. A straight line tangent to a circle leaves the circle. Pythagoras says by how much:

\[ r_{n+1} \;=\; \sqrt{\,r_n^2 + (v_n h)^2\,}, \qquad \delta \;=\; r_{n+1} - r_n \;\approx\; \frac{v^2 h^2}{2r} \] r(n+1) = sqrt(r(n)^2 + (v(n) h)^2); the drift is about v^2 h^2 / (2r)

The small-step form has a physical reading: v²/r is the centripetal acceleration, and δ = ½·(v²/r)·h² is how far the bob would have "fallen outward" in one step if the rod had let go of it — which, for the duration of a position update, it has. And the next step has a second consequence. The bob is now slightly outside the circle and moving along the old tangent, which is no longer perpendicular to the rod; the solve removes the radial component of its velocity. Removing a radial velocity leaves r × v alone, so

\[ v_{n+1}\,r_{n+1} \;=\; v_n\,r_n \qquad\text{(angular momentum about the pin, exactly)} \] v(n+1) r(n+1) = v(n) r(n): angular momentum about the pin is conserved exactly

and the kinetic energy, ½mv², falls as 1/r² while the rod stretches. Those two lines are a recurrence that can be iterated in double precision with nothing from the engine in it, and compared against the engine one step at a time.

Worked. At 5 m/s on a 1 m rod at 60 Hz, v·h = 0.08333 m, so the first step lands at √(1 + 0.006944) = 1.003466 m — measured 1.003466. Sixty such steps stretch the rod by 16% in one second, and the recurrence tracks every one of them to 1.7 × 10⁻⁷. The energy after a second is 0.740938 of the start against a predicted 0.740937 — and the first draft of that check missed by 0.4%, because it paired the velocity at the end of a step with the final radius. The velocity a step ends with was solved at the radius the step started with, one drift earlier. An instrument that reads two quantities from different instants is an instrument with an error of one step in it.

For a body, only the orbit is projected

A bead on a rod loses (ω·h)² of its kinetic energy per step — that is what 1/r² amounts to, one step at a time, with ω = v/r. A rigid body on a pin is a bead plus a spin: its centre orbits the pin, and it also turns about its own centre. The point constraint's projection touches only the orbit. So the energy it removes should be (ω·h)² times the fraction of the kinetic energy that is orbital, and that fraction has a name older than this course. The body's inertia about the pin is I_cm + m·d² by the parallel-axis theorem 8.3 derived; the orbit is the m·d² part; so

\[ \rho \;=\; \frac{m\,d^2}{I_{\text{cm}} + m\,d^2}, \qquad \frac{\Delta E}{E} \;=\; \rho\,(\omega h)^2 \ \text{per step} \] rho = m d^2 / (I_cm + m d^2); the fraction of energy lost per step is rho (w h)^2

     socket, d/2   0.7481        0.002986        0.002987    0.9997
     socket, d     0.9224        0.003597        0.003598    0.9998
     hinge,  d/4   0.4261        0.001778        0.001779    0.9994
     hinge,  d/2   0.7481        0.002986        0.002987    0.9997

To 0.1% on six configurations, under both joint kinds. lever_ratio computes ρ and projection_loss_per_step computes the loss, and this is the first of three appearances ρ makes in this lesson. At 60 Hz it is not small: a joint turning at 5 rad/s has ω·h = 0.083, and a point-like body on it loses 0.7% of its energy every step. That is what took 10% out of §4's bob before it reached the release point.

This is not a bug, and there is no line of code to fix. It is what a velocity-level constraint is: the solve is exact at the start of the step and the motion is a straight line for the rest of it. Every sequential-impulse engine has it, and the honest statement is that it is an energy loss of ρ(ωh)² per step. That is second order in the step, and a second has 1/h steps in it, so the loss over any fixed time is first order: quarter the step and you quarter it, which is what §4's release height did (17% low at 60 Hz, 3.6% at 240, 0.9% at 960) — and why §14 ends on sub-stepping. It reads as damping, and a game can live with damping. What §6 asks is whether the position correction makes it better or worse.

The control is a rod that does not turn: the same rod between two bodies moving together at 5 m/s. Same speed, no curvature, and after a second its length error is 0.000e+00 m — exactly zero. The drift is not a property of speed. It is a property of turning.

6. Correcting a joint, and what Baumgarte does to one

A joint that drifts needs a position correction, and 8.10 built two. Both compute a bias velocity −β·C/h — "remove a fraction β of the error this step" — and differ only in which velocity it is applied to. Baumgarte adds it to the target of the ordinary velocity solve, so the body gains a real velocity; split impulse drives a shadow velocity that moves the position at the end of the step and is then thrown away. 8.10 §7 measured Baumgarte throwing a crate spawned 200 mm inside a floor 99 mm into the air, and settled on split impulse as the default. This section set out to show the same failure on a joint.

First the part that is the same. Each step removes a fraction β of the error and the motion adds a drift δ, so the error obeys e_{n+1} = (1 − β)·e_n + δ, whose fixed point is where what is removed equals what is added:

\[ e^{*} \;=\; (1-\beta)\,e^{*} + \delta \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad e^{*} \;=\; \frac{\delta}{\beta} \] e* = (1 - beta) e* + delta, so e* = delta / beta

A turning joint does not sit at zero error under either correction; it sits stretched by five times its per-step drift at β = 0.2. §5's rod again, with the corrections on: 

     correction      stretch at 1 s     delta/beta     |v| at 1 s    energy per step
     none                163.9531 mm       0.0000 mm      4.3039 m/s     0.00386  (w h)^2 0.00380
     Baumgarte            16.5538 mm      16.5268 mm      4.9226 m/s     0.00000  (w h)^2 0.00651
     split impulse        12.4993 mm      12.2235 mm      4.2241 m/s     0.00488  (w h)^2 0.00483

The stretch lands on δ/β under both: 16.55 mm against 16.53 predicted, 12.50 against 12.22. So far, so expected. The last column is the one that refused the section. Without a correction the rod loses (ω·h)² of its energy per step, exactly §5's projection. Under split impulse it loses (ω·h)² per step too — 0.00488 against 0.00483 — because split impulse moves the bob back onto the circle without touching its velocity, and the projection happens anyway. Under Baumgarte the rod loses nothing. A second in, it is still turning at 4.92 m/s where split impulse has let it fall to 4.22.

The reason is Figure 5's picture, and it is the kind of result a derivation would not have gone looking for. At the steady state Baumgarte's bias is a real inward velocity of β·e*/h = δ/h. Next step the rod has turned through ω·h, so a fraction ω·h of that inward velocity now lies along the new tangent:

\[ \frac{\delta}{h}\cdot\omega h \;=\; \frac{v^2 h}{2r}\cdot\frac{v h}{r} \;=\; v\,\frac{(\omega h)^2}{2} \] (delta/h)(w h) = v (w h)^2 / 2

which is precisely the speed the projection removes, since v·(1 − cos ωh) ≈ v(ωh)²/2. Baumgarte adds energy — 8.10 was right about that — and on a joint that is turning, it adds it exactly where the velocity projection takes it away. The stretch δ/β is, in effect, the joint storing the centripetal term a velocity-level constraint leaves out, and feeding it back one step late.

And the dislocated limb

So is Baumgarte the better correction for joints? Not on the evidence of the other half of this section. 8.10's crate left the floor because a contact stops acting once the overlap is gone, and the body keeps the last bias velocity it was given. A joint never stops acting, so the bias should decay with the error — and along the joint's constrained directions it does. The question is the directions a joint leaves free. The fixture is a limb spawned out of its socket, which a ragdoll does every time an animation hands it a pose the joints disagree with: a 0.5 m, 10 kg cube, gravity off, held by a ball-socket at one corner from an anchor 0.2 m away.

Baumgarte's pull on the corner is a real impulse applied off the centre of mass, so it spins the cube, and a ball-socket has no row that resists spin. The energy lands in the free degrees of freedom and stays there: 0.35 J of rotation the cube was never given, forever, because nothing damps a ball-socket. Split impulse closes the same gap with a velocity it throws away and leaves the cube exactly at rest. The control puts the socket through the centre: no lever arm, nothing to spin, and Baumgarte leaves nothing behind either — which is the confirmation that the energy went into the free directions and not into the constrained one.

So the two corrections fail differently, and the engine has to choose. Split impulse adds no energy to anything and costs every turning joint ρ(ωh)² per step. Baumgarte conserves a turning joint's energy and invents energy in any joint that is pulled apart. A joint that loses energy reads on screen as damping; a joint that invents it reads as a limb twitching on a body lying still, which is the bug report every ragdoll system receives. The default stays split impulse, for joints and contacts alike, and §8 puts a number on what that costs a pendulum. Letting joints choose their own correction is §18's second exercise.

7. The ball-socket, and the block

A ball-socket holds two anchor points together: C = p_b − p_a = 0, three components, three rows. Each is the point row of §2 along one world axis, J_k = [−e_k, −(r_a × e_k), e_k, r_b × e_k] — a ball-socket is literally three contacts along x, y and z with their clamps removed. Solve them one after another and it works. It also leaves something behind, for a reason 8.10 §2 already met on a crate's four contact points: the rows are coupled. An impulse along x at an anchor off the centre of mass spins the body, and the spin moves the anchor in y and z, so solving the x row disturbs the other two.

Stack the three rows and J M⁻¹ Jᵀ stops being a number. It is a 3×3 matrix, and expanding it with 8.3's cross-product matrix [r]ₓ (the matrix whose action is v ↦ r × v) gives

\[ K \;=\; J M^{-1} J^{\mathsf T} \;=\; \Bigl(\tfrac{1}{m_a} + \tfrac{1}{m_b}\Bigr)\,\mathbf 1 \;-\; [r_a]_\times\, I_a^{-1}\, [r_a]_\times \;-\; [r_b]_\times\, I_b^{-1}\, [r_b]_\times \] K = (1/m_a + 1/m_b) 1 - [r_a]x Ia^-1 [r_a]x - [r_b]x Ib^-1 [r_b]x

The first term is diagonal: a push through the centre of mass moves the anchor along the push and nowhere else. The other two are not, and their off-diagonal entries are exactly the coupling. Solving the rows one at a time is Gauss–Seidel on K·λ = −Ċ, and Gauss–Seidel's error after each sweep is multiplied by a fixed matrix whose largest eigenvalue — its spectral radius — is computable from K alone, without running anything. Solving it as a block is one matrix inverse at prepare time and one matrix–vector product per visit:

\[ \lambda \;=\; K^{-1}\,\bigl(\text{bias} - \dot C\bigr) \] lambda = K^-1 (bias - dC/dt), a vector impulse applied at the two anchors

Worked. §2's crate on a fixed anchor at its corner, r_b = (0.25, −0.25, 0.25), 1/m = 0.1, I⁻¹ = 2.4·1. For a multiple of the identity, −[r]ₓ(c·1)[r]ₓ = c·(|r|²·1 − r rᵀ), the identity 8.3 §4 checked the inertia tensor with. With |r|² = 0.1875 the diagonal of K is 0.1 + 2.4 × (0.1875 − 0.0625) = 0.4 — each axis alone is §2's 2.5 kg contact — and the off-diagonals are −2.4 × r_i r_j = ±0.15. A diagonal of 0.4 against off-diagonals of 0.15 is strong coupling: the Gauss–Seidel iteration matrix of that K has a spectral radius of 0.568, so three rows solved in turn leave 57% of the error after each visit — about eight visits to reach 1%, where the block needs one. A cube hung by its corner is close to the worst case: the thousand random sockets below never exceed 0.574.

The rows contract at 0.2323 per visit against a spectral radius computed from K of 0.2325: the convergence of a Gauss–Seidel solve is a property of its effective-mass matrix, and can be predicted before a single impulse is applied. The block is at 1.2 × 10⁻⁷ after one visit and exactly zero after three. Over a thousand random sockets — random box, mass, pose, corner and velocity — one visit of the block leaves at worst 5.9 × 10⁻⁷ of the violation it started with; one visit of the rows leaves up to 92% of it, with a median radius of 0.443 and a worst of 0.574.

Two further measurements decide what ships. The block is cheaper as well as exact — 10.8 ns a visit against 23.1, and 58 ns to prepare against 65, because one 3×3 inverse is less work than three row preparations and one matrix–vector product is less than three row solves. And the control: put the socket through the body's centre of mass, r = 0, and K is 1/m times the identity. Nothing couples, Gauss–Seidel's radius is zero, and the rows are exact in one visit too (9.7 × 10⁻⁸). The coupling lives in the lever arms, and so does everything the block is for.

A block is affordable here and not for contacts, and the reason is the clamp. The three rows of a socket are equalities: λ may be any vector, so the block is a linear solve. A manifold's four normal rows are inequalities, each λ ≥ 0, so a block of them is a small linear complementarity problem — Box2D solves the two-point case by enumerating its four sign patterns, and nobody ships the four-point one. That is 8.10's open question about block solvers, answered for the case where the answer is easy. The engine solves a socket's point as a 3×3 block and a hinge's two alignment rows as a 2×2 one, and keeps the switch (joint_config::block_solve) so that this section can measure the difference.

And the honest limit of the block: it makes each joint exact and does nothing for a chain of them. On a twelve-link chain released from horizontal, eight sweeps leave a worst anchor gap of 94 mm with the block against 102 with rows. A sweep still carries information across one joint at a time, which is 8.10's tower lying on its side, and §14's subject.

8. The pendulum, and the parallel axis

A physical pendulum is the best test an engine's joints can be given, because its period has a closed form that contains the mass, the inertia tensor, the joint and gravity, and gets every one of them wrong in a different, recognisable way. Hang a 0.2 × 1.0 × 0.2 m, 2 kg box from a ball-socket at the middle of its top face, so its centre is d = 0.5 m below the pin. A point mass at the same distance would swing with T = 2π√(d/g). A body swings about the pin, so its inertia is the inertia about the pin:

\[ T \;=\; 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{I_{\text{pivot}}}{m\,g\,d}}, \qquad I_{\text{pivot}} \;=\; I_{\text{cm}} + m\,d^2 \] T = 2 pi sqrt(I_pivot / (m g d)), I_pivot = I_cm + m d^2

Worked. About the swing axis the box's inertia is m·(w² + h²)/12 = 2 × (0.04 + 1.0)/12 = 0.173333 kg·m². The parallel-axis term adds m·d² = 2 × 0.25 = 0.5, so I_pivot = 0.673333. Then T = 2π√(0.673333 / (2 × 9.81 × 0.5)) = 2π × 0.261990 = 1.646116 s. The point-mass formula gives 2π√(0.5/9.81) = 1.418503 s: 13.8% short, at every amplitude, because it is missing the box's own inertia about its centre.

A large swing, and the loveliest formula in the lesson

The exact period at amplitude θ₀ is the small-swing period times (2/π)·K(sin θ₀/2), where K is the complete elliptic integral of the first kind — a function most people meet once and look up forever after. It has a much better form. Gauss showed that K(k) = π / (2·AGM(1, √(1 − k²))), where AGM is the arithmetic–geometric mean: replace two numbers by their arithmetic and geometric means, repeatedly, and they converge — quadratically — to a common value. With k = sin(θ₀/2), √(1 − k²) = cos(θ₀/2), and the π's cancel:

\[ T(\theta_0) \;=\; \frac{T_0}{\operatorname{AGM}\bigl(1,\ \cos(\theta_0/2)\bigr)} \] T(theta0) = T0 / AGM(1, cos(theta0/2))

Worked. At 90°: cos 45° = 0.707107. The means are (0.853553, 0.840896), then (0.847225, 0.847201), then equal to six figures at 0.847213. So a 90° swing takes 1/0.847213 = 1.180341 times as long as a small one: 1.646116 × 1.180341 = 1.942977 s. pendulum_period_at is those dozen lines.

9. The hinge

A hinge is a ball-socket that also refuses to let its two bodies tilt relative to each other about anything but its axis. The socket takes three degrees of freedom away; the hinge takes two more, and leaves one rotation. The two new rows are pure angular rows — they involve no linear velocity at all — and they say: the relative angular velocity ω_b − ω_a may point along the axis and nowhere else. Pick two unit vectors p₀, p₁ perpendicular to the axis and the rows are

\[ J_i \;=\; \bigl[\;0 \;\big|\; -p_i \;\big|\; 0 \;\big|\; p_i\;\bigr], \qquad J_i V \;=\; p_i\cdot(\omega_b - \omega_a) \;=\; 0 \] J_i = [0, -p_i, 0, p_i], so J_i V = p_i . (w_b - w_a) = 0

and their position error comes from the two copies of the axis the joint stores — one in each body's frame, a₁ as body a carries it and b₁ as body b does. When the hinge is healthy they coincide. When they drift apart, their cross product a₁ × b₁ is (for a small misalignment) the rotation vector that carries one onto the other, and its components along p₀ and p₁ are the two angles the hinge must not allow: C_i = (a₁ × b₁)·p_i. Differentiate and you get J_i·V back, which is the check that the error and the row agree. The two rows share a body and are coupled through its inertia tensor just as a socket's three are, so the engine solves them as a 2×2 block, K_ij = p_i·(I_a⁻¹ + I_b⁻¹)·p_j, with no linear terms at all: turning a body about its centre moves its centre nowhere. 

     joint              worst anchor gap    worst misalignment    worst tilt
     hinge                    0.088 mm           0.0001 deg         0.00 deg
     ball-socket only           4.694 mm           0.0000 deg       175.98 deg

A door given 2 rad/s about its vertical hinge stays within 0.0001° of true for five seconds of swinging. The same door on the socket alone — the three rows, without the two — falls over, 176°. That is the control and it is also the definition: the socket holds the point, and gravity's torque about the point is exactly what the two angular rows exist to answer.

The frame the angular impulse is cached in

The two rows' accumulated impulses are warm-started like everything else, and 8.10 §4 is the reason to be careful about how. There, two cached friction impulses turned out to be coordinates in a tangent basis that tangent_basis rebuilt every frame from a world normal, choosing its seed from the normal's smallest component. On a near-vertical normal two of its components are both near zero and cross constantly, the basis flips by 90°, and last frame's friction was applied sideways. A hinge's two perpendicular impulses have exactly the same shape, and a hinge axis along a coordinate axis has exactly the same near-zero components.

The engine avoids it twice over. The basis is chosen from the axis in body a's own frame — a constant, so the choice is a constant — and rotated into the world with the body, so it turns exactly as smoothly as the body does. And the cached impulse is stored as a world vector (joint::angular_impulse), which carries its own frame and is simply re-measured in this step's basis. Either would have been enough. The harness probes what the obvious way would have done, on a twelve-plank bridge hinged about z, sagging under a bouncing ball: 

     basis chosen from           rotated > 30 deg      worst step-to-step
     the world axis (8.10's way)     127 of 7787            90.000 deg
     body a's own axis (8.11)          0 of 7787             9.767 deg

127 flips, every one exactly 90°, against none. A plank moving in the x–y plane carries a z axis whose x and y components are rounding noise either side of zero — the precise condition 8.10 found on a crate's normal. The body-chosen basis's worst step-to-step turn is 9.8°, which is the plank itself turning under an impact. A cached quantity must carry its frame was 8.10's lesson, learned from a bug; here it was a design input, and the probe says what it was worth.

10. Limits: a contact that is always detected

A hinge limit is a one-sided angular row about the axis. For the upper stop, C = θ_max − θ ≥ 0, with θ the hinge angle, and the row pushes only one way, λ ≥ 0. It is a contact on an angle, and it would inherit a contact's defect: 8.10 §6 measured that a contact is only noticed on the step after the bodies cross, so it arrives with an overlap that is uniform on [0, v·h]. A limit that exists only once it is violated arrives with an overshoot uniform on [0, ω·h], for the same reason.

Except that a limit has one thing a contact does not: a hinge always knows its angle. So the row can exist before the stop is reached, with a target of −C/h — "you may close this gap, but no faster than it would take one step to close it" — and the [0, ∞) clamp makes it do nothing at all unless the door is approaching fast enough to overshoot this step. That is a speculative constraint, and for a contact it would need the narrow phase to run on pairs that are not touching yet, which is why 8.10 left speculative contacts as an open problem and this lesson gets speculative limits for free.

The hinge angle itself is 7.4's quaternion read in the one way that ignores everything a limit should not see. The joint stores the relative orientation it was authored in, rest = conj(q_a)·q_b; the deviation from it is q = conj(q_a)·q_b·conj(rest), expressed in a's frame, and a pure turn about the axis by θ gives exactly q = (cos θ/2, sin θ/2 · axis). So θ = 2·atan2(q.v·axis, q.w), with q first moved to the hemisphere w ≥ 0 — the double cover again — and any swing of the axes, which the alignment rows prevent anyway, drops out of the dot product.

Overshoot past the stop, in units of ω·h, over 200 starting angles spaced evenly across one step's travel. The door, and the second appearance of ρ

That was a turnstile, hinged through its centre. Hinge the same slab at its edge — a door — and the speculative limit is no longer exact, for a reason that is worth deriving because it is not a defect of limits or of doors. It is a property of every angular row that shares a body with a pin.

The limit row is angular, J = [0, a, 0, −a], so its effective mass is the inertia about the body's centre, I_cm. It computes the change in ω that would stop the door at the stop if the door turned about its centre. But a door does not turn about its centre; it turns about the pin. Having changed ω, the row has left the door's centre moving at a velocity that no longer matches ω × r about the pin, so the pin row pulls it back into line — and in doing so gives back part of the change in ω. Work it through for a body on a fixed pin at distance d from its centre and the fraction given back is

\[ \rho \;=\; \frac{m\,d^2}{I_{\text{cm}} + m\,d^2} \] rho = m d^2 / (I_cm + m d^2), the fraction of the limit's change the pin undoes

so Gauss–Seidel between the limit and the pin contracts by ρ per sweep. It is §5's ρ — the parallel-axis ratio — arriving the second time, as a rate rather than a loss. For any uniform slab hinged at its edge, I_cm = m·W²/12 and d = W/2, so ρ = (1/4)/(1/12 + 1/4) = 3/4 whatever the door's size or mass. 11. Motors, and what friction was all along

A motor is the axis row with a target that is not zero — the speed it wants — and a symmetric clamp, |λ| ≤ τ_max·h, which is how much impulse its torque can supply in a step. That clamp is what makes it a motor and not a hinge welded to a spinning frame: a load heavier than the motor can turn simply stalls. While the motor is short of its speed the clamp is saturated, it applies exactly τ_max·h every step, and the spin-up should take

\[ t \;=\; \frac{I_{\text{pivot}}\,\omega}{\tau_{\max}} \] t = I_pivot * w / tau_max

with I_pivot the inertia about the pin — because once the clamp is deciding the impulse, the row's own I_cm-based effective mass never enters. That was the claim.

     through centre    0.8417 kg m2  0.000     0.6733 s       0.6733 s       0.6833 s
     door, at edge     3.3417 kg m2  0.748     2.6733 s       2.8847 s       2.8833 s

Through the centre, right to the step. At the edge, 7.9% slow — the fourth measurement in this lesson to refuse its section. The clamp really does apply τ_max·h per step. What the claim forgot is that the joint is also losing energy the whole time, at §5's rate: ρ(ωh)² of its kinetic energy per step, which is ½·ρ·h·ω² of its angular momentum per second. So the motor is fighting

\[ \frac{d\omega}{dt} \;=\; \frac{\tau_{\max}}{I_{\text{pivot}}} \;-\; \frac{\rho\,h}{2}\,\omega^3 \] dw/dt = tau_max / I_pivot - (rho h / 2) w^3

and integrating that predicts 2.8847 s against a measured 2.8833 — within a step. That is ρ's third appearance, and the one that ties the other two together: the loss a motor has to overcome is the same parallel-axis fraction that set the pendulum's period and the limit's convergence.

The stall is the cleaner test. A 1 m, 2 kg arm hinged at its end about a horizontal axis, held level, motor asked to lift it: gravity's torque about the pin is m·g·d = 9.8100 N·m, and bisecting the motor's torque over eighteen halvings for the smallest that lifts the arm finds 9.8100 N·m, to 4.8 × 10⁻⁶. The clamp is a torque, exactly.

Friction was this row all along

8.9's friction rows clip the tangential impulse to μ times the normal impulse. A motor's row clips to τ_max·h. Set a motor's target speed to zero and it is a brake with a fixed maximum torque — which is Coulomb friction in the hinge: a constant decelerating torque while the door slides, and exactly zero relative speed held once the required impulse falls inside the clamp, which is static friction from the same line of code. 

     tau          predicted stop    measured stop    speed after 5 s
      2.0 N m        3.2752 s        3.2833 s     7.29e-43 rad/s
      4.0 N m        1.6538 s        1.6833 s     1.40e-45 rad/s
      0.0 N m         never           never       1.7904 rad/s   (control: no brake)

The prediction includes §5's loss, which here helps the brake. The door stops within two steps of it and then stays stopped to 10⁻⁴³ rad/s. What lateness there is — under two steps at 4 N·m — is the last step, where the door is slower than τ·h can stop, so the brake is not saturated, and an unsaturated axis row on a door converges at §10's ρ per sweep. The control has no brake and is still turning at 1.7904 rad/s after five seconds. The missing speed is §5 once more, and with the motor's term deleted the equation above has a closed form, ω(t) = ω₀ / √(1 + ρ·h·ω₀²·t), which says 1.7894.

That is why contact_material — a restitution and a friction coefficient — stops being the right shape for "what a constraint may do". Friction is a bound derived from another row's impulse; a motor is a bound from a torque; a limit is a bound at zero on one side. They are all jacobian_row::lower and upper.

12. Islands, sleeping and joints

8.10 built islands on one rule: a contact is an edge of the island graph when both of its bodies can move, because a fixed body cannot propagate an impulse and would otherwise weld the level into one island. A joint is an edge by exactly the same rule — and in the code it is literally the same function, union_edge, lifted out of the contact loop so that joints could call it and the two could never disagree. 

     islands with joint edges       2   (the solver reports 2)
     islands without them          16

With joint edges, two chains hung from one ceiling are two islands: each chain is joined along its length, and the fixed ceiling joins nothing. Without them, every link is an island of one, because the links of a chain never touch — their joints hold them apart.

What that would cost is sleeping, and a chain is the case where it is guaranteed to bite. 8.10 §10 showed that a body slept on its own becomes, to everything else, an immovable body, and hangs in the air when its support is knocked away. A chain's links go quiet together, so they would sleep together under either rule — the difference only appears when something wakes one of them. 

     rule                 asleep before   awake after   moved > 1 mm   bottom swung
     per island (engine)        8 of 8          8 of 8         8 of 8     0.329 m
     per link                  8 of 8          1 of 8         1 of 8     0.129 m

Under the island rule the whole chain wakes and swings. Under a per-link rule — emulated, as 8.10 §10 did it, by making the other seven links fixed, which is not an approximation but the same statement in the type system — one link swings as a 0.25 m pendulum hung from a frozen chain in mid-air. (The first draft counted links that had moved more than a centimetre and reported seven of eight under the island rule: the top link, pinned to the ceiling, moves less than that. The direct instrument is whether each link is awake.)

13. Writing it

The code arrived in the order the sections did, and it is worth following in that order, because each layer is short once the one beneath it exists. There is one new pair of files, phys/constraint.hpp and .cpp, and it sits below the solver: it knows what a body's velocity and inverse inertia are and nothing about contacts, manifolds or the loop that will visit it. solver.hpp includes it; it includes nothing from the solver. That dependency direction is the physical shape of §3's claim — a contact is a special case of a row, not the other way round.

Step 1: a row

A row is the Jacobian's four blocks, the angular half of M⁻¹Jᵀ precomputed, an effective mass, and the two bounds that say what kind of constraint it is. Nothing in it knows whether it is a contact, a rope or a motor: 

struct jacobian_row
{
    vec3 linear_a{};
    vec3 angular_a{};
    vec3 linear_b{};
    vec3 angular_b{};

    vec3 inv_inertia_angular_a{};
    vec3 inv_inertia_angular_b{};

    float mass = 0.0f;
    float target = 0.0f;
    float bias = 0.0f;
    float error = 0.0f;
    float lower = -k_unbounded;
    float upper = k_unbounded;
    float impulse = 0.0f;
    float pseudo_impulse = 0.0f;

    std::uint8_t role = 0;
    std::uint8_t component = 0;
};

The four blocks are four vec3s rather than a float[12] because each is a quantity with a meaning — a direction, or a lever arm crossed with one — and every operation on a row is four dot products or four scaled adds, never a twelve-long loop that hides which half is which. Two constructors cover every row in the lesson, and §2 derived both: 

jacobian_row point_row(vec3 direction, vec3 r_a, vec3 r_b)
{
    jacobian_row row;
    row.linear_a = -direction;
    row.angular_a = -cross(r_a, direction);
    row.linear_b = direction;
    row.angular_b = cross(r_b, direction);
    return row;
}

jacobian_row angular_row(vec3 axis)
{
    jacobian_row row;
    row.angular_a = -axis;
    row.angular_b = axis;
    return row;
}

The minus signs are the whole of the correctness here. J·V is to be the rate at which C grows, and a's point velocity is subtracted; get one of them backwards and §3's second control describes the result.

Step 2: prepare a row, and solve it

prepare_row multiplies each angular half through by its body's world inverse inertia tensor and sums the four non-negative terms of J M⁻¹ Jᵀ. Then the solve, which is 8.9's three lines with the contact removed — and the one function in the file every other one calls: 

float solve_row(jacobian_row& row, float inv_mass_a, float inv_mass_b, vec3& v_a, vec3& w_a,
                vec3& v_b, vec3& w_b, float extra)
{
    const float jv = row_velocity(row, v_a, w_a, v_b, w_b);

    float delta = row.mass * (row.target + extra - jv);

    const float total = std::clamp(row.impulse + delta, row.lower, row.upper);
    delta = total - row.impulse;
    row.impulse = total;

    v_a = v_a + row.linear_a * (inv_mass_a * delta);
    w_a = w_a + row.inv_inertia_angular_a * delta;
    v_b = v_b + row.linear_b * (inv_mass_b * delta);
    w_b = w_b + row.inv_inertia_angular_b * delta;
    return delta;
}

Three things about it. It clamps the accumulated impulse, never the increment — 8.9's rule, and with [0, ∞) it is 8.9's clamp exactly, with (−∞, ∞) it is no clamp at all, and with [−τh, τh] it is a motor running out of torque. extra is added to the target, and the solver passes the row's bias there under Baumgarte and zero otherwise; that one argument is the entire difference between the corrections on the velocity side. And it takes four vec3& and no bodies. The velocity pass hands it a body's real velocities and the position pass hands it the pseudo ones, and a row cannot tell which it has — 8.10's solve_positions, which already took no bodies, finished.

Step 3: a joint is authored in the bodies' own frames

A joint's anchors and axes are stored in each body's own frame, because that is what a joint is: the hinge pin is screwed to the door, not to a place in the world. The make_* functions take the pose you want in world terms and convert it. A hinge stores its axis twice, once per body, because the whole job of its two alignment rows is to notice when those two copies stop agreeing: 

joint make_hinge(const rigid_body& a, const rigid_body& b, vec3 world_anchor, vec3 world_axis)
{
    joint j = make_ball_socket(a, b, world_anchor);
    j.kind = joint_kind::hinge;

    const vec3 axis = normalised(world_axis);
    j.axis_a = local_direction_of(a, axis);
    j.axis_b = local_direction_of(b, axis);
    return j;
}

make_ball_socket, which it builds on, also records rest = conjugate(q_a) * q_b — the relative orientation at which the hinge angle reads zero — so a door authored half open reads zero half open, and its limits are measured from the pose a level designer placed it in. The second half of the joint struct is its warm-start state, and every field of it is in a frame that cannot rotate out from under it: the point and perpendicular impulses as world vectors, and the limit and motor impulses as scalars along an axis fixed in body a.

Step 4: prepare a joint — rows, blocks, and one lambda for every one-sided row

prepare_joint is a switch on three kinds that mostly differs in which rows it emits. Every one-sided row — a rope's long end, a distance joint's short end, each end of a hinge limit — goes through one lambda, and it is §10's argument in eight lines: 

const auto one_sided = [&](jacobian_row row, float c, row_role role, float warm) {
    if (c > 0.0f && !cfg.speculative_limits) { return; }
    row.error = c;
    row.lower = 0.0f;
    row.upper = k_unbounded;
    row.target = c > 0.0f ? -c * inv_h : 0.0f;
    row.bias = c < 0.0f ? std::min(-cfg.baumgarte * c * inv_h, cfg.max_correction_speed) : 0.0f;
    finish(row, role, 0, warm);
};

C is the gap left. If it is still positive, the row exists anyway (speculation) with a target of −C/h; if it has closed, the target is zero and a bias pushes back out. Which is why the rope is two lines and the hinge limit is two lines: 

one_sided(point_row(-u, batch.r_a, batch.r_b), j.max_length - len,
          row_role::limit_upper, j.limit_impulse[1]);

one_sided(angular_row(a1), batch.angle - j.limit.lower, row_role::limit_lower,
          j.limit_impulse[0]);
one_sided(angular_row(-a1), j.limit.upper - batch.angle, row_role::limit_upper,
          j.limit_impulse[1]);

The point constraint — a socket's whole content and a hinge's pin — is where the block is built. Everything K needs is already in the batch, and 8.3 wrote skew for exactly this moment ("8.9's contact Jacobian is written in terms of [r]ₓ", its doc comment says, waiting): 

const mat3 sa = skew(batch.r_a);
const mat3 sb = skew(batch.r_b);
const float im = batch.inv_mass_a + batch.inv_mass_b;
const mat3 k = diagonal(vec3{im, im, im}) - sa * batch.inv_inertia_a * sa
               - sb * batch.inv_inertia_b * sb;

batch.point_mass = inverse(k);

inverse returns zeros for a singular matrix — 2.6's contract — and a singular K means two immovable bodies, for which zero is the right effective mass, exactly as 8.9's scalar version returned zero. The hinge's 2×2 alignment block is the same idea with no linear terms and an explicit inverse of a symmetric 2×2, and its perpendiculars come from perpendicular_pair applied to the axis in a's frame, then rotated out — §9's design, in three lines.

The rows are emitted in a deliberate order, and it is solved in that order: motor, then limits, then the alignment block, then the pin. The rows that may give way — a motor out of torque, a limit that lets go — go first, so the last thing each sweep satisfies is the thing that must never give way. A motor pushing into its own limit then loses to the limit, and a limit and a pin that disagree are settled in the pin's favour.

Step 5: solve a joint

for (int i = 0; i < batch.row_count; ++i)
{
    jacobian_row& row = batch.rows[i];
    report.impulse += std::abs(solve_row(row, batch.inv_mass_a, batch.inv_mass_b, va, wa, vb,
                                         wb, use_bias ? row.bias : 0.0f));
}

if (batch.angular_block)
{
    const vec3 w_rel = wb - wa;
    const float r0 = (use_bias ? batch.angular_bias[0] : 0.0f) - dot(batch.perp[0], w_rel);
    const float r1 = (use_bias ? batch.angular_bias[1] : 0.0f) - dot(batch.perp[1], w_rel);
    const float l0 = batch.angular_mass[0] * r0 + batch.angular_mass[1] * r1;
    const float l1 = batch.angular_mass[1] * r0 + batch.angular_mass[2] * r1;
    batch.angular_impulse[0] += l0;
    batch.angular_impulse[1] += l1;
    apply_angular_impulse(batch, wa, wb, l0, l1);
    report.impulse += std::abs(l0) + std::abs(l1);
}

if (batch.point_block)
{
    const vec3 cdot = anchor_velocity(batch, va, wa, vb, wb);
    const vec3 lambda = batch.point_mass * ((use_bias ? batch.point_bias : vec3{}) - cdot);
    batch.point_impulse = batch.point_impulse + lambda;
    apply_point_impulse(batch, va, wa, vb, wb, lambda);
    report.impulse += length(lambda);
}

No clamp on either block: a pin may push in any direction, which is what makes a block a linear solve. solve_joint_positions is the same three stages against the pseudo velocities and the bias, with the motor skipped — a motor has no position error, only a speed — and write_back copies every accumulator into the joint, zeroing the fields this step had no row for, so that a limit that was left is a limit that is forgotten.

Step 6: the solver holds joints

Four changes to 8.10's solver.hpp, and none of them touches the contact solve:

  • pseudo_velocity moved down into constraint.hpp, unchanged, because joints need it and joints sit below the loop. 8.10's comment on it had predicted the move: "8.11's joints will want the same array and will get it for free".
  • The class was renamed constraint_solver, with using contact_solver = constraint_solver; so that 8.10's demo and harness compile unchanged. It gained add(a, b, joint&) beside add(a, b, manifold&, material), a joint_pair beside contact_pair, and a range of joints on each island.
  • union_edge was lifted out of the contact loop so that joints could call it. There is exactly one statement of the rule "an edge is an edge only when both ends can move", and a contact and a joint cannot come to disagree about it.
  • Every stage that visits a manifold now visits the island's joints first: prepared and warm-started once, visited in each velocity sweep, visited in each position sweep under split impulse, written back at the end. 
for (int k = 0; k < isl.joint_count; ++k)
{
    const int ji = island_joints_[static_cast<std::size_t>(isl.first_joint + k)];
    const joint_pair& p = joints_[static_cast<std::size_t>(ji)];
    const joint_report r = solve_joint(bodies_[p.body_a], bodies_[p.body_b],
                                       joint_batches_[static_cast<std::size_t>(ji)],
                                       joint_bias);
    stats_.joint_residual = std::max(stats_.joint_residual, r.max_residual);
}

for (int k = 0; k < isl.contact_count; ++k)
{
    const int ci = island_contacts_[static_cast<std::size_t>(isl.first_contact + k)];
    const contact_pair& p = pairs_[static_cast<std::size_t>(ci)];
    const solve_report r = solve_contacts(bodies_[p.body_a], bodies_[p.body_b],
                                          batches_[static_cast<std::size_t>(ci)], cfg);
    stats_.max_residual = std::max(stats_.max_residual, r.max_residual);
    stats_.normal_impulse += r.normal_impulse;
    stats_.friction_impulse += r.friction_impulse;
}

Joints before contacts is Box2D's order, and in this engine it is argued rather than measured: a contact is the constraint whose violation is least forgivable on screen — a limb through a floor is worse than a limb a millimetre off its socket — so it gets the last word in each sweep. Measuring the other order is §18's fourth exercise. The joint's correction follows solver_config::correction, one setting for the whole scene, and solver_config gained a nested joint_config for the joints' own knobs.

Step 7: the caller's step, and the pairs a joint excuses

From outside, a scene with joints is 8.10's step with one more loop, and one more test in the narrow phase. Two bodies a joint connects overlap at the joint by construction — two links of a chain, two hinged planks — and a contact solver that sees that overlap spends every step pushing apart two bodies the joint is pulling together. The engine does not run the narrow phase, the caller does, so the engine provides the tool: collision_filter, a sorted vector of 8.7's pair keys with a binary search per broadphase pair. 

for (const broadphase_pair& p : grid_.pairs())

    // Lesson 8.11: a joint's two bodies do not collide with each other.
    if (filter_.excluded(p.a, p.b)) { continue; }

    if (m.count == 0) { continue; }

    if (const contact_manifold* previous = cache_.find(key)) { carry_impulses(m, *previous); }
    manifolds_.push_back(m);
    keys_.push_back(key);
    pair_a_.push_back(p.a);
    pair_b_.push_back(p.b);
}

solver_.begin(world_.bodies());

14. What joints leave behind

§7's block made each joint exact, and §7 already said what it cannot do: a chain is a system that Gauss–Seidel crosses one joint per sweep, exactly as 8.10's tower was crossed one contact per sweep. A chain adds the one thing a tower of equal crates does not have, which is a mass ratio. A heavy lamp on a light chain, a wrecking ball on a cable, a character's torso on a hand: every sweep, the light links have to transmit the heavy one's weight, and an impulse that a light link can deliver barely moves the heavy one. The harness hangs ten 1 kg links from a fixed ceiling with an end weight of M kg, lets them settle, and measures how far the whole chain has stretched — the sum of every joint's gap, which ideally is zero. 

     end weight    8 sweeps    16         32         64        128
          1 kg      1.33 mm     0.60 mm     0.24 mm     0.08 mm     0.01 mm
         10 kg     18.36 mm     8.86 mm     4.13 mm     1.79 mm     0.66 mm
        100 kg    388.14 mm   158.73 mm    70.39 mm    34.66 mm    16.72 mm
       1000 kg   2471.50 mm   558.67 mm  1216.53 mm   341.77 mm   165.20 mm

At 1:1 eight sweeps leave a millimetre and a third over ten joints. The stretch grows roughly in proportion to the ratio — 18 mm at 10:1, 388 mm at 100:1, a chain stretched by 16% of its own length — and each doubling of the sweeps roughly halves it, which is the signature of a solve that has not converged rather than of a steady state it has reached. At 1000:1 the numbers stop meaning anything: the chain is not settling at all, it is bouncing, and the mean over half a second is not even monotone in the sweep count. That is the honest capability of this solver on a chain, and it is 8.10 §14's table again, in the other orientation.

The same work, spent differently

There is a better answer than more sweeps, and it follows from §5. The error a joint creates in a step is second order in the step — the drift is (ωh)²·d/2 — and the error a sweep has to remove is whatever the last step left. So consider spending the same number of joint visits per frame differently: instead of one 1/60 s step with eight sweeps, take eight 1/480 s steps with one sweep each. 

     schedule                       stretch
     8 sweeps x 1 step              388.142 mm
     4 sweeps x 2 steps             163.119 mm
     2 sweeps x 4 steps              49.051 mm
     1 sweep  x 8 steps              20.491 mm

Nineteen times less stretch for the same visits. Two things compound. Each small step starts from the answer the last one reached an eighth of a frame ago, so the error its single sweep must remove is small; and eight small steps each create an eighth of the drift of one big one. Iterating the big step addresses neither. This is the argument of Macklin and colleagues' "Small Steps in Physics Simulation" (2019) and of Box2D v3's soft step, and it is where real-time rigid-body solvers have been moving. It is not free here: every sub-step re-runs prepare, and in a scene with contacts it re-runs the narrow phase too, which 8.10 §13 priced at 1.1 ms for two thousand bodies. So it is named, measured, and left for Module 9's profiling lessons to price properly — and the demo's U key does it, for a chain, so you can watch the difference.

The budget

     joint                      prepare + warm start    per sweep
     ball-socket, block                90.46 ns            13.70 ns
     ball-socket, rows                104.21 ns            35.79 ns
     hinge, block                     112.54 ns            23.90 ns

Against 8.10 §13's 129 ns per contact manifold per sweep, a hinge with a limit and a motor costs less than half of one crate resting on another, and a socket about a tenth. The rows-versus-block line is §7's finding in the solver's own loop: 35.8 ns against 13.7. A ragdoll of fifteen joints at eight sweeps is under 10 µs a frame even if every one of them is a hinge with a limit and a motor, which is to say that joints are not where a physics budget goes; contacts are, and 8.12's ragdoll lying on a floor will spend far more on its contacts than on its joints.

And the things named rather than built

Every one of these is measured in this lesson, and none is a bug. Each is a known limitation of solving joints by sequential impulses, with a known answer the engine does not yet have:

  • Chains and mass ratios (above). The answers are sub-stepping, which this section measured, and a direct solve over a tree of joints — Baraff's linear-time method, or Featherstone's reduced coordinates — which is exact for a ragdoll's tree in O(n) and is what several production engines use for articulated characters.
  • The limit–pin coupling (§10). A door bounces off its stop at 7.5% of its speed at eight sweeps. The fix at any sweep count is to solve the active limit and the pin as one 4×4 block, trying the limit both active and inactive — a block with one clamp, the easiest possible case of the LCP 8.10 said nobody ships for contacts.
  • Velocity-level dissipation (§5, §8). ρ(ωh)² per step: a 90° pendulum keeps 75.4% of its energy per period. Sub-stepping cuts it by the square of the sub-step count per step and linearly per second; a per-joint choice of correction, which is §18's second exercise, is the cheaper partial answer.
  • No swing or twist limits on a ball-socket. A shoulder needs a cone that limits how far the arm swings and a separate limit on how far it twists. That is 8.12's first job, and it will be built from the same one-sided angular row this lesson's hinge limit is.

Press 3 for a ten-link chain with a 100 kg weight: the orange joint crosses sit visibly off the link centres, and the panel's total joint gap reads a few hundred millimetres. U switches to eight sub-steps of one sweep each and the chain pulls nearly true; W cycles the weight through 1, 10 and 100 kg. 17. Common pitfalls & debugging

A joint pulls the bodies apart, or does nothing at all.
Symptom: a rod that lengthens under load, or a rope that never catches; or a limit that pushes the wrong way and flings the door.
Cause: a sign in the Jacobian. J·V must be the rate at which C grows, with C ≥ 0 the satisfied side of a one-sided row. Flip it and an equality row still converges — the equation is the same — but a one-sided row's clamp is on the wrong side and it acts only when it should not.
Fix: check each row against a finite difference once: move the bodies by a small velocity for a tiny step and confirm ΔC ≈ h·J·V. §3's second control is what the wrong sign does to contacts.
A chain or a ragdoll jitters and then explodes.
Symptom: a chain that is fine in isolation shakes violently once it is in the same scene as the collision pipeline.
Cause: the two bodies a joint connects are also colliding. Adjacent links overlap at the joint by construction, and the contact solver pushes them apart every step while the joint pulls them together.
Fix: collision_filter — exclude every jointed pair in the caller's narrow phase, unless the joint sets collide_connected.
A door with restitution zero bounces off its stop.
Symptom: a hinged arm hits its limit and comes back off it at a few percent of the speed it arrived at; a turnstile hinged through its centre does not.
Cause: §10. The limit row converges against the pin at ρ = m·d²/(I_cm + m·d²) per sweep — 3/4 for a door — so eight sweeps leave 9.8% of the stop undone, and the warm start returns it as a bounce.
Fix: compute lever_ratio for the body and choose sweeps from ρⁿ; or solve the limit and pin as one block.
Pendulums and swinging lamps die away too quickly.
Symptom: a large swing loses a quarter of its energy per period; a small one barely any.
Cause: §5. A velocity-level joint dissipates ρ(ωh)² of its kinetic energy per step, so faster swings lose disproportionately more.
Fix: sub-steps (the loss per second is first order in the step), or Baumgarte for joints if you can guarantee they are never pulled far apart. Diagnose by halving the step: if the loss per second halves, it is this.
A limb twitches forever on a body that should be lying still.
Symptom: a ragdoll at rest has one part slowly spinning or rocking.
Cause: Baumgarte on a joint that was pulled apart — spawned in a pose the joints disagree with, or yanked by a heavy impact. The bias impulse off the centre of mass spins the body about the joint's free axes and nothing takes the energy back out. §6 measured 0.35 J.
Fix: split impulse, the default.
A chain hangs from a frozen link in mid-air after being hit.
Symptom: the struck link swings; everything above it stays exactly still.
Cause: the joints are not edges of the island graph, so each link slept as its own island and a sleeping body is an immovable body to its neighbours.
Fix: union joint edges in build_islands, by the same rule as contacts. The island count on two chains from one ceiling should be two.
A hinge limit fires at the wrong angle, or the angle jumps by a full turn.
Symptom: a door stops a few degrees early, or a wheel's limit trips as it passes half a turn.
Cause: the angle is measured from the wrong rest pose, or read from a quaternion in the w < 0 hemisphere — 7.4's double cover, which adds 2π.
Fix: record rest when the joint is made, and move the deviation to w ≥ 0 before atan2.
A hinge is solid until warm starting is on, then twists sideways now and then.
Symptom: an occasional kick perpendicular to the hinge axis.
Cause: the two perpendicular impulses cached as coordinates in a basis rebuilt from a world axis every step — 8.10 §4's bug. §9 counts 127 flips on a bridge.
Fix: cache a world vector, or choose the basis in the body's own frame. The engine does both.

18. Exercises

  1. Warm-up: how many sweeps does an arm need? A 0.1 × 1.2 × 0.1 m arm is hinged at one end. Compute ρ, then the number of sweeps for which a limit leaves less than 1% of its correction undone. Check it in the demo's second scene with Right.

    Hint

    I_cm about the hinge axis is m(L² + T²)/12, d = L/2, and you want ρⁿ < 0.01.

    Solution

    I_cm/m = (1.44 + 0.01)/12 = 0.12083 and d² = 0.36, so ρ = 0.36/0.48083 = 0.7487 — a hair under the 3/4 of a thin slab, because the arm's thickness adds to I_cm. Then n = ln 0.01 / ln 0.7487 = 15.9: sixteen sweeps. Note that the mass never appeared, and neither did the length on its own: ρ is a ratio of shapes.

  2. Let joints choose their correction. Add an override to joint_config so that joints can use Baumgarte while contacts use split impulse. Measure §8's 90° pendulum (target: 92.0% of its energy kept per period, against 75.4%) and §6's dislocated limb (0.35 J of spin). Then design a policy that gets the first without the second.

    Hint

    The energy Baumgarte injects is proportional to the error it corrects, and a healthy turning joint's error is δ/β — millimetres. A dislocated one's is centimetres.

    Solution sketch

    Apply the Baumgarte bias only while |C| is below a threshold of a few times the expected steady stretch, and use split impulse above it. The pendulum then keeps its Baumgarte energy balance and the limb is pulled home by a correction that throws its velocity away. The threshold is a length and so, like 8.10's slop, a scale decision.

  3. Engine challenge: the hoist. §3 found the row form 27% faster than 8.9's contact solve because it stores M⁻¹Jᵀ. Add the two angular halves — I_a⁻¹(r_a × n) and I_b⁻¹(r_b × n), and the same for both tangents — to contact_constraint, compute them in prepare_contacts, and use them in apply_impulse_pair. Target: 8.75 ns a point per sweep. Then run 8.10's harness and report how far its numbers moved.

    Hint

    The friction solve applies a vector impulse built from two tangents, so store the six vectors, not a matrix, and apply each tangent's share separately.

    Solution sketch

    The saving comes almost entirely from removing the two matrix-vector products from the apply; the memory grows by three vec3s per body side per point. 8.10's numbers move in their last printed digits and its conclusions do not — which is the evidence that the change is safe and the reason this lesson did not make it silently.

  4. Measure the order. The solver visits joints before contacts in every sweep, on an argument rather than a measurement (§13). Add a switch, and measure the bridge under five crates both ways: the deepest crate penetration and the worst hinge gap, at 4 and 8 sweeps.

    Solution sketch

    Expect each order to favour whichever constraint goes last: contacts-last gives shallower crates and larger hinge gaps, and the reverse. Which is right is a question about which error the player will notice — and the answer is the order that makes the most visible error the smallest one.

  5. Engine challenge: sub-stepping as a feature. §14 measured eight sub-steps of one sweep holding a 100:1 chain nineteen times tighter than one step of eight. Build it for a scene with contacts: decide which stages must run every sub-step and which can run once per frame, and measure the cost against the stretch.

    Hint

    The broadphase can run once with a margin wide enough for a frame's motion. The narrow phase is the question: re-running it is exact and expensive; reusing the frame's manifolds and updating only their separations along the normal is Box2D v3's answer.

    Solution sketch

    Integrate, prepare, one velocity sweep, a relaxation and the position update per sub-step; broadphase and narrow phase once per frame, with each contact's separation re-evaluated from the bodies' current poses. The chain and the bridge both hold far better than at eight sweeps for a similar cost, and a deep stack is the case to check, because a manifold computed once per frame is a manifold that can go stale inside it.

19. Recap & next

The engine has joints, and it has them without a second solver: a joint is a set of rows, a row is twelve numbers and two bounds, and the loop that stacked 8.10's crates visits them in the same sweeps, sorts them into the same islands and puts them to sleep by the same rule.

Getting there took one derivation and a lot of measurement. A constraint is a question about velocity: Ċ = J·V, the impulse is Jᵀλ because an ideal constraint does no work on what it allows, and the effective mass is 1/(J M⁻¹ Jᵀ) — which is 8.9's contact, checked to 1.9 × 10⁻⁷, and which runs 27% faster as a row than it did by hand. A joint is a choice of rows and bounds: a rod is a contact without its clamp, a rope is a contact turned inside out, a socket is three rows best solved as one exact block, a hinge adds two angular rows, a limit is a contact on an angle that can be speculative for free, and a motor is a clamp at a torque — which is what friction was all along.

And three things about solving joints at the velocity level that were measured rather than assumed. A joint drifts along a tangent, by a Pythagorean recurrence, and loses ρ(ωh)² of its energy each step. The position correction cuts both ways: Baumgarte conserves a turning joint's energy and invents energy in a dislocated one, and the default stays split impulse because damping is the failure a game survives. And one number, ρ = m·d²/(I_cm + m·d²), set a pendulum's period, a joint's dissipation, a motor's spin-up and a limit's convergence — the parallel-axis theorem from 8.3, turning up four lessons later as the organising fact of a solver.

Next: 8.12 — Ragdolls: Joints on a Skeleton. 7.6's skeleton becomes a tree of capsules joined by the sockets and hinges built here, with the two limits a shoulder needs and this lesson did not build — a swing cone and a twist range — and the hard part, which is handing a character from the animation system to the solver mid-motion and back. Everything this lesson measured arrives at once: a torso is ten times a hand, a ragdoll is a chain, and every limb is a body on a pin with its own ρ.
