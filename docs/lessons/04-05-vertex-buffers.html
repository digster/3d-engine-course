Vertex Buffers and Layouts

Three vertices typed into the source became a triangle. Now: 1,225 vertices read off a disk, each carrying a position, a normal and a texture coordinate, arranged in memory the way the hardware wants them, described by three numbers that nothing checks — and drawn seven times without seven copies. This is the lesson where the engine's geometry finally lives on the device.

1The Problem

Here is the geometry Lesson 4.4 drew, in its entirety:

Three vertices. Eighty-four bytes. Already in clip space, so no matrix was needed; each carrying a colour, so no texture was needed; typed by hand, so the layout could hardly be wrong. Every one of those conveniences was deliberate — a first triangle should be about getting a pipeline built, not about anything else — and every one of them is now gone.

assets/torus.obj has been sitting in this repository since Lesson 3.5. It is 1,225 vertices and 2,304 triangles. Each vertex carries a position, a normal and a texture coordinate: 39,200 bytes of geometry, none of it written by us, all of it in model space. Module 3 drew it on the CPU at a few frames a second. Getting it onto the GPU raises four questions that three hand-typed vertices never could:

  1. How are the bytes arranged? Lesson 3.5 stores positions, normals and uvs in three separate arrays. The hardware fetches a vertex as a unit. Those are not the same shape, and something has to convert — which raises the question of which shape is right, which turns out to depend on what you are about to do with it.
  2. How does the shader know what it is looking at? A vertex buffer is bytes. There is nothing in it that says where one vertex ends and the next begins, or which of those bytes are a normal. You tell the pipeline, in three numbers per attribute, and the hardware believes you.
  3. What happens to the sharing? Our torus has 2,304 triangles naming 6,912 corners between them, and only 1,225 distinct vertices to name. Lesson 2.12 built the index array for exactly this reason. Does the GPU have one?
  4. How do we draw it more than once? A scene with seven tori in it should not need seven copies of the torus.

And there is a fifth question, which is really the first one wearing a disguise. Lesson 4.4 ended by observing that the vertex is declared twice — once in HLSL and once in C++ — with nothing checking that the halves agree, and left the cross-check as an exercise. That was tolerable for two attributes typed a screen apart. At six attributes across two buffers it is not tolerable at all, because every one of the mistakes you can make here creates a pipeline successfully and draws something. We measured that in Lesson 4.4 and we will measure it again, more thoroughly, in a moment: of six broken layouts, SDL refuses exactly one.

Not yet

What this lesson does not have. A uniform buffer. The camera in shaders/mesh.vert.hlsl is a pile of static const floats, and the per-instance rotation is a cosine and a sine rather than a matrix, because a matrix has to arrive from somewhere and that somewhere is Lesson 4.6. Nothing about the projection is simplified — it is Lesson 2.10's matrix written out as arithmetic, line for line — but the camera cannot move until next lesson. There is also no depth test until Lesson 4.7, which is why the tori are laid out so they barely overlap.

2Building Intuition

2.1 The buffer is a tape; the layout is a ruler

Forget graphics for a paragraph. You are handed a roll of paper tape with numbers printed along it, and told it contains a list of records. You cannot read it. Not because the numbers are unclear — because nothing on the tape says where a record starts. It could be a list of thirty-two-byte records or a list of eight-byte records; the bytes are identical either way.

So you are given three more things, and now you can read it: how far apart the records are (call it the pitch), where within a record each field sits (its offset), and how to interpret the bytes at that spot (its format). With those, reading field f of record i is one line of arithmetic:

\[ \text{address} = \text{base} + i \times \text{pitch} + \text{offset}_f \]

In words: start at the buffer, step forward i whole records, then step forward again to where this field lives inside the record.

That is the entire mechanism. A GPU's vertex fetch is that formula, in fixed-function silicon, run once per attribute per vertex — and the three numbers it uses are exactly the three you hand to SDL_GPUVertexBufferDescription and SDL_GPUVertexAttribute. There is no validation step in the middle where the hardware notices that your pitch is wrong, because there is nothing to notice against. Any pitch produces addresses; any addresses produce bytes; any bytes produce floats. The picture is the only place the mistake becomes visible, and §4.4 below shows exactly what it looks like when it does.

2.2 Two ways to arrange the same bytes

Our torus has 1,225 vertices, each with a position (three floats), a normal (three floats) and a texture coordinate (two floats). That is 39,200 bytes however you arrange them, and there are two sensible arrangements.

Interleaved puts one vertex's data together: position, normal, uv, then the next vertex's position, and so on. Separate — sometimes called planar, or "structure of arrays" — puts one attribute's data together: every position, then every normal, then every uv. Lesson 3.5 chose separate arrays, and said at the time that Module 4 would revisit the decision with a diagram. Here is the diagram.

The argument for interleaving is the one Lesson 4.1 spent a whole section measuring: memory arrives in cache lines, sixty-four bytes at a time, and you pay for the line whether you use all of it or not. A vertex shader reads a whole vertex. Interleaved, that whole vertex is thirty-two contiguous bytes — half a line — so it arrives in one transaction and the neighbouring vertex arrives free with it. Separate, the same vertex is twelve bytes here, twelve bytes a hundred kilobytes away, and eight bytes somewhere else again: three addresses, three transactions, and at worst five lines because a twelve-byte field can straddle a boundary.

That is the bottom-left number in the figure, and it is measured rather than reasoned: 1 line per vertex interleaved, up to 5 separate.

And then the bottom-right number, which is why this is a genuine trade and not a rule. Consider a pass that reads only positions — a shadow map, or a depth pre-pass, both of which arrive in Module 6. Sweeping every position touches 613 cache lines in the interleaved buffer, because every line dragged in carries twelve useful bytes and twenty wasted ones, and 230 in the separate one, where every byte of every line is a position. Separate wins that pass by 2.7×.

The honest answer

So which? Interleaved, for this engine, for now — because every pass we have reads the whole vertex, and the layout that is right for the common case is the right default. Real engines split the difference: a very common production layout is position in its own buffer, everything else interleaved in a second, which makes the depth-only pass fast without making the main pass slow. We will not build that until there is a depth pass to justify it — but notice that SDL_GPU already supports it with no new concepts at all, because a "separate" layout is just more than one vertex_buffer slot, and Lesson 4.5's instance buffer is already the second slot.

2.3 A contract written in two languages

Here is the sentence Lesson 4.4 ended on, and it is worth restating because everything in §3.3 follows from it:

The vertex is declared twice, in two languages, and nothing checks that they agree.

In HLSL you write float3 position : TEXCOORD0. In C++ you write FLOAT3 at offset 0, location 0. The two are joined by the number 0 and by nothing else — not by a name, not by a type, not by a header they both include. If you renumber the semantics in the shader and forget the C++, the wrong attribute arrives, silently, at full frame rate.

Except that there is a third document, and we have been generating it since Lesson 4.3 without using this half of it. When shadercross compiles a shader it also emits a JSON reflection, and Lesson 4.3 read four numbers out of it — the resource counts SDL demands. The same file has been carrying the vertex inputs all along:

That is the shader's half of the contract, in machine-readable form, produced by the compiler that read the actual HLSL. Comparing it against our half is a loop over two small arrays. It is the whole of pipeline_desc::check_layout, it settles exercise 4.4.4, and it is the only thing in this engine that will ever notice a renumbered semantic.

Read the lower half of that figure carefully, because it is the reason the upper half matters. Six layouts were built and handed to SDL_CreateGPUGraphicsPipeline. Five of them are wrong. SDL refused exactly one — the layout whose shader input nothing supplies, where Metal's compiler produced a genuinely excellent message ("Vertex attribute input_uv(2) is missing from the vertex descriptor"). The other four were created without a word and drew something.

3The Theory

3.1 The address arithmetic, with real numbers

Take our interleaved vertex: pitch 32, position at offset 0, normal at offset 12, uv at offset 24. Suppose the buffer begins at address B, and the vertex shader is running for vertex 700. Where does its normal come from?

\[ B + 700 \times 32 + 12 \;=\; B + 22{,}400 + 12 \;=\; B + 22{,}412 \]

Base, plus seven hundred whole vertices at thirty-two bytes each, plus twelve bytes into that vertex. Twelve bytes are read from there and interpreted as three consecutive floats, because the format says FLOAT3.

Now make one mistake — declare the pitch as 28 instead of 32 — and run the same arithmetic:

\[ B + 700 \times 28 + 12 \;=\; B + 19{,}600 + 12 \;=\; B + 19{,}612 \]

Base, plus seven hundred vertices at twenty-eight bytes each, plus twelve.

That is 2,800 bytes early, which is eighty-seven and a half vertices back. Not eighty-seven: eighty-seven and a half. The address does not land on a vertex boundary at all, so the three floats read as a normal are the last two floats of one vertex's normal and the first float of its uv. The result is a perfectly valid float3 made of pieces of two different fields of the wrong vertex, and the hardware has no way to know and no reason to care.

Two things follow, and both matter for diagnosis. First, the error accumulates: vertex 1 is four bytes off, vertex 700 is 2,800 bytes off, vertex 1,224 is 4,896 bytes off. The mesh does not shift — it disintegrates progressively, worse the further into the buffer you look. Second, the first vertex is always right, because i is zero and the pitch never multiplies anything. A triangle drawn from three vertices at the very start of a buffer can look perfectly correct with a completely wrong pitch, which is exactly how this bug survives a small test and appears on a real mesh.

3.2 What a format is — and the two questions it answers

SDL_GPUVertexElementFormat looks like a type name and behaves like two. Ask it "how many bytes does this attribute occupy in the buffer?" and ask it "what type does the shader receive?", and for some formats you get two different answers. That gap is not a wart; it is one of the most useful things fixed-function hardware still does for free.

A representative slice of SDL_GPUVertexElementFormat. The last two rows are where the two questions come apart.
formatbytes in the buffertype in the shaderwhat the hardware does on the way
FLOAT312float3nothing
FLOAT416float4nothing
UBYTE44uint4nothing — these are integers and stay integers
UBYTE4_NORM4float4divides each byte by 255, giving 0…1
SHORT2_NORM4float2divides each short by 32,767, giving −1…1
HALF48float4expands 16-bit floats to 32-bit

Worked example. A colour channel stored as the byte 235 arrives in the shader as 235 ÷ 255 = 0.921569. Lesson 4.4's triangle asked for 0.92. The difference is 0.0015 — and since the render target is itself eight bits per channel, both values write the code 235 at full brightness. Four bytes of storage have replaced sixteen, the shader's declaration has not changed by a character, and the arithmetic that made it possible cost nothing, because it happens in the fetch unit rather than in your shader.

We put that to work in §4.7: Lesson 4.4's vertex goes from 28 bytes to 16, a 43% saving, and §G of the harness confirms the two pictures cover exactly the same 47,124 pixels and differ nowhere by more than one code out of 255.

Six characters

The _NORM suffix is the whole difference and it is easy to miss. UBYTE4 and UBYTE4_NORM occupy the same four bytes and differ by six characters. Use the first where you meant the second and a colour of (235, 89, 79) arrives in a float4 as 235.0, 89.0, 79.0 — which, after clamping, is white. That is a real bug with an unhelpful symptom, and check_layout catches it, because the reflection says float4 and UBYTE4 presents as uint4.

3.3 Locations, and who checks them

A location is an integer, and it is the only thing joining your buffer to the shader's inputs. In HLSL it is the index on the TEXCOORD semantic — Lesson 4.3 established that SDL requires every non-system-value semantic to be TEXCOORDn, numbered from zero with no gaps, precisely so that this join is a small integer rather than a string match. In C++ it is the first argument to pipeline_desc::attribute.

Two rules govern them, and they come from different places. SDL's header states the first plainly: "All vertex attribute locations provided to an SDL_GPUVertexInputState must be unique." It does not say what happens if they are not, and a rule with no stated consequence is one you want to be checking yourself. The second is not stated anywhere, because it is not a rule at all — it is just what happens: a location the shader never declares is ignored, and a location nothing supplies reads undefined data.

Those two failures are wildly asymmetric in consequence, and it is worth being clear about which is which:

Notice the shape of that table. The one case SDL refuses is the one that would have been most obvious anyway — an unfed input produces geometry stretching to infinity, which is hard to miss. The silent ones are the ones that draw a plausible-looking wrong picture. This is the same temperament Lesson 4.3 found in shader creation, which validates the entry point's name and none of the four resource counts, and it is why an engine grows its own checks: not because the API is bad, but because the API cannot know what you meant.

Be equally clear about what our own check cannot see. A pitch that is too long passes, because every attribute still fits inside an over-long vertex — nothing about the declaration is inconsistent, it is just wrong. Swapped offsets pass, because FLOAT3 at 12 and FLOAT3 at 0 are both perfectly legal placements of a float3. A checker that catches three of five mistakes and says so is worth having; one that catches three of five and implies it caught them all is worse than nothing.

3.4 Supplying less than the shader asks for

A layout may legally supply FLOAT3 to a shader input declared float4. This is common — a colour without alpha, a position without w — and the hardware fills in the components you did not supply. With what, though? SDL's header does not say. Vulkan's specification and D3D12's both fill missing components with (0, 0, 0, 1), but SDL_GPU's contract is SDL's, not the backend's, and this course does not repeat a fact it has not checked.

So we measured it, and the measurement is unusually direct. The per-instance record's fourth component is the scale. Supply FLOAT3 instead of FLOAT4 and the mesh is drawn at whatever the hardware put in w — 0 makes it vanish, 1 makes it appear at unit scale. Coverage reads the answer straight off the screen:

On this machine, on Metal, the missing w is 1, which agrees with what Vulkan and D3D12 require — so the behaviour is almost certainly uniform across SDL_GPU's backends, and now it is a thing we have seen rather than a thing we assumed. check_layout reports this case as a note rather than a problem, for exactly that reason.

3.5 Indices: the arithmetic of sharing

Lesson 2.12 introduced the index array with an argument about an icosahedron: twelve corners, twenty faces, sixty corner-slots to fill. Store a vertex per corner-slot and you store sixty; store twelve and name them sixty times and you store twelve. The GPU has exactly this, spelled SDL_GPU_BUFFERUSAGE_INDEX, and the numbers on a real mesh are more dramatic than on a toy one.

Our torus: 2,304 triangles, so 6,912 corner-slots, drawn from 1,225 distinct vertices. Every vertex is named, on average, 6,912 ÷ 1,225 = 5.64 times.

In bytes, on the device, measured both ways:

verify_45 §E. The same mesh, uploaded twice.
verticesvertex bytesindex bytestotal
indexed1,22539,20013,82453,024

4.17× smaller. And note where the win comes from: the index buffer is not free — it costs 13,824 bytes of its own — but it removes 181,984 bytes of duplicated vertices. It pays for itself roughly thirteen times over, and the reason is simply that an index is two bytes and a vertex is thirty-two. Anything that lets you write the small thing instead of the big thing 6,912 times is going to win.

Why two bytes? Because SDL_GPUIndexElementSize has exactly two values, _16BIT and _32BIT, and sixteen bits can name 65,536 distinct vertices. That number has been in mesh.hpp since Lesson 3.5 as k_max_mesh_vertices, with a comment saying it came from the hardware rather than from us. This is the lesson where the claim gets cashed: gpu_mesh::create refuses a mesh above it, and the loader has been refusing one since 3.5.

The second saving is subtler and is the one the figure's lower strip is about. An expanded draw must invoke the vertex shader once per vertex, and it has 6,912 of them: 6,912 invocations, exactly, with no possibility of doing better. An indexed draw can do better, because hardware keeps a small post-transform cache of recently transformed vertices keyed by index — so a vertex named six times may be transformed once. May. The best case is 1,225 and the worst is 6,912, and where you actually land depends on the order the triangles appear in, which is a property of the file.

Why "vertex cache optimisation" is a thing

Reordering pays off here, and nowhere else. Reordering a mesh's triangles so that consecutive ones share vertices does not change what is drawn by a single pixel; it changes how often the cache hits, and therefore how many times the vertex shader runs. It is a pure win available at import time, and Forsyth's linear-speed algorithm (in Further Reading) is the standard one. We are not implementing it — but you now know exactly what it would be optimising, which is more than most people who invoke it do. Lesson 4.9's RenderDoc capture is where you can finally see the real invocation count instead of a range.

3.6 Vertex rate and instance rate

Seven tori. One torus's worth of geometry. The mechanism is one field of one struct.

SDL_GPUVertexBufferDescription has an input_rate, and it takes one of two values. SDL_GPU_VERTEXINPUTRATE_VERTEX means the cursor into this buffer advances by pitch once per vertex — the behaviour you have been assuming all along, and the one Lesson 4.4 used without noticing there was a choice. SDL_GPU_VERTEXINPUTRATE_INSTANCE means it advances once per instance. So while the vertex shader runs 1,225 times for instance 3, every one of those runs reads element 3 of the instance buffer.

Everything else is identical. It is an ordinary buffer, created with the ordinary _VERTEX usage bit, bound with the ordinary SDL_BindGPUVertexBuffers, read by ordinary attributes at ordinary locations. The shader cannot tell. Look at the input struct in mesh.vert.hlsl and there is nothing marking locations 3, 4 and 5 as per-instance — because from the shader's point of view they are simply six values that arrived.

That is worth sitting with for a moment, because it is a small piece of very good API design and it is easy to walk past. Instancing could have been a separate draw path, a separate shader stage, a separate buffer type. It is none of those. It is the same everything with a different rate on the cursor, which means every tool you already have — layouts, formats, check_layout, the reflection JSON — works on instance data unchanged.

The last argument of SDL_DrawGPUIndexedPrimitives that we have been passing 1 since Lesson 4.4 is the instance count. Passing 7 draws seven tori. The cost on the CPU is one integer; the cost in memory is 28 bytes per instance, against 53,024 bytes of geometry that is uploaded once at startup and never moves again — 0.37% of the data on the device changes per frame.

That ratio is the whole argument for instancing, and it is also the reason the per-instance buffer needs a different kind of object from the geometry: it is written every frame, so it keeps its staging buffer and cycles it, which §4.6 unpacks.

4Implementation

Eight steps. The first three build the machinery, the next three break it on purpose and measure the wreckage, and the last two are the payoff.

4.1 The interleaved vertex, and the layout beside it

Start with the type. Three attributes, in the order the shader declares them, with no padding and no cleverness:

Twelve plus twelve plus eight is thirty-two, which is exactly half a cache line, which is why §2.2's measurement came out at one line per vertex rather than the one-or-two you would get from an unaligned size. That is a happy accident of choosing three attributes a surface actually needs — but the static_assert is not decoration. If a future field made the compiler insert padding, the code would still work, because the pitch we declare is sizeof(gpu_vertex_pnu) whatever that is; what would break is Figure 1, which would quietly become a lie. A diagram that has stopped matching the code is worse than one that never did, because it is believed.

Now the layout that describes it — and the important thing about this function is where it lives:

Not one numeric literal. sizeof for the pitch, offsetof for every offset. Write 12 there and it is correct today and wrong the morning somebody inserts a field above the normal — at which point every vertex reads the wrong bytes, nothing reports an error, and the mesh becomes a cloud. This is the single cheapest defence in the whole lesson and it costs a header.

C++ — offsetof, and why it is allowed here

offsetof(T, member), from <cstddef>, is the byte distance from the start of a struct to one of its members. It is technically only guaranteed for standard-layout types — no virtual functions, no mixed access specifiers, no base class with data — which gpu_vertex_pnu is, being eight floats and nothing else. That is not a coincidence you should rely on quietly: a vertex type is a memory layout first and a C++ type second, so it never grows a constructor, a virtual, or a private section. If it ever needs one of those, it has stopped being a vertex.

And the conversion itself, which is the entire reason this file exists and is twenty lines long:

Three arrays walked in lockstep into one. It deserves a name and a lesson not because it is difficult but because the choice it embodies — a vertex's data contiguous rather than an attribute's — is the one the hardware cares about and the one the code makes completely invisible. Nothing in those ten lines hints that they are the difference between one cache line and five.

make_vertex does one thing worth calling out: it normalises the normal. Lesson 3.5 established that the loader stores what the file said, so that two loads of a file are diffable and the loader is never a place where data quietly differs from its source. The consumer normalises, because the consumer is the one that needs a unit vector — and a mesh carrying no normals at all stays zero rather than becoming a NaN, because mesh::normal_at already defines zero as the meaning of "absent".

Binding is two calls, and the second carries a piece of state that is easy to put in the wrong place:

The element size is passed at bind time — not baked into the pipeline, not carried by the buffer. One buffer of bytes can therefore be read as 16-bit or 32-bit indices depending on this one argument, and passing the wrong one is not an error: it reads consecutive pairs of your 16-bit indices as single enormous 32-bit ones, every one of them out of range, and produces a mesh made of glass shards. The one with no counterpart in the un-indexed version is vertex_offset, which is added to every index before the fetch. That is how several meshes share a single vertex buffer without having their indices rewritten — you upload them end to end and give each draw the offset of its own block. We pass zero, and Module 6's batching is where it earns its keep.

4.3 Reading the shader's half of the contract

Lesson 4.3 built a scanner that pulls four integers out of the reflection JSON. The inputs need a slightly bigger one, and the bound on its scan is the whole correctness argument:

The same file contains an outputs array with byte-identical key names. A scanner that simply looked for "location" would walk straight out of one array and into the other and report a vertex shader as taking nine inputs when it declares six. Find the array, find its end, never read past it.

Note also the two different contracts in that snippet, which mirror Lesson 4.3's reasoning exactly. A missing inputs key returns true, because a shader with no vertex inputs is legal and common — Lesson 4.4's fragment stage is one. A malformed array returns false, because half a description would let check_layout give a clean bill of health to a contract it never actually read. Absent is an answer; corrupt is an error.

With both halves in hand the check is a double loop with an eye for detail. The part that earns its place is the type comparison, which has to distinguish two very different kinds of disagreement:

A float3 supplied to a float4 is legal and defined (§3.4), so it is a note. A float supplied where the shader reads an int is a reinterpretation of bits, so it is a problem. Counting both as "mismatch" would make the report useless in the direction that matters: a check that cries wolf is a check that gets ignored, and a check that is ignored is worse than no check at all, because it looks like coverage.

4.4 Watching a wrong pitch

Descriptions of layout bugs are cheap. Here are the actual pixels, from three pipelines that differ in one integer and in nothing else — same shader, same buffer, same attributes, same everything:

Three things in that figure are worth naming, because they are what you use to recognise this bug rather than merely to fix it once you already know.

  1. It is not blank. The instinct on seeing nothing is to check culling, winding, viewport, near plane. A wrong pitch draws plenty; it draws a mess. Coverage actually goes up — 3,696 pixels correct against about 5,000 wrong — because the garbage geometry sprays outward.
  2. It is worse further in. The shape near the start of the buffer is recognisable and it degenerates as the mesh goes on, because the error is i × (pitch error). Draw only the first few hundred vertices and the bug looks like a mild wobble.
  3. Too long and too short look the same. Both panels are shattered clouds of about the same size. The symptom tells you that the pitch is wrong, not which way — so do not try to read the direction out of the picture. Print sizeof instead.

A wrong offset is a different animal, and the difference is genuinely useful. Point the position attribute at the normal's bytes and the mesh does not shatter — every vertex is still read from its own record, just from the wrong twelve bytes of it. Since a normal is a unit vector, the whole mesh collapses onto a sphere of radius 1: 3,126 pixels in a 62 × 64 box, a compact blob where a torus should be. The symptom names the mistake. Wrecked and scattered means the pitch; wrong but coherent means an offset.

4.6 Twenty-eight bytes a frame

The per-instance record is where a real engine puts a matrix. We have no uniform buffers until Lesson 4.6, so we put in the smallest honest thing that still animates:

Its layout is described by the twin of gpu_mesh::describe, and there is exactly one line that differs in kind:

instance_buffer instead of vertex_buffer. Inside, that is input_rate = SDL_GPU_VERTEXINPUTRATE_INSTANCE and nothing else. Note two details in the attributes: location 3 is a FLOAT4 covering four struct fields, because ox, oy, oz, scale are four contiguous floats and the shader wants them as one float4 — three floats plus a lone float would be two attributes and two fetches for the same bytes. And locations continue at 3 rather than restarting at 0, because locations are numbered across the whole pipeline, not per buffer. A shader has one input list; which slot each entry is fetched from is precisely what these calls decide.

The field with one legal value

SDL_GPUVertexBufferDescription also has an instance_step_rate, and SDL's header says: "Reserved for future use. Must be set to 0." instance_buffer sets it to zero and does not expose it. A setter for a field with exactly one legal value is an invitation to a bug and buys nothing — and if SDL ever un-reserves it, adding the parameter then is a two-line change, whereas removing a parameter people have started passing is not.

Now, the buffer. This one is written every frame, which makes it a different kind of object from the geometry — and the difference is one line:

And a second difference, which is about correctness rather than cost. Both hops of the write pass cycle = true:

This is the same hazard gpu_present_target faced in Lesson 4.2, now on the geometry side. The buffer you are about to write may still be referenced by last frame's command buffer, which the GPU may not have reached yet. Cycling hands you a different internal buffer in exactly that case. Pass false and nothing goes wrong — until the GPU falls behind, which is to say until somebody with a slower machine than yours runs it. 7Common Pitfalls & Debugging

The mesh is a cloud of shards

Symptom: geometry sprayed everywhere, coherent near one edge, worse the further in you look.
Cause: the pitch. Almost always a literal where a sizeof belonged, or a struct that gained a field.
Fix: print sizeof(your_vertex) and compare it with the pitch you declared. Do not try to read the direction of the error out of the picture — too long and too short look the same (§4.4).

The mesh is compact but the wrong shape

Symptom: a recognisable but wrong object — often a ball, often much smaller than expected.
Cause: an offset, not a pitch. Each vertex is read from its own record, just from the wrong bytes of it. Reading a normal as a position gives a unit sphere; reading a uv as a position gives a flat patch near the origin.
Fix: offsetof, everywhere, with no exceptions.

Everything is white, or everything is black

Symptom: the shape is right and the colour is not.
Cause: UBYTE4 where you meant UBYTE4_NORM (or the reverse). The first delivers 0…255 into a float; the second divides by 255 first. Six characters, same four bytes.
Fix: check_layout catches this one, because the reflection says float4 and UBYTE4 presents as uint4. Read the log.

"Vertex attribute input_x(n) is missing from the vertex descriptor"

Symptom: pipeline creation fails with that message, or its Vulkan/D3D12 equivalent.
Cause: the shader declares a location nothing supplies. This is the one layout mistake SDL refuses (§3.3).
Fix: it is telling you the location. Add the attribute — or delete the input from the shader, if it was left behind by an edit.

A mesh made of glass shards, but the pitch is right

Symptom: the shard picture, with a verified-correct layout.
Cause: the index element size. Binding 16-bit indices as _32BIT reads consecutive pairs as single enormous values, every one out of range.
Fix: the element size is an argument to SDL_BindGPUIndexBuffer, not a property of the buffer. Ours is SDL_GPU_INDEXELEMENTSIZE_16BIT because mesh::indices is std::uint16_t; keep the two in one place.

The buffer is bound but nothing draws

Symptom: no geometry, no error, no validation message.
Cause: a buffer created without the usage bit it is being bound for. A vertex buffer needs SDL_GPU_BUFFERUSAGE_VERTEX; an index buffer needs _INDEX. It is a bitmask, and it is not advisory.
Fix: run with debug = true. The validation layer is good about this one, unlike almost everything else in this lesson.

The instanced draw shows one object, or seven identical ones

Symptom: the instance count clearly reached the draw call, but the placements did not.
Cause: input_rate left at its default. Every enum in that struct has its first enumerator at zero, so a zero-initialised description means _VERTEX — the same trap Lesson 4.4 recorded for rasterizer_state.cull_mode. At vertex rate, instance 0 reads placements 0, 1, 2, … as its vertices walk the buffer, and every instance beyond the first reads past the end.
Fix: set it explicitly. That is what instance_buffer exists for.

Tearing or flickering in the instance data under load

Symptom: placements occasionally wrong for one frame — and only on a slower machine, or with the frame rate uncapped.
Cause: cycle = false on a per-frame write. You overwrote data the GPU had not finished reading.
Fix: true, on both the map and the upload. And note that this is why the bug did not appear on your machine: it needs the GPU to be behind.

8Exercises

  1. 4.5.1 — Shrink the vertex. The normal is three floats and it is a unit vector, so every component is in −1…+1. Change it to SHORT4_NORM or BYTE4_NORM and take the vertex from 32 bytes to 24 or 20. Measure the difference in the picture the way §G does — same pipeline, both buffers, compare per pixel.

    Hint: _NORM formats come in 2- and 4-component widths only, so you will store four components and ignore the fourth — or use it, since a fourth component next to a normal is exactly where a tangent's handedness goes in Module 6. Encode with round(x * 127.0f) for BYTE4_NORM. Expect visible banding on smooth shading at 8 bits and none at 16; that difference is the exercise.

  2. 4.5.2 — Make check_layout catch the long pitch. §3.3 admits it cannot. But it has all the information: if the highest attribute end across a slot is 28 and the pitch is 36, eight bytes per vertex are being fetched and discarded. Report it as a note (like widening), with the wasted byte count.

    Hint: compute max(offset + size_of(format)) per slot and compare with that slot's pitch. Be careful not to report padding as waste when it is deliberate — some hardware prefers 4- or 16-byte-aligned pitches, so a note is right and an error would be wrong.

  3. 4.5.3 — Two buffers instead of one. Split the vertex into a position-only buffer at slot 0 and a normal+uv buffer at slot 2, as §2.2's production layout does. Nothing about the shader changes; only describe and the binding do. Then measure: does the frame time change at all on your hardware?

    Hint: it almost certainly will not, at 2,304 triangles — and that is the finding, not a failure. The layout matters when you are fetch-bound, and a torus on a modern GPU is not. Write down the number of triangles at which you would expect it to start mattering, then check with 4.1's bandwidth figures.

  4. 4.5.4 — Break it on purpose, in the running demo. Add a key that swaps the position and normal offsets at runtime by rebuilding the pipeline. Predict the picture before you press it — §4.4 tells you what to expect and why — then check.

    Hint: pipeline creation is 2.4 ms for a permutation the driver has not seen (Lesson 4.4 §4.4), so build all the variants at startup rather than on the keypress. The prediction is a unit sphere; the interesting part is what happens to the shading, since the normal is now the position.

  5. 4.5.5 — Many more instances. Raise k_max_instances to 200 and lay them out on a grid. Find the instance count at which the frame time starts to rise, and work out whether you became vertex-bound, fill-bound, or bandwidth-bound.

    Hint: shrink the tori as you add them so that coverage stays roughly constant; then a rise in frame time is not simply more pixels. If the time tracks instances × 1,225 you are vertex-bound. The per-frame upload grows too — 200 instances is 5,600 bytes, still nothing — which is the point being demonstrated a second time.

9Recap & Next

The engine can now put real geometry on the device and describe it correctly, which is the difference between having drawn a triangle and having a renderer.

  • A vertex layout is three numbers and one formula — base + i × pitch + offset — evaluated by fixed-function hardware that has no way to know whether the numbers are right. The failures are silent by construction, not by oversight.
  • A format answers two questions. UBYTE4_NORM is four bytes in the buffer and a float4 in the shader; that gap made Lesson 4.4's vertex 43% smaller with no visible change and no shader edit.
  • Interleaved beats separate for whole-vertex reads and loses for single-attribute passes — 1 cache line against 5, and 613 against 230. Both measured on our own mesh. The production answer is a hybrid, and Module 6's depth pass is where we will want it.
  • The layout is a contract in two languages, and the reflection JSON is the notary. check_layout catches three of five common mistakes; SDL catches one; the remaining ones are found by looking at the picture, which is why §4.4 catalogued what the pictures look like.
  • Index buffers are 4.17× smaller on our torus and turn a fixed 6,912 vertex-shader invocations into a range of 1,225–6,912. The picture is identical to the pixel, which the harness proves.
  • Instancing is one enum value. Same buffer type, same bind call, same attributes, one different input_rate — and the shader cannot tell. 196 bytes a frame drive seven copies of 53,024 bytes of geometry.

What is still missing is the thing you will have noticed the moment the window opened: the camera does not move, and it cannot, because every number in the projection is a static const compiled into the shader. Placing an object is per-instance data and we have that; placing the camera is per-frame data shared by every vertex of every instance, and pushing it through the vertex buffer would mean writing the same sixteen floats 1,225 times.
