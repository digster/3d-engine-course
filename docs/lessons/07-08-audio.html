SDL3 Audio: Streams, Mixing, and 3D Sound

Every subsystem this course has built so far can be judged by looking at it. This one cannot, and that single fact reshapes everything — because a bug you cannot see has to be measured, on a thread you do not own, against a deadline that does not negotiate. The engine gets ears in three files and about a thousand lines, and the arithmetic in them is unreasonably simple: mixing is addition, a distance curve is one divide, and panning is two trigonometric functions. What is not simple is everything that follows from addition. Sum sixteen uncorrelated voices and the level grows as √N, not N — measured at exactly 0.577·√N — so the obvious 1/N headroom throws away 12 dB you did not have to spend; but the peak follows neither, and it keeps growing the longer you listen (6.98× at ten milliseconds, 10.46× at eight seconds), which makes headroom a bet rather than a bound. Pan with the first law that occurs to you and a sound sweeping across the image ducks by 3.01 dB in the middle, because two speakers add in power and 0.5² + 0.5² is not 1. Attenuate with 1/d² — the law everyone remembers, and the right law for the wrong quantity — and every sound in the game falls off at −12 dB per doubling instead of −6, because a sample is a pressure and pressure is the square root of intensity. And then the finding that is 7.7's rule arriving in a subsystem that had never heard of it: the gain is a lossy reconstruction too. Sampled at 60 Hz by the game and reconstructed at 48 kHz by the mixer, a zero-order hold injects a step of 0.1007 in one sample — 7.7× the largest step the waveform itself ever takes — of which 98.8% is broadband, which is exactly what a click is. Along the way: SDL3's audio-stream model and the resume SDL2 did not need, a WAV loader that is honest about which half is worth hand-rolling, a linear resampler measured at 62.40 dB against SDL's 85.05 and falling to 20.43 dB at 10 kHz, generational handles finally meeting the case they were designed for, one mutex on a real-time thread with the compromise stated rather than hidden, and a dropout counter that turned out to be measuring SDL working correctly.

1. The problem: an engine with no ears

Count what this engine can do. It evaluates a microfacet BRDF, cascades a shadow map, prefilters an environment cube, bloom and tonemaps and culls and deduces its own frame order; it skins a character from a keyframed clip, cross-fades two clips without shortening a limb, and puts text on the screen in linear light. Seventy-nine lessons. And if you close your eyes it is indistinguishable from a program that is not running.

That is the gap this lesson closes, and it is worth being precise about why it has waited until the end of Module 7 rather than appearing in Module 1, where a beep would have been easy. The reason is not that audio is hard. The arithmetic below is the simplest in the course — there is no matrix in this lesson, no derivation longer than four lines, and the entire spatial model is one divide and two trigonometric functions. The reason is that audio is the first subsystem in this engine whose output you cannot look at, and that changes what the work is.

Consider how every bug in Modules 2 through 7 announced itself. The z-buffer bug was triangles fighting. The perspective-correct-interpolation bug was a texture swimming. The gamma bug was a picture too dark, the TBN bug was lighting that moved the wrong way, the matrix-blend bug in 7.7 was a limb that got visibly shorter. In every case the symptom told you half of what you needed, because a picture is two-dimensional and persistent: it sits there while you look at it, you can compare it to a reference by subtraction, and you can point at the wrong part.

A sound is one-dimensional and gone. The symptom is "it sounds a bit wrong", reported by somebody who cannot point. You cannot pause it and look at it. You cannot breakpoint the code that produced it, because stopping at a breakpoint changes the result — the device keeps consuming, finds nothing, and the thing you were trying to debug is replaced by the sound of having stopped at a breakpoint. And you cannot print from it, for reasons §10 measures.

So the discipline of this lesson is set by that constraint, and it is the same discipline every section of verify_78.cpp follows: get the samples into an array and look at the array. Not "listen and adjust" — measure. Every claim below has a number behind it and the number was produced by a program that opened no audio device at all.

A promise with a date on it. Lesson 5.2 built the application layer and gave app_config a field it did not use: 

/// Subsystems beyond what `draw_to` already implies. Video is initialised
/// automatically for `renderer` and `gpu` and deliberately NOT for
/// `headless`; audio arrives in Module 7 and goes here.
SDL_InitFlags extra_subsystems = 0;

Lesson 5.5 made the same bet one directory over, arguing that the asset system belongs outside gfx/ because "it loads meshes, images, and in Module 7 sounds — and putting them under gfx/ would make the audio loader's home a joke six lessons before it is written." Both predictions come due here, and §4 explains why the second one turns out to have named the wrong directory anyway.

2. Building intuition: a sample is a measurement of pressure

Start with the physical thing, because every arithmetic decision below is a consequence of it.

A sound is a travelling variation in air pressure. A speaker cone moves out and the air just in front of it is momentarily compressed; it moves in and that air is rarefied. The compression propagates, your eardrum is pushed and pulled by it, and that is hearing. There is no "sound" hiding in the air as an object — there is only pressure, as a function of time, at a point.

A sample is that function measured once. A recording is the function measured at even intervals, forever. That is the whole of digital audio, and figure 1 is the whole of the idea.

Three consequences fall straight out of the picture, and each of them decides something later in this lesson.

The samples are the sound. There is nothing else in the file — no envelope, no pitch, no notion of a "note". Everything you might want to do to a sound is therefore an operation on an array of numbers, which is why this subsystem has no geometry in it and why its bugs look like arithmetic rather than like scenes.

The interval is fixed and it is short. At 48,000 samples a second, one sample is 1/48000 = 20.83 µs. Hold that number next to a frame: at 60 Hz a frame is 16,667 µs, so one frame of your game is 800 samples. Anything the game decides once per frame — a volume, a position, a pan — is a decision the mixer has to spread across 800 samples, and §7 is entirely about what happens if you do not.

Amplitude is a number, and its range is a convention. This engine keeps samples in [-1, 1] as 32-bit floats. Nothing physical happens at 1.0 — it is simply the value the hardware treats as full deflection, and a sample of 1.5 is a perfectly well-formed number that the device cannot play. That is the whole of §5's headroom problem, stated a section early.

2.1 A frame is not a sample, and the difference is silent in mono

One piece of vocabulary, because getting it wrong produces a bug that is invisible in testing and obvious in shipping. A sample is one number. A frame is one sample per channel: in stereo, a frame is two samples, left then right, stored next to each other. Duration is counted in frames, buffer sizes are quoted in frames, and the device consumes frames.

A mono sound has one sample per frame, so the two words mean the same thing and code that confuses them works perfectly. Feed it a stereo file and everything plays at double speed, half length, with the channels swapped on alternate frames — which sounds like a corrupt file rather than like an off-by-two. Every count in audio/sound.hpp says which unit it is in for exactly this reason.

3. The two clocks

Since Lesson 1.4 this engine has had one clock. The accumulator runs the simulation at a fixed 60 Hz and draws whenever it can; frames vary in length; a long frame is absorbed by the accumulator and the next one catches up. That design is built around the assumption that being late is survivable.

The device has its own clock and it does not negotiate.

Read the two timelines against each other. On top, the simulation: nine frames, one of them 38 ms instead of 16. The player may notice — probably not — and by the following frame it is over. Nothing is lost; the picture simply held for longer.

Underneath, the device: a buffer of 512 frames consumed every 10.67 ms, on a crystal oscillator, whatever else is happening on the machine. If the data is not there, the device does not wait. It plays what is in the buffer — silence, usually, or the previous buffer again — and that is a dropout.

Here is the part worth internalising, because it is the reason audio programming has a reputation it does not otherwise deserve. A 21-millisecond gap in a picture is invisible. A 21-millisecond gap in a sound is not quiet — it is loud. The gap has an edge at each end: the waveform was at some value, then it is at zero, then it is back. Those are step discontinuities, and a step contains energy at every frequency. Your ear, which is a bank of frequency-selective hair cells, hears broadband energy as a click. §7 measures this directly: of the difference between a correct render and one with a gain discontinuity in it, 98.8% of the energy is not at the tone's own frequency.

So the audio budget is not "be fast on average". It is "never be late", and those are different engineering problems. The mean does not matter; the tail does. A mixer that takes 30 µs for ninety-nine buffers and 12 ms for the hundredth is a mixer that clicks once a second, and its average load is 0.3%.

3.1 What the queue buys you

The margin between those two clocks is the queue: audio you have already mixed and handed to SDL, which the device will consume before it needs anything else from you. It is the only slack in the system, it is measured in milliseconds, and it is exactly the number at the bottom of figure 2.

Which makes the buffer size the single most consequential constant in audio/mixer.hpp, and a straight trade with nothing clever available:

  • Small buffers mean a sound starts sooner after the game asks for it — 128 frames is 2.67 ms of latency — and mean less slack before a late mix becomes a dropout, and mean 375 callbacks a second instead of 94.
  • Large buffers mean margin, and mean a gunshot that fires 43 ms after the trigger, which is about two and a half frames and is perceptible as sloppiness rather than as latency.

512 frames — 10.67 ms at 48 kHz — is the usual compromise and is what this engine defaults to. It is roughly two thirds of a frame of latency, and it gives the mixer a deadline it beats by a factor of 358 (§10).

4. Loading a sound

A file on disk becomes an array of floats. Three steps, and only one of them is interesting.

4.1 The container, and which half to hand away

This course hand-rolls almost everything, and the exceptions have always come with an argument rather than a shrug. Lesson 4.7 wrote it for stb_image: the reason not to write a PNG decoder is not that it is difficult, it is that there is nothing about an engine to be learned from the fifth filter mode. WAV looks like the case where that argument fails, because WAV really is simple — and the harness proves it by writing one: 

put_tag(bytes, "RIFF");
put_u32(bytes, 36u + data_bytes);      // everything after this field
put_tag(bytes, "WAVE");

put_tag(bytes, "fmt ");
put_u32(bytes, 16u);                   // PCM fmt chunk is 16 bytes
put_u16(bytes, 1u);                    // WAVE_FORMAT_PCM
put_u16(bytes, static_cast<std::uint16_t>(channels));
put_u32(bytes, static_cast<std::uint32_t>(freq));
put_u32(bytes, static_cast<std::uint32_t>(freq * channels * 2));   // byte rate
put_u16(bytes, static_cast<std::uint16_t>(channels * 2));          // block align
put_u16(bytes, 16u);                                               // bits

put_tag(bytes, "data");
put_u32(bytes, data_bytes);

Forty-four bytes. That is a complete, valid, standards-conformant WAV header, and a reader for it is twenty lines. So why does load_wav call SDL_LoadWAV instead?

Because the twenty-line reader reads files nobody has. A file exported from Audacity has a LIST chunk before the data. A file from a compressor has a fact chunk. Anything with more than two channels is WAVE_FORMAT_EXTENSIBLE, which is a different fmt layout with a GUID in it. Then IEEE float, A-law, µ-law, ADPCM. The twenty-line reader does not fail on these — it reads the header, finds a chunk it does not recognise where it expected data, and either refuses the file or, worse, interprets the chunk's contents as samples. The harness's control section does exactly this on purpose: 

A.3  CONTROL  junk file loaded: no   samples 0
     CONTROL  missing file loaded: no

That control matters more here than it looks. Forty kilobytes of text interpreted as 16-bit PCM is full-scale white noise — the loudest, most alarming thing a computer can emit — so a loader that returns true on a file it did not understand has handed the mixer a scream. A graphics loader with the same bug produces a wrong picture; this one produces a sound that makes people lunge for the volume knob.

4.2 Widening: six formats in, one out

SDL hands back bytes in the file's own format. The mixer sees exactly one: interleaved 32-bit float. The conversion is a divide by the format's full scale, and it holds one small trap: 

case SDL_AUDIO_S16LE:
case SDL_AUDIO_S16BE:
{
    std::uint16_t raw = 0;
    std::memcpy(&raw, bytes, sizeof(raw));
    raw = SDL_AUDIO_ISBIGENDIAN(fmt) ? SDL_Swap16BE(raw) : SDL_Swap16LE(raw);
    return static_cast<float>(static_cast<std::int16_t>(raw)) / 32768.0f;
}

32768, not 32767. A signed 16-bit sample runs from −32768 to +32767, so dividing by 32768 maps the most negative value to exactly −1.0 and the most positive to 0.99997. Dividing by 32767 instead maps +full-scale to 1.0 and −full-scale to −1.00003, which is a value outside the stated range produced by a file that did nothing wrong. Every audio API in existence takes the first option; the three-hundred-millionths of a decibel it costs at the top end is the correct thing to spend. And the harness confirms the round trip is exact: 

A.1  SDL_AUDIO_S16LE 1ch 48000 Hz -> f32 1ch 48000 Hz
     frames 48000 -> 48000   bytes 96000 -> 192000  (2.00x)
     peak 0.5000  rms 0.3535  over-unity 0  resampled no
     round trip worst error  0.000e+00  (1 LSB = 3.052e-05)

Zero error, because every 16-bit integer is exactly representable in a float and the divisor is a power of two. Note the other number in that block, though: 2.00×. Converting to float doubles the file in memory, permanently, and a four-minute music track that was 21 MB on disk is 44 MB resident. That is the honest cost of the one-format rule, it is paid by every engine that mixes in float, and the fix is streaming — decode a buffer at a time rather than a track at a time — which is a Module 9 problem and is named as one in the header.

The unsigned case is the one that bites people. SDL_AUDIO_U8 is centred on 128, not 0, because the format predates the idea that silence should be zero. Getting that wrong does not sound quiet; it sounds like a very loud DC offset, which most speakers reproduce as a thump followed by nothing, and which looks in a waveform viewer like the file is fine.

4.3 Where this code lives, and the prediction it corrects

Lesson 5.5 argued that the asset system deserved its own directory because "it loads meshes, images, and in Module 7 sounds". The prediction was right about the lesson and wrong about the directory. load_wav is not in asset/; it is in audio/, beside the mixer that consumes it, for the same reason gfx/image.cpp decodes a PNG and lives under gfx/. Which makes audio/ the fourth new directory since Module 5's refactor, and therefore the fourth time the umbrella lint has had to notice a whole subsystem rather than one more file in a familiar folder. It fired. That is six catches in five lessons, and the tally is no longer interesting as a tally — what is interesting is that it keeps firing on people who know it exists. 5. Mixing is addition, and what addition costs

Two sounds arriving at your ear are two pressure variations arriving at your ear, and pressure at a point is a scalar. They add. That is not an approximation or a model — it is superposition, and it is why mixing is one line: 

out[f * 2]      += sl * gl;
out[f * 2 + 1]  += sr * gr;

The harness checks it literally rather than trusting the sentence, because a mixer that did anything else to a single voice would be doing it to every voice: 

B.1  one voice, centre: worst error 7.412e-09
     centre gain per channel 0.707107  (= 1/sqrt(2))
B.2  two voices vs the arithmetic sum: worst 1.442e-08
B.3  CONTROL  zero voices: peak 0.0  rms 0.0

B.3 is not a joke. A mixer that forgets to clear its scratch buffer between calls replays the previous buffer forever, which is not silence and is not obviously a bug — it is a drone that sounds like something in the scene. std::fill first, then +=, and a mix of zero voices is correctly silent rather than uninitialised.

5.1 Sixteen footsteps are not sixteen times as loud

So mixing is addition — and now the question that decides your whole gain structure. If one footstep peaks at 0.1, what does sixteen of them peak at?

The arithmetic bound says 1.6, and if you believe the bound you will divide everything by 16 to stay under full scale. Figure 3 is what actually happens.

The blue line is the RMS of N uncorrelated voices and it lands on √N exactly. Here is why, in three lines. Two uncorrelated signals a and b have E[(a+b)²] = E[a²] + 2E[ab] + E[b²], and the cross term is zero precisely because they are uncorrelated. So the mean squares add:

\[ \mathrm{RMS}_N \;=\; \sqrt{N}\,\cdot\,\mathrm{RMS}_1 \]

…energy adds, so amplitude grows as the square root of the count.

Check it against the table: a voice of amplitude 0.05 uniform noise has RMS 0.05/√3 = 0.0289, which is the N = 1 row, and the rms/a column reads 0.58, 0.82, 1.16, 1.64, 2.32, 3.24, 4.63 for N = 1…64. Divide each by 0.577 and you get 1.00, 1.42, 2.01, 2.84, 4.02, 5.61, 8.02. That is √N to two decimal places, measured, with no fitting.

And the control is what makes it mean something. Mix the same sound sixteen times — now perfectly correlated, the cross term is not zero, amplitudes add — and the peak column reads exactly 1, 2, 4, 8, 16. If that row had also said √N, the meter would be measuring itself.

5.2 …but the peak follows neither, and that is the useful finding

Look again at the amber line in figure 3. The peak of N uncorrelated voices is not √N and it is not N: it is 7.68× at sixteen voices, where √N says 4 and N says 16. That is because the peak is not a property of the signals at all. It is the largest coincidence that happened to occur in the window you looked at, and a longer window contains more chances: 

     16 voices, peak against how long you listen:
       seconds   peak    peak/a
          0.01   0.3490    6.98
          0.10   0.4226    8.45
          1.00   0.5046   10.09
          8.00   0.5232   10.46

The same sixteen voices, the same code, the same gains: 6.98× after ten milliseconds and 10.46× after eight seconds. It has not stopped growing; it is growing logarithmically, and it will keep doing so for as long as the game runs.

So headroom is a bet, not a bound, and the two obvious policies price out like this:

  • 1/N — gain 0.0625 at sixteen voices, which is −24.08 dB. Safe against a coincidence that will never happen, and it throws away twelve decibels of signal you cannot get back. Everything in your game is quiet, so somebody turns the master up, and you are back where you started with the dynamic range spent.
  • 1/√N — gain 0.25, which is −12.04 dB. Correct on average, occasionally exceeded, and what real engines do — usually with a limiter behind it to catch the occasions.

And if you budget for neither, the harness prices that too: thirty-two voices at 0.1 each peak at 1.2675 and clip 33 samples out of 40,960. Which sounds negligible — 0.08% — and is the subject of the next section.

5.3 What clipping actually does

The last thing mix_into does is clamp: 

if (mag > 1.0f)
{
    ++local_clipped;
    v = v > 0.0f ? 1.0f : -1.0f;
}

The clamp is a last resort and not a plan, and figure 4 is why.

Clipping replaces the peaks of a waveform with straight lines. A straight line is not part of a sine, so what you now have is a sine plus something else — and because the clipping is symmetric and periodic, that something else is a series of odd harmonics. Measured: 

  gain  peak   clipped     H3      H5      H7    THD
  1.00  0.500         0  -166.8  -160.3  -159.7   0.00%
  2.00  1.000         0  -166.8  -160.3  -159.7   0.00%
  3.00  1.000     27731   -16.5   -35.7   -32.5  15.22%
  6.00  1.000     40532   -11.0   -18.5   -27.5  31.00%

At gain 3.0 the third harmonic is 16.5 dB below the fundamental. That is not a subtle artefact; it is a buzz you would describe as "distorted" without needing to be told. Total harmonic distortion is 15.22%, against a specification of about 0.01% for any amplifier you would buy.

Two rows are controls and both matter. Gain 1.0 and gain 2.0 report harmonics at −160 dB, which is the measurement's floor rather than the signal's — a sine has no harmonics, so those numbers are the Goertzel estimator's numerical noise, and quoting them establishes what "zero" looks like on this instrument. And gain 2.0 is the interesting one: a 0.5-amplitude tone times 2.0 is exactly 1.000, the peak column says 1.000, and nothing clips. The clamp is >, not >=, and full scale is a legal value.

Why 0.08% of samples is audible. Thirty-two voices clipping 33 samples out of 40,960 sounds like a rounding error, and it is not, for the reason §3 gave: each clipped sample is a place where the waveform's slope changes abruptly, and an abrupt slope change is broadband. You do not hear 0.08% of a distortion — you hear 33 clicks. This is the general shape of every audio bug in this lesson: the quantity that matters is almost never the proportion of samples affected, it is whether the affected ones are discontinuous with their neighbours.

6. Where a sound is, in two numbers

Everything so far would work in a game with no world in it. This section is 3D audio, and the entire model is: take a listener and an emitter, produce two gains. One for the left channel, one for the right. Occlusion, reverb, HRTFs, ambisonics — every refinement that exists is a refinement of these two numbers, and every one of them still multiplies by them.

6.1 A listener is a transform

Six modules of this course have used a placement to decide what is drawn. A camera is a transform, a light is a transform, a joint is a transform. Here a placement decides what is heard, and nothing about transform needs to change for it: 

listener listener_from(const transform& placement)
{
    listener l;
    l.position = placement.position;
    l.right    = rotate(placement.rotation, vec3{1.0f, 0.0f, 0.0f});
    l.up       = rotate(placement.rotation, vec3{0.0f, 1.0f, 0.0f});
    // THE MINUS. conventions.html §2: this course's world space is right-handed,
    // Y-up, -Z forward, so the direction a placement faces is where -ẑ lands.
    l.forward  = rotate(placement.rotation, vec3{0.0f, 0.0f, -1.0f});
    return l;
}

That the subsystem sharing nothing with graphics shares its most basic type with graphics is the clearest evidence this course can offer that transform was the right thing to build back in Module 2. It is also the first time in this course that a placement is used for something that is not drawing.

One warning, which is a real bug in real games: the listener is usually the camera and must not be assumed to be. A third-person game looks from behind the character and hears from the character, and a game that conflates the two has footsteps arriving from six metres away. So listener is its own struct, built by a named function, and the demo deliberately drives it from something that is not a camera.

6.2 The pan parameter is one dot product

Where is the sound, left to right? Take the direction from the listener to the emitter and dot it with the listener's right axis:

\[ \mathrm{pan} \;=\; \hat{d} \cdot \hat{r} \;\in\; [-1, 1] \]

…pan is the component of the direction-to-source along the listener's right axis.

Which is the sine of the angle off the median plane, and is therefore already the quantity a pan law wants — no atan2, no normalisation of an angle, no special cases. Two consequences fall out for free, and both are visible in the right-hand panel of figure 5.

Elevation collapses to the centre, correctly. A sound directly overhead is perpendicular to right, so its pan is 0 — which is exactly where two speakers can put it. Had you projected onto the ground plane and taken an atan2, a sound at the zenith would have had an undefined azimuth and you would have needed a special case for it.

Front and back are indistinguishable. The 45° and 135° rows of the orbit table both give pan +0.71; the sound in front and the sound behind produce identical gains. That is not a bug in the dot product, it is what a stereo pair can express — with two speakers in front of you there is no gain pair that says "behind". Real systems fix it with head-related transfer functions, which need a measured impulse response per ear per direction and a convolution per voice, and are named in the header as the beginning of the missing ten percent.

6.3 Two pan laws, and the hole in the middle of one

Now turn the pan value into two gains. The first rule anybody writes is the one that makes the gains sum to one:

\[ L = 1 - u, \qquad R = u, \qquad u = \tfrac{\mathrm{pan}+1}{2} \]

It is correct at both ends — hard left is (1, 0), hard right is (0, 1) — and it is wrong everywhere in between, for a reason that is the same one as §5.1. The two speakers are playing the same signal, so they are perfectly correlated, so they add in amplitude at your ear — but their powers are what determines how loud that is. Compute the power the pair delivers:

\[ P(u) \;=\; L^2 + R^2 \;=\; (1-u)^2 + u^2 \]

At the ends, P = 1. At the centre, P = 0.25 + 0.25 = 0.5. Half the power is −3.01 dB, and it is a hole in the middle of your stereo image: a sound swept from left to right audibly ducks as it crosses. Measured across a 201-point sweep: 

E.2  over a full sweep, worst deviation from unity power:
       linear           3.010 dB   (min power 0.5000)
       constant power   0.000 dB   (min power 1.0000)

So ask for the law that keeps L² + R² = 1 instead. That is the equation of a circle, and the parameterisation of a circle is a cosine and a sine:

\[ L = \cos\theta, \qquad R = \sin\theta, \qquad \theta = \frac{\pi}{4}\,(\mathrm{pan} + 1) \]

…sweep θ from 0 to π/2 as pan goes −1 to +1, and L² + R² = cos²θ + sin²θ = 1 identically.

The whole of the difference is one number. At the centre, θ = π/4 and both gains are cos(π/4) = 0.7071 rather than 0.5. If you ever wondered why audio people write 0.707 everywhere, that is the reason: it is the gain that puts half the power in each of two speakers, and half the power is not half the amplitude.

The control is the one that makes the comparison mean anything: at hard left and hard right the two laws must agree exactly, because both are (1, 0) and (0, 1). Measured difference 0.000e+00 and 4.371e−08 — the second being cos(π/2), which is not quite zero in floating point. Worth a sentence, because a gain of −4.4×10⁻⁸ is technically a phase inversion; at that magnitude it is 147 dB below the signal and nothing can hear it.

6.4 Distance: the exponent is 1, not 2

Now the other number. A sound gets quieter with distance, and everybody remembers the inverse square law. Everybody is right about the law and wrong about what it applies to.

Energy radiating from a point spreads over the surface of an expanding sphere. The area of that sphere is 4πd², so the energy per unit area — the intensity — falls as:

\[ I \;\propto\; \frac{1}{d^{2}} \]

That part is correct and is the part people quote. But a sample is not an intensity. A sample is a pressure, and intensity is proportional to pressure squared — the same relationship as power to amplitude, and the same factor of two that put the 20 rather than the 10 in the decibel formula. So:

\[ p \;\propto\; \sqrt{I} \;\propto\; \frac{1}{d} \]

…the number you multiply a sample by falls as 1/d, not 1/d².

Get this wrong and every sound in your game falls off twice as fast in decibels. Measured: 

F.2  dB per doubling of distance, 2 m -> 32 m:
       2.0 ->   4.0   1/d  -6.02 dB    1/d^2 -12.04 dB
       4.0 ->   8.0   1/d  -6.02 dB    1/d^2 -12.04 dB

−6 dB per doubling is the physical answer and is the number to keep in your head; −12 is what a plausible-sounding mistake costs, and it sounds like every sound in the game is at the bottom of a well.

6.5 …and then the physical law has to be made to end

Figure 6 also shows the problem with being physically correct. 1/d never reaches zero. At fifty metres a sound is at ref/d = 1/50 = 0.0200, which is −33.98 dB — quiet, certainly, and audible, and still costing a voice slot and a trip through the inner loop.

So every engine puts a max_distance on it, and the obvious implementation — clamp the curve and cull past the edge — creates exactly the artefact this whole lesson is about. The sound is at −34 dB, and then it is at −∞, and the difference between them happens between one buffer and the next. That is a step. A step is a click. It is quiet, and it is a click.

falloff::inverse_ranged is this engine's default and is four lines: take the inverse curve, subtract what it would have been at max_distance, and rescale so the value at ref_distance is still 1.

\[ g_{\text{ranged}}(d) \;=\; \frac{g_{\text{inv}}(d) - g_{\text{inv}}(d_{\max})}{1 - g_{\text{inv}}(d_{\max})} \]

It is 1 at the reference distance, exactly 0 at the maximum, and physical in between. What it costs is honest and visible in the figure: subtracting a constant steepens the far field — at 32 m the bare inverse law is at 0.0312 and the ranged one at 0.0115, which is 8.7 dB quieter. That is the price of a curve that ends, and the harness prints both so the choice is made with the number in front of you rather than behind you: 

F.3  bare `inverse` at max_distance = 50 m:
       gain 0.0200 = -33.98 dB, then cut to silence.
       `inverse_ranged` there: 0.000000 = -120.00 dB
       and at 49.0 m: 0.000416  (approach, not a cliff)

6.6 The whole thing, with real numbers

Put it together on one emitter. Listener at the origin, unrotated, so right is (1, 0, 0). Emitter at (3, 0, −4) with a gain of 0.8, the default reference and maximum distances of 1 m and 50 m, and the ranged inverse law.

Distance. |(3, 0, −4)| = √(9 + 16) = 5.

Attenuation. The bare inverse law at 5 m with ref = 1 is 1/(1 + 1·(5 − 1)) = 0.2. At 50 m it would be 1/(1 + 49) = 0.02. So the ranged value is (0.2 − 0.02)/(1 − 0.02) = 0.18/0.98 = 0.1837.

Pan. The unit direction is (0.6, 0, −0.8), and its dot product with (1, 0, 0) is 0.6.

Gains. θ = (0.6 + 1)·π/4 = 1.2566 rad = 72°, so L = cos(72°) = 0.3090 and R = sin(72°) = 0.9511. Multiply both by emitter.gain × attenuation = 0.8 × 0.1837 = 0.14696:

\[ L = 0.0454, \qquad R = 0.1397 \]

And the harness agrees to every digit it prints: 

F.5  worked example: emitter at (3, 0, -4), gain 0.8
       distance 5.0000   attenuation 0.1837
       pan 0.6000  ->  L 0.0454  R 0.1397
       power 0.0216 = -16.66 dB

7. The gain is a lossy reconstruction too

§6 produces a pair of gains. The game recomputes them once a frame, because that is when things move. The mixer needs a gain per sample, and there are 800 samples in a frame.

That sentence should look familiar, because it is Lesson 7.7's, transposed. There the rule was:

A lossy transformation must be fitted with its consumer's exact reconstruction.

7.7 found it in keyframe reduction — a curve fitted with slerp and played back with nlerp overshoots its budget by 4.5×, and nothing reports it. Here the same structure appears in a subsystem that has never heard of animation. The gain curve is sampled at 60 Hz and reconstructed at 48 kHz, and the reconstruction is either a zero-order hold or a linear one. Writing the setter the obvious way — 

v->gain = g;   // and the mix multiplies by v->gain

— chooses the zero-order hold, which means the gain is constant for 800 samples and then jumps. Figure 7 is what that sounds like.

7.1 How big is the jump, really?

The size of the discontinuity is Δg × |v|: the change in gain times whatever the waveform happened to be at that instant. So it is worst when the gain is changing fastest and the signal is near a peak — which is exactly the moment an emitter passes closest to the listener. The harness flies one past at 20 m/s with a closest approach of one metre: 

G.1  the gains a 60 Hz game would push, near the pass:
       t(s)    x      dist    L       R     dL/frame
      1.983  -0.33   1.054  0.8144  0.4849   0.0354
      2.000   0.00   1.000  0.7071  0.7071   0.1073
      2.017   0.33   1.054  0.4849  0.8144   0.2222
       worst gain change in one frame: 0.2222

A gain change of 0.2222 in a single frame. And here is what it does to the samples, measured against the only honest yardstick — the largest step the waveform itself ever takes: 

G.2  largest jump between adjacent samples (left):
       the signal's own slope   0.013088
       gain applied as a step   0.100745  (7.70x)
       gain applied as a ramp   0.013072  (1.00x)

The step injects a jump 7.7 times larger than anything in the signal. The ramp injects nothing at all: 1.00×, which is to say the ramped render's largest sample-to-sample change is the waveform's own motion and the gain has become invisible.

7.2 Where the energy goes

A 7.7× jump sounds like it should be obvious, but "7.7 times a small number" is still a small number, and the interesting question is why you can hear it at all when it is a fifth of full scale for one sample out of eight hundred. Subtract the two renders and look at the difference: 

G.3  step minus ramp, over one second:
       rms of the difference    2.608e-02
       at the tone's own 200 Hz 2.847e-03  (1.2% of it)
       the rest is broadband: that is the click.

1.2%. Only a fiftieth of the injected energy sits at the frequency of the sound being played; the other 98.8% is spread across every frequency there is, which is what a step discontinuity contains and what the ear is exquisitely good at noticing. You are not hearing a level change. You are hearing sixty impulses a second, and impulses sixty times a second is a 200 Hz buzz riding on top of your sound effect.

The fix is four lines in the inner loop, and it is a linear reconstruction instead of a zero-order one: 

const float dl = (v.tgt_left - v.cur_left) * inv_frames;
const float dr = (v.tgt_right - v.cur_right) * inv_frames;
float gl = v.cur_left;
float gr = v.cur_right;
// ... and inside the per-sample loop:
gl += dl;
gr += dr;

Spread the same change over every sample in the buffer and the largest step any single sample sees is 1/512 of it. That is the entire fix, it costs two adds per sample, and it is why mixer::set_gain does not simply assign.

The control is the one that makes G.2 believable: hold the gain constant, where a ramp has nothing to interpolate and the two paths must agree bit for bit. Worst difference 8.941e−09, which is float rounding on a multiply. So the difference measured in G.2 is a difference between two reconstructions, not between two code paths.

7.3 The same argument one level up: a sound that starts on a peak

The ramp fixes discontinuities the mixer creates. It cannot fix one that is in the content — and the most common one in any game is a sample that does not begin at zero: 

G.5  a sound that starts on a peak:
       first sample value       0.2500000
       worst step, no fade      0.2500000  (38.2x)
       worst step, 5 ms fade    0.0065443  (1.00x)
       the waveform's own slope 0.0065450

Before a voice starts, the mixer's buffer holds silence. Its first sample is therefore preceded by zero, so a sound whose first sample is 0.25 steps by 0.25 — 38 times the largest step the waveform itself ever takes. A five-millisecond fade brings that to 0.0065443, and the row underneath is the point: 0.0065450 is A·2πf/rate, the waveform's own slope. The fade does not make the step small. It removes the step, and what is left is the sound.

Which is why apply_fade is in the public header despite being three lines, and why every looping tone in the demo is faded at both ends: a one-second 440 Hz tone does not end where it began, so the loop point is a step, once a second, forever.

8. Pitch and rate: one cursor, two jobs

A voice's playhead is a double counting source frames, and it advances by

\[ \mathrm{step} \;=\; \mathrm{pitch} \times \frac{f_{\text{source}}}{f_{\text{device}}} \]

per output frame. One number carries both jobs, because they are the same job: playing a 44.1 kHz file on a 48 kHz device means reading 0.91875 source frames per output frame, and playing a sound an octave up means reading 2 of them. Neither lands on an integer, so both need the same thing — a value between two samples.

Linear interpolation is the two-tap answer: take the two neighbours and weight them by how far between them you are. It is what fetch() does in the mixer and what resample_linear() does at load, deliberately written once and used twice, because two copies of an interpolation rule is two copies that will eventually disagree.

8.1 And it is not very good, with a number

This is the place where a course can either say "linear interpolation is fine" and move on, or measure it. Measured, on a 1 kHz tone resampled from 44.1 to 48 kHz: 

H.1  a 1 kHz tone, 44100 -> 48000, measured in the middle:
       ours (linear, 2 taps)      62.40 dB SNR
       SDL_ConvertAudioSamples         85.05 dB SNR
H.2  CONTROL  no resample at all        91.67 dB SNR

The control is what makes the other two readable. Loading the same file at its own rate — no resampling at all — gives 91.67 dB, which is the 16-bit quantisation floor and nothing else: 6.02 × 16 = 96.3 dB for a full-scale sine, minus 1.76 dB for the sine's crest factor, minus 6 dB because this tone is at half scale. About 88–92 dB is the arithmetic, 91.67 is the measurement, and now we know what the meter reads when the answer is "perfect".

Against that, our resampler throws away 29 dB and SDL's throws away 7. That gap is real and it is the difference between a two-tap filter and a properly designed one — a good resampler is a windowed sinc with dozens of taps and a design procedure, and it is an entire subject rather than a paragraph.

8.2 The number is a fact about your content, not about your code

The honest version of that result is the one that shows how much it moves: 

H.3  the same resampler at four frequencies:
       tone      SNR
         200 Hz    89.00 dB
        1000 Hz    62.40 dB
        4000 Hz    38.01 dB
       10000 Hz    20.43 dB

Same code, same rates, same everything — and the answer ranges from "indistinguishable from perfect" to "audibly wrong". Linear interpolation is a low-pass filter: it draws a chord between two samples, the chord is a poor fit when the signal moves a lot between them, and how much it moves depends on frequency relative to the sample rate. At 200 Hz there are 220 samples per cycle and the chords are indistinguishable from the curve. At 10 kHz there are four, and joining four points with straight lines does not make a sine.

Which gives the practical rule this engine follows, stated as a preference rather than as a guarantee: ship files at the device's rate. A sound that does not need resampling does not pay for it, and the only reliable way to get a good resample is to do it offline in a tool that can afford the taps.

9. The voice table, and a thread you do not own

A voice is one sound currently playing: a pointer to the sound, a cursor, a step, four gains and two flags. Fifty-six bytes. The mixer holds up to 64 of them and walks the live ones once per buffer.

9.1 Twenty-five lessons later, the case handles were built for

Lesson 5.4 built generational handles and justified them on meshes and textures, which was honest but not compelling — a mesh lives for the whole level, and a raw pointer to one is wrong only in the abstract. A voice is the opposite, and it is the case that makes the design inevitable:

  • It exists for as long as a footstep — a third of a second.
  • It ends on a thread you do not control, at a moment you did not choose.
  • Its slot is immediately reused by the next footstep, because footsteps are the most common thing in the game.

A caller holding a voice* across a second is holding a pointer to a different sound. The symptom is the kind that gets filed as unreproducible: turning down the explosion made the music quiet. There is no plausible mechanism, it happens minutes later, and it depends on how many sounds played in between.

With a voice_id, the stale handle simply stops resolving, and every function that takes one returns false rather than acting on the wrong voice. That is not defensive programming — "the sound you are asking about has finished" is a true and useful answer, and it is the normal answer for a fire-and-forget effect: 

/// Fire and forget. The handle returned here is DROPPED — which is the
/// normal way to play a sound effect and is exactly the case generational
/// handles exist for: the slot is reused within a second, and nothing that
/// kept this id can ever act on the sound that replaces it.
void ping()
{
    const transform placement{.position = listener_pos_, .rotation = listener_rot_};
    const voice_id id = mixer_.play_spatial(ping_, listener_from(placement),
                                            sources_[0].e, {});
    pings_ += id.valid() ? 1 : 0;
}

The declaration in the public header is the other half of the trick, and it is handle<T>'s phantom parameter used for exactly what it is for: 

struct voice;                    // never defined in any header
using voice_id = handle<voice>;  // four bytes, and T is never stored

A caller may hold, copy, compare and store handles to voices without the layout of one ever being public. voice is completed in mixer.cpp and nowhere else.

9.2 The one shared mutable thing, and the mutex on it

SDL runs the mix on its own thread. The game calls play, set_gain and stop on the main thread. The voice table is touched by both, which makes it the only shared mutable state in the subsystem — the sounds themselves are immutable (§4's asset/playhead split, for the third lesson running) and everything else is a counter.

This engine guards it with one mutex, and the header says so rather than pretending otherwise:

A mutex on a real-time thread is a compromise and this comment is where it is admitted. If the main thread is inside play() when the callback fires, the callback waits — and on a machine under load the OS can descend into priority inversion, where the audio thread blocks on a lock held by a thread that has been descheduled.

The correct answer is a lock-free command queue: the main thread pushes "start voice 7" into a ring buffer and the audio thread drains it, so neither ever waits. That needs the memory-ordering vocabulary of Module 9's job system, which is why it is there and not here. What makes the mutex acceptable in the meantime is that the critical sections on the main thread are a handful of stores, and that we can measure them: 

I.2  set_gain (lock, resolve, two stores): 9.0 ns

Nine nanoseconds, uncontended, and bounded — there is no loop in there and no allocation. Against a 10,667 µs deadline that is a rounding error even if every voice's gain is set in the same microsecond the callback fires.

Two smaller decisions follow from the same constraint, and both are worth naming because they look like sloppiness and are not:

The audio thread never removes a voice. When a non-looping sound runs off its end, mix_into sets finished = true and moves on; mixer::update() does the actual pool::remove on the main thread, next frame. No container is mutated on the real-time path, so no reallocation can happen there.

report() does not take the lock — except for one field. The counters are std::atomic and are read with memory_order_relaxed, so a debug panel drawing at 60 Hz cannot stall the audio thread. A relaxed read of a counter can be one increment out of date, which for a diagnostic is exactly right. live_voices is the exception and takes the lock, because it is a container's size rather than a counter, and reading a size while another thread inserts is a race rather than a stale number.

9.3 The hard rule, and the one thing in this file that is undefined behaviour

A voice holds const sound*. Non-owning, raw, and deliberate: a four-megabyte sound is not going to be copied per voice, and a shared_ptr would put a refcount decrement — and therefore possibly a free — on the audio thread.

So the rule is: the sound must outlive the voice, and the discipline that makes it true is stop_sound() immediately before releasing any sound. It is the one rule in this subsystem whose violation is undefined behaviour rather than a counter, and devcheck_78.cpp demonstrates it in code that is meant to be copied: 

// The lifetime rule, obeyed: `tone` is about to go out of scope and the
// mixer holds a pointer into it. `close()` would do this too; saying it
// explicitly is what the rule looks like in code you are meant to copy.
mix.stop_sound(tone);
mix.close();

9.4 Offline, because real-time code you cannot call from a test is undebuggable

mix_into is public, and mixer::open_offline() builds the voice table with no device at all. Everything works afterwards except making a noise.

That is not a testing convenience bolted on afterwards; it is what makes the subsystem legible. Offline, the real-time path is a pure function from a voice table to an array of floats, and an array of floats can be diffed, plotted and asserted on. Every waveform in this lesson and every number in verify_78.cpp was produced this way, on a machine that was never asked whether it had speakers. The demo uses it too: --shot opens no device, so a screenshot is silent, deterministic, and reproducible on a build machine.

9.5 …which leaves exactly one thing untested, and it found a bug

An offline mix never opens a device, never runs SDL's callback, never resumes a stream and never shuts one down. Those are precisely the paths that are either completely right or completely silent, so devcheck_78.cpp exists to exercise them: four lines of setup, a tone, 700 milliseconds, and a report.

Its first run said this: 

freq 44100  buffers 58  frames 29696  starved 28  clipped 0
live 1  peak 0.0141  mix max 25.83 us of 11609.98 us (0.223%)

Twenty-eight of fifty-eight buffers starved, on a mix using 0.2% of its budget, on a quiet machine, with nothing audibly wrong. That is not a dropout problem; that is the shape of a counter measuring the wrong event.

And it was. The counter incremented when SDL_GetAudioStreamQueued() returned zero as the callback began, on the reasoning that an empty queue means the device has already run out of what we last gave it. The reasoning is wrong for SDL3's pull model: the callback is invoked because the device wants more, so finding the queue drained at that instant is the steady state, not a failure.

The counter is now called queue_empty and says what it observes. The question it was meant to answer is answered by late, which compares the mixer's own time against the mixer's own deadline and needs nothing from the driver at all: 

// The deadline for THIS chunk, not for a full buffer: the callback is
// free to ask for a partial one, and charging a 64-frame chunk with a
// 512-frame budget would hide exactly the overrun this counter exists to
// find.
const double chunk_budget_us = cfg_.freq > 0
    ? 1e6 * static_cast<double>(frames) / static_cast<double>(cfg_.freq)
    : 0.0;
if (chunk_budget_us > 0.0 && us > chunk_budget_us)
{
    st_->late.fetch_add(1, std::memory_order_relaxed);
}

A healthy run now prints late 0, which is a check whose degenerate case is a failure. That is the general lesson, and it is 7.7's arriving in a new subsystem for the fourth lesson running: a control that fires on the healthy case tells you nothing about the sick one. The old counter would have been "working" — printing non-zero — for the entire life of the project, and the day a real dropout happened it would have said nothing new.

10. The budget

Everything above has been about correctness. This section is about the deadline, and the numbers are unexpectedly comfortable — which is itself the finding, because it relocates the danger. 

I.1  buffer 512 frames at 48000 Hz = 10.667 ms of audio.
     voices    us/buffer   % of budget   ns/voice/frame
          0       0.619         0.01%           0.00
          1       1.032         0.01%           2.01
          4       2.304         0.02%           1.13
         16       7.957         0.07%           0.97
         32      15.056         0.14%           0.92
         64      29.761         0.28%           0.91

Sixty-four simultaneous voices — each one a fractional-step read with a linear interpolation, two multiplies, two adds and two gain increments — cost 29.76 µs of a 10,667 µs budget. That is 0.28%, and the per-voice-per-frame column settles at 0.91 ns, which on this machine is about three cycles.

Two things in that table are worth a sentence each. The zero-voice row is not zero: 0.62 µs goes on clearing the buffer and on the master-gain-and-clip pass, which touches all 1,024 samples whatever is playing. And the per-voice cost falls from 2.01 ns to 0.91 ns as voices are added, because that fixed cost is being amortised — the first voice is charged for the whole scan.

These are the only numbers in this lesson that move. Run the harness twice and diff the two transcripts: every section from A to H comes back byte for byte identical — the round-trip error, the √N table, the pan powers, the distance curves, the injected step, the resampler's SNR — because all of them are arithmetic on the same inputs. Section I moves by two or three per cent, because it is a timing, and a timing on a general-purpose machine is a measurement of the machine as much as of the code. Quote it to three decimals if you like; believe the first two.

That split is worth noticing as a property of the harness rather than as a caveat. Nine sections and only one of them needs a warm-up spin, a best-of-three and a control for the clock's own overhead — and it is the only one whose claims are about the machine.

10.1 So the danger is not the mixing

If sixty-four voices are 0.28% of the budget, the thing that will cause a dropout is not the mixing. It is anything unbounded that happens on that thread, and the list is short and absolute:

  • No allocation. new, push_back, std::string — all of them can take a global heap lock that the main thread is holding, for an unbounded time. The scratch buffer is sized in open() and never grows.
  • No logging and no printf. Both do I/O.
  • No unbounded waiting. One mutex, held for the length of the mix, with the compromise stated in §9.2.
  • No exceptions and no RTTI. The engine-core rule from 5.3, which here is also a latency rule.

The second one is the one people argue about, so it is measured: 

I.3  one log line from inside the mix:
       format only, output discarded        82.0 ns
       format + write + flush             1021.6 ns

A single log line costs 1.02 µs — more than mixing four voices. But the number is not the argument, and this is the part worth carrying away: 1,022 ns is a mean, taken on an idle machine writing to a local file. It is a syscall, and a syscall has no upper bound. The same line costs milliseconds when the terminal is a pipe nobody is reading, or the file is on a network share, or the disk is busy. Sixty-four voices cost 29.8 µs and cannot cost more; one unlucky log line can cost more than the whole buffer's deadline.

Which is why everything the audio thread wants to say, it says by incrementing a counter — clipped, late, voices_finished, peak — and the main thread reads them through report() and logs them if it likes.

10.2 And on real hardware

device   44100 Hz   512 frames   budget 11609.98 us
buffers  58   frames 29696   live voices 1
late     0   queue_empty 28   clipped 0
peak     0.0141   worst mix 11.29 us (0.097% of budget)

Note the device rate: 44,100 Hz, not the 48,000 this engine defaults to. That is the machine this was written on, and it is why mixer_config::freq defaults to 0 meaning "ask the device" — a mixer that assumed 48 kHz here would have had SDL resample every buffer, silently, adding a filter to the path that no measurement in this lesson accounts for.

11. What this does not do

The honest list, because every one of these is a thing a shipping game has and this engine does not:

  • Streaming. Every sound is fully decoded in memory, so a four-minute music track costs 44 MB. Module 9's job system is where a decode-a-buffer-at-a-time path belongs, because it needs a worker thread that is not the audio thread.
  • Compressed formats. WAV only. Ogg Vorbis and Opus are what games actually ship, and both are decoder libraries with the same "why we don't hand-roll this" argument as stb_image.
  • Effects. No filters, no reverb, no submix buses, no compressor. A distance low-pass — air absorbs treble, so distant sounds are duller as well as quieter — is two lines and is an exercise.
  • Voice stealing. A play past max_voices is refused and counted. §5.1's arithmetic is why that is less brutal than it sounds: the 65th simultaneous sound contributes 1/√65 of the level, which is 0.6 dB. A real engine steals the quietest voice instead, which is an exercise.
  • Front/back and elevation. §6.2 — two speakers cannot express it. HRTFs are where the next ten percent begins.
  • Doppler. A pitch ratio computed from relative velocity along the line of sight. The machinery is already here (set_pitch); it is an exercise.
  • A lock-free voice queue. §9.2, and Module 9.

14. Common pitfalls & debugging

Symptom: complete silence, and every counter reads zero.
Cause: the stream was never resumed. SDL_OpenAudioDeviceStream returns a stream that is paused, and this is a deliberate change from SDL2, where opening a device started it. Fix: SDL_ResumeAudioStreamDevice(stream) after opening. The tell is that report().buffers stays at 0 — the callback is not running at all, as opposed to running and producing silence.

Symptom: complete silence, and buffers is climbing.
Now the callback is running, so the problem is in the mix. Check live_voices: if it is zero, either play returned a null handle (the table was full, or the sound was empty) or update() retired the voice immediately, which happens when the sound has zero frames. If live_voices is right and peak is 0.0000, the gains are zero — which on a spatial voice usually means the emitter is past max_distance.

Symptom: everything plays at the wrong speed and the channels sound scrambled.
Frames confused with samples somewhere. In mono the two are the same number, so this bug is invisible until the first stereo file. Grep for anything that divides a buffer length without dividing by channels.

Symptom: a very loud thump at the start, then normal sound.
An unsigned 8-bit file widened as if it were signed. SDL_AUDIO_U8 is centred on 128, so treating it as signed produces a constant offset of about −1.0 for the whole file, which is a DC step at the start and a DC step at the end. In a waveform viewer the file looks fine, because the shape is right and only the baseline is wrong.

Symptom: a regular tick, roughly sixty times a second, under a moving sound.
§7. The gain is being applied as a step. Confirm it without ears: render the voice offline twice, once with the gains pushed and once with them held constant, subtract, and look at where the energy is. If it is broadband, it is the reconstruction.

Symptom: a click exactly once per loop.
The sound's last sample does not meet its first. apply_fade(s, 0.004f, 0.004f), and §7.3 for why the fade removes the step rather than shrinking it. If the sound is a musical tone, the alternative fix is to author it at a whole number of cycles.

Symptom: a harsh buzz that gets worse as more sounds play.
report().clipped is non-zero. Lower master_gain; §5.1 for how much. Do not make the clamp cleverer — by the time it runs, the damage is already in the sum.

Symptom: sounds are correct but everything is a semitone and a half sharp.
A 44.1 kHz file being played at 48 kHz without resampling: the ratio is 1.0884, which is 1.47 semitones. Check wav_report::resampled, and check that the rate you passed to load_wav is the device's rate and not a constant you assumed.

Symptom: a crash on level unload, in the audio callback, with a stack that makes no sense.
A sound was destroyed while a voice was still reading it (§9.3). Call mixer::stop_sound() before releasing any sound, or stop_all() before tearing down a level. This is the one failure in this subsystem that is undefined behaviour rather than a counter, which is why it is the last item here and the loudest comment in the header.

Symptom: occasional clicks under load that you cannot reproduce.
Check report().late. If it is non-zero, something on the audio thread is taking longer than the buffer's deadline — and §10 says it will not be the mixing. Look for anything that allocates, logs, or locks something the main thread holds for a long time.

15. Exercises

  1. Hear the step (guided). Add a --step flag to the demo that bypasses the ramp: keep a bool in the app, and when it is set, call set_gain twice in a row with the same value so that cur_ and tgt_ are already equal when the buffer runs. Listen to the fly-by with it on and off.

    Hint: the two calls work because mix_into assigns cur_ = tgt_ at the end of every buffer, so the second call finds nothing to ramp. Then check your ears against the meter: render both offline and compare max_step, exactly as §G.2 does.

  2. Distance low-pass (guided). Air absorbs high frequencies, so a distant sound is duller as well as quieter — and this is a large part of why a distance model with only a gain in it sounds like a volume knob rather than like distance. Add a one-pole low-pass per voice: y += a * (x - y), with a going from 1.0 at ref_distance to about 0.15 at max_distance.

    Hint: y is per-voice state and belongs beside the cursor, one per channel. Ramp a across the buffer for the same reason the gain is ramped — a filter coefficient that jumps is a click too, and this is the cheapest way to prove to yourself that §7's rule was about reconstruction and not about gain.

  3. Doppler. Compute the relative velocity of emitter and listener along the line between them and turn it into a pitch ratio: (c + vl) / (c + ve), with c around 340 m/s. Feed it to set_pitch. Then find the case that breaks it.

    Hint: the case is an emitter approaching at or above the speed of sound, where the denominator reaches zero. Clamp the ratio; games clamp it far tighter than physics requires (typically to about [0.5, 2]) because a correct Doppler on a fast projectile is unlistenable. Also: your velocity must be measured over the fixed step, not over the frame — Lesson 1.4.

  4. Voice stealing. When the table is full, instead of refusing, find the voice with the lowest current gain and replace it. Then measure whether it was worth it: play 96 overlapping sounds through both policies, render offline, and subtract.

    Hint: the interesting part is not the search, it is the discontinuity. Stealing a voice mid-sample is a step to zero, which is §7 again; a real implementation ramps the stolen voice to silence over a few milliseconds before reusing its slot. Your subtraction should show the stealing policy is worse until you do that.

  5. The looping interpolation bug (engine challenge). fetch() clamps i1 to the last frame, so the final fraction of a looping sound interpolates toward itself instead of toward frame 0 — named in the comment and not fixed. Quantify it: build a sound whose last and first samples differ a lot, loop it, and measure the error at the wrap with max_step. Then implement the fix and measure what the extra branch costs in §I.1's table.

    Hint: the branch is in the innermost loop in the engine, which is why the comment declines to guess. Consider a branchless version: since a looping voice's wrap index is 0, i1 = (i0 + 1) % frames is one modulo — but a modulo is a divide, so measure that too rather than assuming it is cheaper than a well-predicted branch.

  6. A limiter (engine challenge). §5.1 leaves the headroom question with two unsatisfying answers. Implement the third: a look-ahead limiter. Delay the mix by a few milliseconds, look at the peak in the window ahead, and pull the gain down smoothly before the peak arrives rather than clamping it when it does.

    Hint: a few milliseconds of delay is a ring buffer sized in open(), because you may not allocate on this thread. The gain reduction must attack fast and release slowly — and if you make it ramp per sample you have already written the mechanism, in §7. Measure it by playing 32 voices at 0.1 and comparing clipped and the THD of §D against the clamp.

16. Recap & next

The engine can hear. Concretely, it gained:

  • A sound asset — WAV in, interleaved float out, six source formats widened and any rate resampled, with a report that counts what it found and what it cost.
  • A mixer — a voice table behind generational handles, gain ramping, pitch, looping, a master gain, a clip counter, and a report with eleven fields the audio thread fills in without ever logging.
  • A spatial model — a listener built from a transform, four distance laws including one of our own that ends without a cliff, and the constant-power pan law derived rather than copied.
  • An offline path — open_offline and a public mix_into, which is what makes every number in this lesson reproducible on a machine with no sound card, and what let the subsystem be tested before it ever made a noise.

And four findings worth carrying past this lesson, none of which is about audio:

Energy adds, amplitudes do not. The √N result, the 3.01 dB pan hole and the 20 rather than 10 in the decibel formula are all the same fact wearing three hats. Whenever you combine two signals that are not correlated, the quantity that adds is the square.

Every sampled quantity has a reconstruction, and choosing it by accident is choosing the worst one. 7.7 found this in keyframes and thought it was about animation. It is about any value produced at one rate and consumed at another, and a gain is one of those.

A counter that fires on the healthy case is not a counter. The dropout proxy said 28 of 58 on a perfectly healthy run, which is the most dangerous kind of wrong: it would have been "working" forever, and it would have had nothing new to say on the day a real dropout happened.

Real-time code you cannot call from a test is undebuggable. Making mix_into a pure function with a public entry point cost one extra method and bought every measurement in this lesson.

Module 7 is finished. Eight lessons that began with three gimbal rings locking and end with a listener walking past a siren. The thread running through them is that orientation and motion are data: Euler angles could not compose, axis-angle could not either, the plane showed why a rotation carries a half-angle, quaternions made composition one multiplication, slerp made interpolation a schedule, skinning made a pose deform a surface, clips made motion a function of time — and this lesson used the same transform that carries all of it to decide what a thing sounds like.
