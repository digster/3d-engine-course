Module 8 — Physics · Lesson 8.3

Angular Dynamics: Torque and the Inertia Tensor

Eighty-five lessons in, nothing in this engine can tumble. 8.2 gave a body a mass, which answers one question — how hard is it to push? — and this lesson answers the other one, which turns out to need nine numbers rather than one. Spin a book about its three axes and you get three different resistances; spin it about an axis between two of them and the angular momentum does not point along the spin axis at all. A thing that eats a vector and hands back one pointing somewhere else is a matrix, and this one is the inertia tensor. We derive it in four lines from r × (ω × r) and a triple product — and then discover that the expression the derivation produces returns exactly zero for a plank, because |r|² − x² is a sum of three squares with one subtracted straight off again and a float at 10⁶ cannot hold a 10⁻⁶. The cross-product-matrix form is the same algebra and never forms the sum. Then the parallel-axis theorem, checked by a test that only passes if the theorem is exactly true; the basis change R I Rᵀ, and the plausible wrong one that has the same trace, the same determinant, the same symmetry and the same principal moments; and the integration of an orientation, which is not an addition — q += ω·h is not merely inaccurate, it is not a rotation, and the update that replaces it inflates a unit quaternion by 0.35% every step and still turns 0.23% short after you renormalise. Then the payoff, which is one of the loveliest results in classical mechanics and is sitting in the engine as soon as the tensor is. A torque-free body conserves L and does not conserve ω: our 1 kg box swings its angular speed over 10.85% with nothing acting on it, between two bounds we derive by hand and then measure to four digits. Spin it about its middle axis and it flips end over end, forever, at a rate Euler's equations predict — 4.8038 s⁻¹ against a measured 4.8084. And the term that makes all of it happen is the one almost every engine drops, because integrating it explicitly gains 162% of a body's angular momentum in a minute and diverges by 3.3 × 10¹¹ at 30 Hz. There are four answers to that, the last of which is the interesting one: the term is not physics at all, it is the price of choosing ω as the state variable, and storing the momentum instead makes it vanish from the equations. That gets the drift to 8.0 × 10⁻⁴ — and produces the one table in this course where a smaller step size is a worse answer.

1. The problem: an engine in which nothing can tumble

Lesson 8.2 ended with a body that has a mass, a force accumulator and a position, and a body_world that advances all of them. Drop a crate and it falls correctly. Push it with a thruster and it accelerates by F/m. Throw it and it traces the parabola Newton says it should.

And whatever you do to it, it arrives facing exactly the way it left.

That is not a missing feature in the sense that anti-aliasing was a missing feature. It is a category of behaviour that the engine currently cannot express at all, and the gap shows up immediately in three places a player would notice:

  • Nothing can be pushed off-centre. add_force takes a vector and nothing else, so every force in the engine acts at the body's centre of mass by construction. A crate shoved at a corner slides away squarely. An explosion beside a barrel launches it without spinning it. There is no way to say where a push happened, which means there is no way for a push to do anything but translate.
  • Nothing has an orientation the physics knows about. Module 7's transform carries a quat, and an animation or a gameplay script can set it. But body_world::step has never heard of it, so a body's facing is something imposed from outside rather than something the simulation computes — which means the moment physics and gameplay both want to control it, one of them silently loses.
  • Nothing can resist being turned. Which is the deep one, and the reason this lesson is long. Resistance to being pushed is a single number, and we have had it since 8.2. Resistance to being turned is not a single number, not even three, and the object it actually is has consequences that reach as far as Lesson 8.11's ragdolls.

Everything else in Module 8 needs this. A contact between two boxes happens at a point on a surface, and the whole difference between a box that slides and a box that topples is whether that point is inside or outside the base — which is a torque question. Lesson 8.9's collision response computes an impulse at a contact point, and applying it without the rotational half gives you objects that bounce off each other without ever starting to spin. Lesson 8.11's joints are constraints on relative angular velocity. None of that can be written until a body can turn.

The linear half above is untouched by everything in this lesson, and that is a fact about physics rather than about how carefully the edit was made. Linear momentum and angular momentum do not mix: the centre of mass of a body moves exactly as F = ma says however wildly the body is spinning. A thrown hammer's centre traces a clean parabola while the hammer tumbles around it. Every number 8.2 measured is still the number it measured.

2. Building intuition: the skater, the book and the wrench

Before any formalism, three things you have watched happen, each of which is a different piece of what we are about to build.

2.1 The skater: where the mass is, not how much there is

A figure skater spins with their arms out, pulls them in, and speeds up. Dramatically — a factor of two or three is routine.

Nothing about their mass changed. They did not become lighter, and they are not pushing against anything: the ice is nearly frictionless and there is nothing for them to push against anyway. What changed is where the mass is. Arms out, a good fraction of their body is a metre from the spin axis; arms in, it is 15 centimetres.

So resistance to turning depends on the distribution of mass, not just the total — which already tells you it cannot be one number stored next to inv_mass. And it gives you the shape of the dependence: further out is harder. How much harder is the first thing we will derive, and the answer is that it goes as the square of the distance, which is why the skater's effect is so much larger than people expect.

2.2 The book: three different answers, and one that is unstable

Find a book — a hardback, or this laptop, or a TV remote. Something rectangular with three clearly different dimensions. Now toss it in the air with a spin about each of its three axes in turn, and catch it.

  • Spun about its longest axis (the one through the spine, end to end), it spins cleanly and comes back the way it went up.
  • Spun about the axis perpendicular to the cover, same — clean, stable, boring.
  • Spun about the third axis — the one across the cover, through the middle — it flips over. Every time. You cannot stop it doing this by throwing more carefully.

That is not a trick and you are not doing it wrong. It is a real and completely deterministic consequence of a rigid body having three different resistances to turning, and it is the single best evidence that resistance-to-turning is not one number. If it were, all three throws would look the same. Section 10 derives the flip, predicts its rate from the three numbers, and measures the prediction; the spin demo puts it on screen with a key that turns it off.

2.3 The wrench: the same force, three different amounts of turning

Undo a tight bolt with a short spanner and you cannot. Put a length of pipe over the handle and you can. Your arm did not get stronger; the force is the same. What changed is where you applied it.

And there is a second half people notice less: push the spanner along its own length, straight at the bolt, and nothing turns at all, however hard you push. Push perpendicular to it and you get everything the force has to give. Push at 30° and you get some fraction in between.

Distance from the axis, and the component of the force perpendicular to the lever arm. Two vectors, and a quantity that vanishes when they are parallel, is proportional to both magnitudes, and is maximal when they are at right angles. If that pattern sounds familiar it should: Lesson 3.4 built exactly that object out of the cross product, for a completely different reason. It is the same object, and it is called torque.

3. Torque is r × F, and it is about a point

Take a force F applied at a point that sits at r from some reference point. The turning effect about that reference point is

\[ \boldsymbol{\tau} = \mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{F} \]

— torque equals the lever arm crossed with the force. Its magnitude is |r| |F| sin θ, which is "distance times the perpendicular part of the force", and its direction is the axis you are being turned about, by the right-hand rule.

Every property of the wrench is already in that one line. Push along the lever arm and r and F are parallel, so sin θ = 0 and the torque is exactly zero — not small, zero, because the cross product of parallel vectors is the zero vector by construction. Double the length of the spanner and you double |r|. Push perpendicular and sin θ = 1.

3.1 A worked example, with real numbers

A one-kilogram box, half a metre across in x and z and two metres tall, sitting at the origin. Push the top face horizontally: F = (10, 0, 0) newtons, applied at (0, 1, 0) — one metre above the centre of mass.

\[ \mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{F} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} \times \begin{pmatrix} 10 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1\cdot 0 - 0\cdot 0 \\ 0\cdot 10 - 0\cdot 0 \\ 0\cdot 0 - 1\cdot 10 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ -10 \end{pmatrix} \]

Ten newton-metres about −z. Check the sign against the Conventions page rather than against intuition: this course is right-handed, so a positive rotation about +z carries +x toward +y. A rotation about −z therefore carries +y toward +x — which is the top of the box moving in the direction we pushed it. The box tips over toward +x. That is what should happen, and the sign says so.

The engine agrees, and the harness checks that add_force_at produces the cross product rather than something that merely resembles it:

3.2 The point it is about, and why we choose the centre of mass

A torque is not a property of a force. It is a property of a force and a point, and the same force has a different torque about every point in space. "The torque on this box" is not a thing; "the torque on this box about its centre of mass" is.

So which point? The engine could pick any. It picks the centre of mass, and the reason is worth dwelling on because it is what makes the rest of the module tractable.

About the centre of mass — and only about the centre of mass — the translational and rotational halves of the motion decouple completely. F = ma governs where the centre goes, τ = ... governs how the body turns about it, and neither equation contains a term from the other. Choose any other reference point and they become coupled: a force through the centre of mass would produce a torque about your chosen point, so pushing a crate squarely in the middle would set it spinning, and the two halves of step() would have to be solved together.

There is a lovely piece of evidence for this that needs no algebra. Throw a hammer. Its centre of mass traces a clean parabola while the hammer tumbles wildly around it — the tumbling does not perturb the parabola at all, and the parabola does not steady the tumbling. That independence is the decoupling, and it is exactly why the code in body_world::step can advance the linear half and the angular half one after the other without either knowing about the other.

3.3 What falls out for free

Two consequences arrive with no extra code, and both matter.

A force through the centre of mass produces no torque. Its lever arm is the zero vector, and every term of the cross product has a factor of it, so the result is exactly zero in floating point too — the harness checks the bit pattern, not a tolerance. This is why gravity does not make things tumble: it acts at the centre of mass, so a uniform body falls without turning, which is why 8.2's whole module worked without ever mentioning rotation.

Two equal and opposite forces at different points produce torque and no force at all. That arrangement is called a couple, and it is the physical thing a "pure torque" models: a reaction wheel, an electric motor mounted on the body, a character twisting to right themselves in mid-air. The harness's A.4 applies +5 N at +y and −5 N at −y and reports a net force of exactly zero and a net torque of −10 N·m. That is why add_torque exists as an entry point beside add_force_at rather than being a special case of it.

And so the API writes itself. add_force_at takes a force and a world-space point, adds the force to one accumulator and r × F to the other, and the two special cases above are not special-cased anywhere — they fall out of the arithmetic.

4. The inertia tensor, derived — and the form that loses it

Now the other half. We know how hard we are turning something; we need to know how hard it is to turn.

The linear case took one line: p = m v, momentum is mass times velocity, and F = dp/dt. We want the rotational analogue, and the honest way to get it is to build it out of the linear one — because a rigid body is nothing but a very large number of point masses that happen to keep their distances from each other.

4.1 Four lines

Line one. Angular momentum of a point mass about the origin is defined as ℓ = r × p = m (r × v). It is a definition rather than a discovery, and the reason it is the useful definition is that its time derivative turns out to be the torque — differentiate it and the v × v term vanishes, leaving dℓ/dt = r × F = τ. So angular momentum is to torque what momentum is to force, which is what makes it worth tracking. For a whole body, sum over its parts:

\[ \mathbf{L} = \sum_i m_i\,(\mathbf{r}_i \times \mathbf{v}_i) \]

Line two. The body is rigid, and that is the entire content of the word: every point in it is at a fixed distance from every other, so the only motion available to it (once you have subtracted the motion of the centre of mass) is a rotation. And a point at r from the centre of a body rotating at angular velocity ω moves at

\[ \mathbf{v}_i = \boldsymbol{\omega} \times \mathbf{r}_i \]

— which is worth reading rather than accepting. The point travels in a circle about the ω axis; its speed is the rate |ω| times the radius of that circle, which is |r| sin θ and not |r|; and its direction is perpendicular to both the axis and the lever arm. Magnitude |ω||r| sin θ, perpendicular to both: that is the cross product, again, arriving for the third time in this lesson from a completely different question.

Line three. Substitute, and look at what one term now is:

\[ \mathbf{L} = \sum_i m_i\,\bigl(\mathbf{r}_i \times (\boldsymbol{\omega} \times \mathbf{r}_i)\bigr) \]

That is a vector triple product, and there is an identity for it — the one usually remembered as BAC minus CAB: a × (b × c) = b (a·c) − c (a·b). With a = r, b = ω, c = r:

\[ \mathbf{r} \times (\boldsymbol{\omega} \times \mathbf{r}) = \boldsymbol{\omega}\,(\mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{r}) - \mathbf{r}\,(\mathbf{r}\cdot\boldsymbol{\omega}) = |\mathbf{r}|^2\,\boldsymbol{\omega} - \mathbf{r}\,(\mathbf{r}\cdot\boldsymbol{\omega}) \]

Line four, and this is the step that matters. Both terms on the right are linear in ω. The first is |r|² times the identity applied to ω. The second — r (r·ω) — takes ω, measures how much of it lies along r, and hands back that much of r. That is a matrix acting on ω, and it is called the outer product r ⊗ r. So:

\[ \mathbf{L} = \underbrace{\left[\sum_i m_i\bigl(|\mathbf{r}_i|^2\,\mathbf{1} - \mathbf{r}_i \otimes \mathbf{r}_i\bigr)\right]}_{\textstyle \mathbf{I}}\;\boldsymbol{\omega} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \mathbf{L} = \mathbf{I}\,\boldsymbol{\omega} \]

Angular momentum equals the inertia tensor times the angular velocity. It is p = m v with a matrix where the scalar was — and the matrix is there because the cross products in lines one and two did not cancel into a scalar, which is not something anyone chose.

4.2 Reading the expression rather than the formula

|r|² · 1 − r ⊗ r looks like arithmetic and is geometry. Take it in two pieces.

|r|² · 1 says: resist every axis equally, by m r². That is the skater's square, and it is where the whole square-law intuition lives.

− r ⊗ r says: …then take all of that back along r itself. The outer product is the projection onto r, scaled by |r|², so subtracting it annihilates the r direction exactly and leaves everything perpendicular untouched.

Together: a point mass resists rotation about any axis perpendicular to it by m r², and rotation about an axis running through it by nothing at all — which is exactly right, because a point spun about an axis through itself does not move. A formula that produced anything else would be wrong.

Here is that in the harness, on a 3 kg mass sitting two metres up the y axis:

       worst relative difference  1.2075e-06

  B.4  3 kg at    |  12.000000   0.000000   0.000000 |
                  |   0.000000   0.000000   0.000000 |
       (0, 2, 0)  |   0.000000   0.000000  12.000000 |

       spun about y (through it):  I = 0.000000
       spun about x (across it) :  I = 12.000000  = m r^2

m r² = 3 × 4 = 12, in the two directions that are not y, and an exact zero in the direction that is. B.1 is the derivation itself under test: 20,000 random (r, ω) pairs pushed through the cross products directly and through the matrix, and the two agree to a part in a million, which is `float`'s noise floor.

B.5 is the control, and it is a control of the second kind — the one that asks what the instrument would say if everything were fine. Every tensor this expression can build is symmetric, because 1 is and r ⊗ r is and sums of symmetric matrices are. So asymmetry() on any of them is not a tolerance to tune; it is a bug detector whose correct reading is exactly zero, and it reads exactly zero across 20,000 tensors. Every subsequent section prints it beside whatever it built.

4.3 Why mat3 only now grows arithmetic

The outer product does not exist in this engine yet, and neither does adding two mat3s, or multiplying one by a scalar. Eighty lessons of graphics code and nobody ever wanted them. That is not an oversight, and the reason is the most interesting thing in mat3.hpp.

Since Lesson 2.5 a mat3 has been exactly one thing: where do the basis vectors land. Under that reading the operators above are meaningless. The sum of two rotations is not a rotation. Half a shear is not half as sheared in any useful sense. Nothing in the renderer ever wanted either, which is why neither was ever written.

An inertia tensor is a mat3 under a different reading. It is not a map from vectors to vectors that happens to be useful; it is a quantity — the way a body's mass is spread out — which turns out to need nine numbers, and which is used as a map only because that is what its nine numbers mean. Quantities add. Two lumps of matter bolted together have the sum of their tensors about a common point, for exactly the same reason they have the sum of their masses, and §5 builds a compound body by doing precisely that.

So the arithmetic arrives eighty lessons late because until today there was nothing in this engine that a matrix could be the amount of.

4.4 …and the form the derivation produced is the one you must not ship

Here is where this lesson stopped going to plan.

There is a second way to write the same thing. Lesson 3.4's cross product can be written as a matrix — the cross-product matrix [w]ₓ, whose action is v ↦ w × v — and if you expand −[r]ₓ[r]ₓ by the same triple-product identity we used in line three, you get |r|² 1 − r ⊗ r back. Same algebra, different operators. So they must agree.

Algebraically, yes. In float, the harness says no: 

  B.2  -[r]x [r]x  vs  |r|^2 I - r(x)r
       algebraically identical; bit-identical in float
       on all 20,000: no

Disagreeing in the last bit would be unremarkable — two expressions evaluated in a different order round differently, and that is every day of floating-point arithmetic. But look at which bits, on a body shaped like the things games are full of: 

  B.3  the same two forms on a LONG THIN body,
       a sample at r = (1000, 0.001, 0):

       |r|^2 I - r(x)r     Ixx = 0.000000e+00
       inertia_of_point    Ixx = 1.000000e-06
       exact                     1.000000e-06
       relative error of the derived form: 100.0%

Not the last bit. All of them.

The cause is visible once you write out what the diagonal of the derived form actually computes. The xx entry is |r|² − x², which for r = (1000, 0.001, 0) is

\[ (1000^2 + 0.001^2 + 0^2) - 1000^2 = 1{,}000{,}000.000001 - 1{,}000{,}000 \]

A float has 24 bits of significand. At a magnitude of 10⁶ the gap between representable values is 2⁻²³ × 2²⁰ ≈ 0.0625, so the 0.000001 is not rounded away at the end — it was never in the sum. The addition produced exactly 1,000,000, and subtracting 1,000,000 from it gives exactly zero. This is catastrophic cancellation in the textbook sense: a small true answer computed as the difference of two large nearly-equal numbers.

The cross-product-matrix form never forms that sum. Its xx entry is y² + z² directly — the two terms that actually belong in it — and it returns 1e−06 exactly.

This is not a contrived input. It is a plank, a rail, a sword, a lamp post, a fence panel — anything far longer than it is thick, which describes a great deal of what a level is built out of. And the consequence is not a small numerical error, it is a behavioural one: a zero principal moment makes the tensor singular, inverse_inertia refuses it, and the body silently will not rotate about its own length no matter what torque you apply. Which is a bug you would chase for a day, because everything else about the body works.

So inertia_of_point ships the well-conditioned form — the diagonal built from the two terms that belong in it, the off-diagonal from the products that belong in that — and the derivation's version appears nowhere in the engine. It is also faster: nine multiplies against two matrix products. That is the rare case where the numerically better choice costs nothing, and the only reason to have written the other one first is that it is the one the derivation hands you.

The transferable version of this: a derivation tells you what to compute, not how to compute it, and the two questions have different answers more often than undergraduate courses suggest. Lesson 6.16 found the same thing in perspective(), where forming A + 1 with A = −1.003009 discarded eight bits and amplified one ulp by 332×. Whenever a formula subtracts two things that are nearly equal, look for the version that does not.

4.5 Six shapes, and why they are all diagonal

Equation (1) is a sum over point masses, and a real shape is an integral of it. Those integrals were done centuries ago and inertia.hpp carries six of them: a solid box, a solid sphere, a thin spherical shell, a solid cylinder, a thin rod, and a capsule. The box, which is the one you will use most, is

\[ I_{xx} = \tfrac{m}{3}\,(b^2 + c^2), \qquad I_{yy} = \tfrac{m}{3}\,(a^2 + c^2), \qquad I_{zz} = \tfrac{m}{3}\,(a^2 + b^2) \]

for half-extents (a, b, c). Note what is not in the x entry: a, the extent along x. Resistance to spinning about an axis depends on how far the mass is from that axis, and mass lying along x is at zero distance from the x axis. Each entry is built from the other two. Getting this backwards is the single most common inertia bug there is, and it produces a body that looks almost right, which is worse than one that looks wrong.

All six are diagonal, and that is neither coincidence nor approximation. Each of these shapes is symmetric about all three coordinate planes, so for every point mass at (x, y, z) there is a mirror one at (−x, y, z) whose −m x y off-diagonal term cancels the first one's exactly. Body axes chosen along a shape's symmetries are its principal axes, and a tensor written in its principal axes is diagonal. This is why engines let you author a box rather than a nine-float matrix.

The capsule is the one whose formula you cannot check by inspection, and §5's parallel-axis theorem is what it is built from. Its two limits are the only practical test: 

  C.4  the capsule's two limits
       H -> 0   capsule 0.76800001  sphere 0.76800007
                max element difference 5.9605e-08
       R -> 0   capsule 1.00013328  rod    1.00000000
                relative difference    1.3328e-04

Shrink the cylindrical section to nothing and a capsule is a sphere; shrink the radius and it is a rod. Both limits land on the independently-derived closed form. A formula that passes two limits it was not built from is a formula you can trust.

5. Moving a tensor: the parallel-axis theorem

A tensor is about a point. Every closed form above is about the shape's own centre of mass, which is the only point that makes sense as a default — and the moment you want to assemble two of them into one body, or spin something about a hinge that is not its centre, you need to move one.

The theorem is three lines from equation (1). Suppose you know I about the centre of mass and want it about a point offset by d. Every sample's position becomes r − d, so

\[ \mathbf{I}_{\mathbf{d}} = \sum_i m_i\Bigl(|\mathbf{r}_i - \mathbf{d}|^2\,\mathbf{1} - (\mathbf{r}_i - \mathbf{d}) \otimes (\mathbf{r}_i - \mathbf{d})\Bigr) \]

Expand, and three groups of terms fall out. The ones with no d are I itself. The ones with d twice sum to M(|d|² 1 − d ⊗ d), since Σ mᵢ = M. And the cross terms carry a factor of Σ mᵢ rᵢ — which is the definition of the centre of mass, times the total mass, and the centre of mass is where we measured r from, so it is zero.

\[ \mathbf{I}_{\mathbf{d}} = \mathbf{I}_{\text{cm}} + M\bigl(|\mathbf{d}|^2\,\mathbf{1} - \mathbf{d} \otimes \mathbf{d}\bigr) \]

— and the correction term is exactly inertia_of_point(M, d), the tensor of a single point mass M sitting at the offset. That is the theorem's real content: moving the reference point costs exactly as much as if the whole body were concentrated at its centre of mass.

And the vanishing cross term is why it only works from the centre of mass. Shifting from an arbitrary point to another arbitrary point by calling it twice is wrong, because the first call's reference is not where the mass balances.

5.1 A test that only passes if the theorem is exactly true

Every closed form in inertia.hpp is a transcription, which means every one of them could be a typo. The honest check is to compute the same tensor by brute force — fill the shape with point masses and sum equation (1) — and see whether the two agree.

Do that naively and something odd happens: 

  C.2  the same box as a grid of point masses

      N    points     Ixx(points)   ratio   1 - 1/N^2
      4        64      4.062500   0.937500  0.937500
      8       512      4.265625   0.984375  0.984375
     20      8000      4.322488   0.997497  0.997500
     50    125000      4.331634   0.999608  0.999600

The point sum is low, and not by a random amount: by exactly 1/N², at every resolution tried, to six decimal places.

That is predictable before it is measured, and predicting it is the point. A grid of N³ point masses replaces each little cell of the box with a point at its centre — which has the cell's mass but none of the cell's own spread. The missing piece per cell is that cell's own tensor, and for a cell of half-size b/N by c/N it is m_cell(b² + c²)/(3N²). Summed over all the cells that is M(b² + c²)/(3N²) — which is I_xx / N². The whole deficit is a factor of 1 − 1/N², exactly, which is what the last two columns say.

Now add it back. Each cell is a solid box, so its tensor about the assembly's origin is its own tensor shifted by the parallel-axis theorem. Sum those instead: 

      N      Ixx(cells)    relative error
      4      4.333332   3.3012e-07
      8      4.333342   1.8707e-06
     20      4.333335   3.3012e-07

Exact at N = 4, which is sixty-four samples. And it can only be exact at sixty-four samples if the parallel-axis theorem is exactly true rather than a good approximation — which is the thing this section is actually claiming. The closed form is 4.333333; the check agrees to float's noise floor with a laughably coarse grid.

5.2 Compound bodies, and a wrong answer that passes every test

Real bodies are assemblies. inertia_assembly takes a list of parts, each with its own mass, its own tensor about its own centre, and where it sits, and does the three steps the theorem implies: total the masses and take the mass-weighted mean of the centres; shift each part's tensor out to that common centre; add.

Here is a dumbbell — two 2 kg balls on a 0.5 kg bar, the balls 1.5 m either side of centre: 

  D.3  a dumbbell: 2 x 2 kg balls, a 0.5 kg bar
       total mass   4.5000 kg
       centre       (0.0000, 0.0000, 0.0000)
                     |   9.631312   0.000000   0.000000 |
              I      |   0.000000   0.256625   0.000000 |
                     |   0.000000   0.000000   9.631312 |
       Ixx / Iyy = 37.5307  (hard across, easy along)

Thirty-seven times harder to turn across the bar than along it, which is exactly the experience of holding one. Almost all of the 9.63 comes from the parallel-axis term: 2 × 2 kg × 1.5² = 9.0, against 0.256 for everything else. The balls' own tensors contribute 2 × (2/5)(2)(0.4²) = 0.256, and the bar the rest.

Now the mistake. Add the parts' tensors without shifting them: 

  D.4  the same parts added WITHOUT shifting:
       Ixx 0.631312 against 9.631312 — 15.3x too small,
       and it still passes every test in inspect_inertia:
       asym 0.00e+00  positive 1  triangle 1  usable 1

Fifteen times too small, and the engine accepts it without a murmur. It is symmetric. It is positive-definite. It satisfies the triangle inequality. Every physical test we have passes, because the wrong answer is a perfectly good inertia tensor — it is the tensor of a body whose parts are all piled on top of each other at the balance point, which is a thing that could exist. It is just not this thing.

A validity check tells you a tensor could exist. It cannot tell you it is the tensor of the object you meant. That distinction is worth carrying: it is the same one that makes 8.7's contact manifolds hard, where a perfectly well-formed contact can be between the wrong pair of features. Validation catches the impossible. It does not catch the merely wrong, and the merely wrong is the common case.

The controls for this section are both of the "what if everything is fine" kind: shifting by the zero vector must return the tensor bit-identically, and an assembly of one part must return that part untouched. Both do. A theorem implementation that changes something when asked to change nothing is a theorem implementation with a typo in it.

6. Carrying a tensor: R I Rᵀ

Every tensor so far is expressed in body axes — a frame glued to the body, that turns with it. That is the only frame in which it is constant: a box's Ixx is m(b² + c²)/3 forever, whatever the box is doing. In world space the same tensor changes the instant the body moves, because the mass that was far from the world x axis a moment ago is now somewhere else.

So the tensor lives in body space and everything else in this engine — position, velocity, force, torque, angular velocity — lives in world space. That asymmetry is forced rather than chosen, and the bridge between the two is one line of code that is worth understanding rather than memorising.

6.1 Deriving the sandwich

Let R be the body's orientation as a rotation matrix — the map that carries body axes to world axes. We know L_body = I_body ω_body, and we want the world-space version. Three substitutions:

\[ \boldsymbol{\omega}_{\text{body}} = \mathbf{R}^{\!\top}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\text{world}}, \qquad \mathbf{L}_{\text{world}} = \mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{L}_{\text{body}} \]
\[ \mathbf{L}_{\text{world}} = \mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{I}_{\text{body}}\,\mathbf{R}^{\!\top}\,\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\text{world}} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \mathbf{I}_{\text{world}} = \mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{I}_{\text{body}}\,\mathbf{R}^{\!\top} \]

Read it right to left, which is the order the matrices apply in. Rᵀ carries the angular velocity into the frame the tensor is written in; I acts there, where it is constant and where its meaning was defined; and R carries the result back out. A quantity that eats a vector and produces a vector has to be converted on both sides, which is the whole reason a tensor transforms with R … Rᵀ where a plain vector transforms with R alone.

Two things follow immediately and both get used.

The inverse sandwiches too. (R I Rᵀ)⁻¹ = R⁻ᵀ I⁻¹ R⁻¹, and for a rotation R⁻¹ = Rᵀ, so this is just R I⁻¹ Rᵀ. Which means a body can invert its tensor once, when it is created, and never invert again — every step just sandwiches the stored inverse. The harness checks the two routes against each other and gets 5.9605e−08, which is one ulp at that magnitude.

A zero tensor survives the sandwich unchanged. Which is 8.2's inverse-mass argument arriving again, nine floats wide: a body that must not rotate stores all zeros, and R · 0 · Rᵀ = 0, so every torque applied to a wall produces exactly no angular acceleration with no branch and no sentinel anywhere in the step.

6.2 The plausible wrong one

Now the part that costs people a day. Write the sandwich the other way round — Rᵀ I R — and ask what goes wrong. 

  E.2  R^T I R instead of R I R^T:
       trace           9.333333  (same)
       determinant    24.074076  (same)
       asymmetry     1.1921e-07  (still symmetric)
       max element difference from R I R^T  1.569413

Every cheap test passes. It is symmetric, so asymmetry() is clean. It has the same trace, the same determinant, and the same principal moments. It satisfies the triangle inequality. inspect_inertia calls it usable, and it is: it is the inertia tensor of a real body, because Rᵀ I R is the basis change in the other direction and describes the same body rotated the opposite way.

It is just not this body, and the tensor is out by 1.57 in its largest element on a body whose moments run from 1.67 to 4.33. That is not a rounding difference, it is a different object — and nothing structural will tell you.

The test that does is behavioural rather than structural: put the body in a known orientation and ask what the world tensor does. And there is a trap even here, which the harness deliberately walks into and reports: 

  E.3  a 90-degree turn about z. The box's x and y axes
       swap, so world Ixx must equal body Iyy.
       body    Ixx 4.333333  Iyy 3.333333
       R I R^T Ixx 3.333333  <- matches body Iyy
       R^T I R Ixx 3.333333
       For THIS rotation the two happen to agree, because
       a 90-degree turn is its own inverse up to a sign on
       a diagonal tensor.

A test built on a right angle passes with the transpose in either place. Which is why E.2's rotation is a deliberately awkward one — 0.7π about (1, 2, 3) normalised — and why "test it with a 90° rotation" is advice that will let this bug through. Convention bugs hide behind symmetric test data. Module 4's conventions page made the same point about winding order; here it is again, in a different subsystem, for the same reason.

And the section's last measurement is 8.2 §8's frame question in its rotational form. Put a scale in the sandwich instead of a rotation: 

  E.5  CONTROL identity rotation: identical  yes
       a SCALE of (2,1,1) in the sandwich:
         trace 9.333334 -> 22.333334, and the body it
         describes is a different body.

The trace is invariant under rotations and not under scales, so it moves — and what comes out is not the tensor of the body you started with. This is the deeper reason 8.2 insisted that bodies live in world space: a non-uniformly scaled parent does not merely tilt a body's gravity, it turns a sphere into an ellipsoid and an inertia tensor into something that is not one.

7. Integrating an orientation is not an addition

We can now compute an angular acceleration: α = I_world⁻¹ τ, which is a = F/m with a matrix in place of the scalar. Multiply by h, add to ω, done — that half is genuinely the same as the linear case.

The other half is not. A position advances by x += v h because positions live in a vector space and adding a displacement to one is the definition of moving it. An orientation does not live in a vector space, and that single sentence is the whole of this section.

7.1 Why the obvious thing is not merely inaccurate

Angular velocity is a vector — a direction (the axis) and a magnitude (radians per second) — so it looks as though a rotation ought to advance by ω h the way a position advances by v h. Here is why it cannot.

Take two rotations of 90°, one about x and one about y, and apply them in both orders to the probe vector (0, 0, 1): 

  F.1  90 deg about x, then y — and the other order

       (0,0,1) -> x then y:  1.000  0.000  0.000
       (0,0,1) -> y then x:  0.000 -1.000  0.000
       angle between the two results: 120.000 deg

A hundred and twenty degrees apart. Now: the two "rotation vectors" you would be summing are (π/2, 0, 0) and (0, π/2, 0), and their sum is (π/2, π/2, 0) either way round, because addition commutes.

So no vector can be the running total of a sequence of rotations. There is nothing to fix with a smaller step size: the quantity you would be accumulating is order-blind and rotations are not. q += ω h is not an approximation to a rotation. It is not a rotation.

7.2 What is true: the quaternion derivative

The quaternion itself does have a derivative, and deriving it needs nothing beyond Lesson 7.4.

Over a small time h, a body turning at ω rotates by an angle |ω| h about the axis ω̂. By 7.4, the quaternion for that is

\[ \Delta q = \left(\cos\tfrac{|\boldsymbol{\omega}| h}{2},\; \hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\sin\tfrac{|\boldsymbol{\omega}| h}{2}\right) \;\approx\;\left(1,\; \tfrac{h}{2}\boldsymbol{\omega}\right) \quad\text{for small } h \]

— using cos x ≈ 1 and sin x ≈ x, so that ω̂ sin(|ω|h/2) ≈ ω̂ |ω| h/2 = ω h/2. The ½ is 7.3's half-angle, which has been in every quaternion in this course since complex numbers, arriving here as a factor in a derivative.

Composing that with the current orientation and taking the limit:

\[ q(t + h) = \Delta q \cdot q(t) \approx q + \tfrac{h}{2}\,\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\text{pure}}\,q \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \dot q = \tfrac{1}{2}\,\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\text{pure}}\, q \]

where ω_pure is the quaternion (0, ω). Note the side: the delta multiplies from the left, because 7.4 fixed the convention that a·b applies b first, so a delta expressed in world axes is the outer one. A body-space angular velocity would multiply from the right. Same arithmetic, other side, and §16 has the pitfall.

7.3 Two failure modes, both predictable before they are measured

Take that derivative literally — q += ½ ω_pure q h — and two things go wrong. Both are computable in advance, which is what makes them worth measuring.

First, the length inflates. The step ½ ω_pure q h is orthogonal to q in 4-D — a fact worth checking once rather than believing, and the note in spin_inflation does the four lines. So adding it is a right-angled triangle, and Pythagoras gives the new length directly:

\[ |q_{\text{new}}| = \sqrt{1 + \left(\tfrac{|\boldsymbol{\omega}| h}{2}\right)^{2}} \;\approx\; 1 + \frac{(|\boldsymbol{\omega}| h)^2}{8} \] 
  F.2  the linearised update inflates. |q| after one
       step, before renormalising:

      omega   omega*h    measured      predicted
       1.0   0.0167   1.000034690   1.000034690
      10.0   0.1667   1.003466249   1.003466249
      30.0   0.5000   1.030776381   1.030776381

       Compounded without renormalising, 10 rad/s for
       one second (60 steps) gives |q| = 1.2307

At 10 rad/s and 60 Hz that is 0.35% per step, compounding to 23% in one second. A non-unit quaternion is not a rotation — mat3_from_quat on one produces a matrix with a scale in it — so the body visibly shears and then collapses. Renormalising every step is not defensive programming here; it is load-bearing.

Second, and this one survives the renormalisation. After you divide by the length, the result is the unit quaternion whose imaginary part points along ω with tan(θ/2) = |ω|h/2 — so the angle it actually turned through is 2 arctan(|ω|h/2), not |ω|h. Expanding the arctangent:

\[ 2\arctan\!\left(\tfrac{x}{2}\right) = x - \frac{x^3}{12} + O(x^5) \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \frac{\theta_{\text{achieved}}}{\theta_{\text{asked}}} - 1 \approx -\frac{(|\boldsymbol{\omega}| h)^2}{12} \] 
  F.3  ...and what renormalising does NOT fix: the angle.

      omega*h    asked      achieved    rel. err   -(wh)^2/12
     0.0167   0.016667   0.016666  -2.313e-05  -2.315e-05
     0.1667   0.166667   0.166282  -2.305e-03  -2.315e-03
     0.5000   0.500000   0.489957  -2.009e-02  -2.083e-02

  F.4  the exponential map, 3,600 steps at 10 rad/s
       linearised   |q| 0.9999999   error   79.2459 deg
       exponential  |q| 1.0000000   error    0.0019 deg

The update always turns short. It is a lag, not a random walk, so it accumulates linearly: 2.305e−03 per radian, times 600 radians of turning in a minute, is 1.383 radians — 79.2°, which is exactly what F.4 measures. Predicted before it was run.

The alternative has neither problem. Build the delta rotation exactly, with the real sin and cos, and compose it: quat_from_axis_angle(ω̂, |ω|h) · q. This is the exponential map, and for a constant ω it is not an approximation at all — it is the closed-form answer, and it needs no renormalisation because a unit quaternion times a unit quaternion is a unit quaternion. 0.0019° after a minute, against 79.2459°.

It costs a sqrt and a sin/cos pair per body per step. Whether that is worth paying is §12's measurement, not an opinion — and the honest answer turns out to be "it depends on what you are simulating", which is why body_world has a switch rather than a decision.

8. Angular momentum, and what is actually conserved

We have L = I ω and we have a way to advance ω and q. Before wiring it together, one question that decides how the rest of the module is written: when nothing is acting on a body, what stays the same?

In the linear case the answer is so obvious it is barely worth asking. Nothing acting means F = 0 means a = 0 means the velocity is constant. Newton's first law, and both v and p = mv are conserved because the mass is a constant scalar that never moves.

Rotationally, the two come apart, and they come apart for a reason that is visible in the algebra. τ = 0 gives dL/dt = 0, so L is conserved. But L = I_world ω, and I_world = R I_body Rᵀ changes as the body turns. If L is fixed and I_world is moving, then ω cannot be fixed.

This is the single most important sentence in the lesson. A body with nothing acting on it does not keep spinning about the same axis at the same rate. Its angular momentum is constant; its angular velocity wanders, changes magnitude, and — for a body with three different moments — can reverse entirely. It looks exactly like something is acting on it, and nothing is.

8.1 How far can it wander? Derive it.

This is measurable, and better than measurable: derivable, which means the simulation can be checked against a number that owes it nothing.

A torque-free rigid body conserves exactly two things: |L|², and twice its rotational kinetic energy, which in body axes is 2T = Σ Lᵢ²/Iᵢ. Write uᵢ = Lᵢ² and look at what those two constraints are:

\[ u_x + u_y + u_z = |\mathbf{L}|^2, \qquad \frac{u_x}{I_x} + \frac{u_y}{I_y} + \frac{u_z}{I_z} = 2T \]

Both are linear in u. Two linear constraints in three unknowns leave a line, and intersecting it with the positive octant leaves a segment. And the quantity we want — |ω|² = Σ (Lᵢ/Iᵢ)² = Σ uᵢ/Iᵢ² — is linear on that segment too.

A linear function on a segment takes its extreme values at the ends. The ends are where one uᵢ reaches zero. So there are only three candidate answers, at most two of them feasible, and finding them is solving a 2×2 system three times.

For our 1 kg box with half-extents (0.5, 1.0, 1.5) — moments 1.083333, 0.833333 and 0.416667 — launched at ω = (0.6, 4.0, 0.3): 

  G.2  the reachable range of |omega|, DERIVED from the
       two conserved quantities and then measured

       |L|^2 = 11.549237    2T = 13.760834

       Lx = 0  infeasible
       Ly = 0  gives |omega| = 4.4880
       Lz = 0  gives |omega| = 4.0524

       predicted  4.0524 .. 4.4880
       measured   4.0527 .. 4.4924
       a swing of 10.85% with NOTHING acting on it.

Four digits of agreement between a hand derivation and sixty seconds of simulation. That is the strongest kind of check this course has: not "the number looked plausible", and not "it matched a second implementation of the same code", but "an argument that knows nothing about the implementation predicted where it would go, and it went there".

It also tells you something the simulation alone could not. The Lx = 0 row is infeasible — that combination of energy and momentum cannot be reached by any real motion — so a simulation that ever produced it would be telling you it had gone wrong, without you needing a reference to compare against.

8.2 Two more things the body now knows

Two functions arrive with L and are worth naming because 8.9 is written entirely in terms of them.

kinetic_energy is ½ m v² + ½ ω·(I ω), and the shape of the second term is the price of the resistance being a tensor: it is a quadratic form in ω rather than a product of two scalars. Like |L|, it should be flat on a torque-free body, and §9 uses it as the instrument that catches what |L| cannot.

point_velocity is v + ω × r, the world velocity of a point rigidly attached to the body. This is the formula that makes a rigid body rigid, and every contact in 8.9 is written against it — because a collision does not happen at a body's centre, it happens at a point on its surface which may be moving very differently. The standard demonstration is a rolling wheel: its centre moves forward at v, and the contact patch touching the road is instantaneously stationary, because ω × r there exactly cancels v. That is why a tyre grips rather than sliding, and it is one line of code we now have.

9. The term with no linear counterpart

So ω changes on its own. What is the equation that says how?

Differentiate L = I_world ω and be careful, because both factors depend on time:

\[ \boldsymbol{\tau} = \frac{d\mathbf{L}}{dt} = \mathbf{I}\,\dot{\boldsymbol{\omega}} + \dot{\mathbf{I}}\,\boldsymbol{\omega} \qquad\text{and}\qquad \dot{\mathbf{I}}\,\boldsymbol{\omega} = \boldsymbol{\omega} \times (\mathbf{I}\,\boldsymbol{\omega}) \]

— the second identity because I_world is being carried around by the body's own rotation, so its rate of change applied to ω is that rotation applied to L. Rearranged:

\[ \dot{\boldsymbol{\omega}} = \mathbf{I}^{-1}\bigl(\boldsymbol{\tau} - \boldsymbol{\omega} \times (\mathbf{I}\,\boldsymbol{\omega})\bigr) \]

That is Euler's equation, and the extra term is the whole difference between rotational and linear dynamics. α = I⁻¹τ is the part that looks like a = F/m. The ω × (I ω) is the gyroscopic term, and it has no linear counterpart at all — there is nothing in F = ma that corresponds to it, because m does not rotate with the body.

It is also the entire source of tumbling. Drop it and a body spun about any axis spins about that axis forever: ω̇ = 0 when τ = 0, so ω is constant, and the thrown book comes back the way it went up. Which is not what happens.

9.1 The obvious implementation diverges

Adding the term explicitly is four lines and it is the first thing anyone writes: evaluate ω × (I ω) at the state you already have, subtract, multiply by h. Here is what that does to a 1 kg box over sixty torque-free seconds, at four step rates: 

  G.5  the SAME physics, three ways. Worst drift in |L|
       over 60 torque-free seconds:

      rate      explicit      implicit     momentum
       30 Hz   3.2967e+11    4.6676e-01   3.8586e-04
       60 Hz   1.6233e+00    3.4814e-01   8.0497e-04
      240 Hz   1.8069e-01    1.2738e-01   3.0143e-03
      960 Hz   3.9355e-02    3.0173e-02   1.1838e-02

At 30 Hz the body's angular momentum grows by a factor of 3 × 10¹¹, from nothing. At 60 Hz it gains 162% of itself in a minute. That is not a small error — a debug overlay watching |L| on a torque-free body would show it climbing steadily with no input.

And the reason is 8.1's, exactly. The gyroscopic term feeds ω back into itself through a cross product, and a cross product with a fixed vector is a rotation of ω. Lesson 8.1 measured what an explicit rule does to a rotation: it scales it by √(1 + (σh)²) every step, because the update matrix's determinant is 1 + h²σ² and area in phase space is what determines whether something grows. The one place in this engine that still integrates something explicitly is the one place 8.1's determinant still bites.

9.2 The implicit fix, which is 8.1's other determinant

The standard answer is to make the step implicit: instead of evaluating the term at the state you have, ask what end-of-step angular velocity would be consistent with it. Write it as a root-finding problem in the unknown ω, in body axes where I is constant:

\[ f(\boldsymbol{\omega}) = \mathbf{I}(\boldsymbol{\omega} - \boldsymbol{\omega}_0) + h\,\boldsymbol{\omega} \times (\mathbf{I}\boldsymbol{\omega}) = \mathbf{0} \]

Differentiate it — d/dω [ω × Iω] applied to a perturbation δ is δ × Iω + ω × Iδ, which as matrices is [ω]ₓ I − [Iω]ₓ — and take one Newton step:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{J} &= \mathbf{I} + h\bigl([\boldsymbol{\omega}_0]_\times \mathbf{I} - [\mathbf{I}\boldsymbol{\omega}_0]_\times\bigr) \\[4pt] \boldsymbol{\omega}_1 &= \boldsymbol{\omega}_0 - \mathbf{J}^{-1} f(\boldsymbol{\omega}_0) \end{aligned} \]

One iteration is enough because f(ω₀) is already O(h) — a second iteration would correct an O(h²) error in a scheme that is first-order anyway. This is what Bullet does, and it is the reason skew() exists in mat3.hpp.

It works, in the sense that it stops diverging. Look at what it does instead: at 60 Hz it loses 35%. That is 8.1's other determinant — 1/(1 + h²σ²), the exact reciprocal of the explicit one — which is why backward Euler damps rather than exploding, and why 8.1 said it is the same disease with the sign flipped. Shippable. Not conserving anything.

9.3 The term is not physics

Here is the observation that reframes the whole section.

Look again at where ω × (I ω) came from. It is not a force, and it is not an effect. It appeared in the algebra on the line where we differentiated L = I(t) ω and insisted that ω be the state variable. I is turning with the body, so holding L fixed requires ω to change, and the gyroscopic term is precisely the size of that requirement.

Store L instead and it is not a term at all:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{L} &\mathrel{+}= \boldsymbol{\tau}\,h &&\text{exactly, since } d\mathbf{L}/dt = \boldsymbol{\tau} \\[4pt] \boldsymbol{\omega} &= \mathbf{I}_{\text{world}}(q)^{-1}\,\mathbf{L} &&\text{derived, every step, from the new } q \end{aligned} \]

There is no Jacobian, no Newton iteration and no ω × (I ω) anywhere. The tumble still happens — L did not move, I_world did, so ω must — and it happens because the equations say so rather than because a correction term was added to make it.

That is the third column, at 8.0e−04 against implicit's 3.5e−01. Three orders of magnitude, for less code.

So why does every shipping engine store ω? Because a contact solver speaks velocities. Lessons 8.9–8.11 iterate impulses against relative velocities at contact points, dozens of times per body per frame, and every one of those iterations would have to convert. The trade is between a cheap exact tumble and a cheap solver, and this module needs the solver — so the engine keeps ω as the state and offers the momentum formulation as a per-body mode for the handful of bodies whose tumbling the player will actually look at.

9.4 The column that gets worse

Read the momentum column of G.5 downwards and something is wrong with it, in an interesting direction:

\[ 3.86\times10^{-4} \;\to\; 8.05\times10^{-4} \;\to\; 3.01\times10^{-3} \;\to\; 1.18\times10^{-2} \]

A smaller step is a worse answer. That is the only table in this course where that happens, and every instinct built over the last eighty-five lessons says it must be a bug.

It is not, and the reason is worth more than the number. The first two columns' errors are truncation: they come from approximating a derivative over a finite step, so halving the step halves the error. The momentum column's error is not truncation at all — nothing in the momentum formulation approximates L. The only thing that can move it is arithmetic.

And there is arithmetic, because the engine stores ω rather than L. Each step rebuilds L from the angular velocity and the orientation, adds the torque, derives a new ω, and throws L away. That round trip — through two rotation matrices — loses a little every time, and "a little, every time" grows with the number of steps rather than their size. Run at 960 Hz for a minute and you have done sixteen times as many round trips as at 60.

Storing L on the body would remove it entirely, at the cost of a conversion at every solver iteration. That is a real trade and the engine does not currently take it; §13 names it as standing debt.

9.5 …and conserving L is not enough

One more measurement, and it is the one that changed the implementation.

The momentum formulation conserves L by construction, whatever it does with the orientation. Which means |L| cannot tell you whether the body is doing the right thing with its momentum — it will look perfect either way. The second conserved quantity can, and nothing in the formulation protects it:

       step. Both conserve L; only one is right.

Advancing the orientation by the angular velocity at the start of the step puts the body at |ω| = 8.04 — nearly twice the maximum §8 derived — while conserving L beautifully. Its kinetic energy, which a torque-free body also holds exactly, drifts by 97%. The momentum was right and the body was doing the wrong thing with it.

The fix is one extra rotation matrix and one extra orientation advance: take a half step first, read the angular velocity there, and use that for the whole step. Energy drift at 60 Hz goes from 9.714e−01 to 1.198e−04 — a factor of 8,100 — and |ω| lands on 4.0525 … 4.4879, inside §8's derived bound of 4.0524 … 4.4880.

This works here and would not work on angular_velocity, and the difference is worth seeing: L is fixed across the whole step, so the only thing the half step is estimating is where the body will be pointing — and that is a question a half step answers well. Midpoint methods are second order for exactly this reason, and this is one arriving in a place where its precondition happens to hold exactly.

10. The tennis racket theorem

Now the book from §2.2, with numbers.

Torque-free Euler's equations in principal axes, with moments A, B, C:

\[ \begin{aligned} A\,\dot\omega_x &= (B - C)\,\omega_y\omega_z \\ B\,\dot\omega_y &= (C - A)\,\omega_z\omega_x \\ C\,\dot\omega_z &= (A - B)\,\omega_x\omega_y \end{aligned} \]

Spin almost exactly about y: ω_y = Ω, with tiny ω_x and ω_z. To first order the third equation's right-hand side is a product of two tiny things and drops out, leaving

\[ \begin{aligned} \dot\omega_x &= \frac{B-C}{A}\,\Omega\,\omega_z, \qquad \dot\omega_z = \frac{A-B}{C}\,\Omega\,\omega_x \\[4pt] \Longrightarrow\quad \ddot\omega_x &= \frac{(B-C)(A-B)}{A\,C}\,\Omega^2\,\omega_x \end{aligned} \]

That is ẍ = k x, and everything turns on the sign of k. Negative gives sin and cos — a bounded wobble, stable. Positive gives sinh and cosh — exponential growth.

Order the moments I₁ < I₂ < I₃ and check all three cases. Spin about the largest: the two differences have opposite signs, k < 0, stable. Spin about the smallest: same, stable. Spin about the middle one: both differences are negative, their product is positive, k > 0, and the perturbation grows like

\[ \sigma = \Omega\,\sqrt{\frac{(I_3 - I_2)(I_2 - I_1)}{I_1 I_3}} \]

So it is not that the middle axis is somewhat worse. Two of the three are stable and one is exponentially unstable, and which is which is decided by nothing more than the ordering of three numbers.

10.1 The numbers

Our box has Ix = 1.083333, Iy = 0.833333, Iz = 0.416667, so y is the intermediate axis. At Ω = 10 rad/s:

\[ \begin{aligned} \sigma &= 10\sqrt{\frac{(1.083333 - 0.833333)(0.833333 - 0.416667)}{0.416667 \times 1.083333}} \\[4pt] &= 10\sqrt{0.230769} \;=\; 4.8038\ \text{s}^{-1} \end{aligned} \]

Spin about each axis in turn with a 1e−3 rad/s nudge on the other two, and run for twenty seconds: 

  H.1  spin at 10 rad/s about each axis in turn, with a
       1e-3 rad/s nudge on the other two. 20 seconds,
       omega measured in BODY axes.

      axis   nudge        grew to      verdict
      x      1.0e-03         0.0015     stable
      y      1.0e-03        10.9863     UNSTABLE
      z      1.0e-03         0.0017     stable

  H.2  the predicted rates, from Euler's equations:
       about x (largest) : oscillates at 6.9282 rad/s
       about y (middle)  : GROWS at e^(4.8038 t)
       about z (smallest): oscillates at 5.5470 rad/s

The two stable axes keep their perturbation at about 1e−3 for twenty seconds. The middle one takes it from 1e−3 to 11 rad/s — larger than the spin itself, which is what a complete reorientation looks like in these coordinates.

And the rate is not just qualitatively right: 

  H.3  fitted over the first decade of growth, at four
       step rates — the prediction is a continuous-time
       one, so the columns should converge toward it

      rate      measured   predicted    ratio

Four step rates, each a factor of four finer, and the measured growth rate walks in on the predicted one: 2.1% out, then 0.56%, then 0.19%, then 0.096%. Each refinement roughly quarters the error, which is the signature of a second-order convergence and is what the midpoint pairing of §9.5 buys.

The first version of this measurement read 3.8498 against a predicted 4.8038 and did not converge — a 20% miss that looked like a failed prediction and was a failed frame. Euler's equations are body-frame equations; the engine stores ω in world space; and for a body spinning about its own y axis, the two perturbation components are being carried around that axis at 10 rad/s. So the world-space ω.x oscillates at 10 Hz while its envelope grows, and sampling it at a threshold crossing samples the oscillation. Everything in §10 measures Rᵀ ω.

10.2 The flip, and the control that makes it mean something

The growth saturates — the body cannot spin faster than |L| allows — and what happens instead is that it turns over: 

  H.4  omega_y reverses sign at t = 2.606, 5.271, 7.877,
       10.414 s
       interval 2.6651 s, and it repeats forever with
       no torque of any kind.

  H.5  CONTROL term OFF, 40 s: worst perpendicular
       |omega| = 1.0000e-04 — it started at 1.0000e-04
       and never moved. No flip, ever.

Every 2.67 seconds, forever, with nothing acting on the body. That is the Dzhanibekov effect, named after the cosmonaut who noticed a wing-nut doing it in orbit in 1985 and reportedly assumed something was wrong with the spacecraft.

H.5 is the control, and it is what makes the rest of the section an argument rather than an anecdote. Turn the gyroscopic term off and the perturbation does not merely flip less — it does not move at all, at the sixth significant figure, over forty seconds. There is nothing left to couple the three components, so the flip is not a numerical artefact or an emergent surprise. It is a direct consequence of the one term most engines drop.

11. The implementation

Everything above is now four files. Taken in dependency order.

11.1 mat3.hpp: arithmetic, an outer product and a skew

Addition, subtraction, negation, scalar multiply, the compound assignments — and then outer, skew, trace, diagonal and asymmetry. The block carries a comment explaining why it arrives eighty lessons late, because a reader who finds arithmetic operators in a file that has managed without them since Module 2 deserves to know what changed.

diagonal(vec3) is numerically identical to scale(x, y, z) and is a separate name on purpose: a diagonal inertia tensor is not a scale, it is a body whose principal axes happen to be the coordinate axes, and code that reads diagonal(moments) says which of the two it meant.

11.2 inertia.hpp: what a shape weighs, distributed

A new header and a new translation unit, and the split from rigid_body is the dependency direction the module has been building toward since 8.1: inertia.cpp knows about shapes and nothing about bodies; rigid_body.cpp knows about bodies and asks this file what a shape weighs. Nothing points back up the chain.

It holds the atom (inertia_of_point), a brute-force sum over a cloud (inertia_of_points), the six closed forms, the two directions of the parallel-axis theorem, the basis change, a guarded inverse, the assembly builder, and inspect_inertia.

inverse_inertia is worth a paragraph. mat3::inverse already returns zeros on an exact zero determinant, which covers the all-zeros tensor of an immovable body. It does not cover the case that actually bites: a tensor that is merely nearly singular — a very thin rod, a very flat plate — where the determinant is a small number rather than zero and the inverse comes back as 10³⁰ with no complaint, and one step later the body is spinning at a speed with no name. So the test is relative: the determinant has units of inertia cubed, so it is compared against the largest diagonal entry cubed.

11.3 rigid_body: five new fields, and one that is redundant

orientation, angular_velocity, torque, inv_inertia_local, angular_damping and a gyroscopic_mode. All of them mirror something on the linear side except the tensor, which mirrors inv_mass nine floats wide — including the argument for storing the inverse, which survives intact: a zero tensor is representable, exact, needs no sentinel, and passes through the sandwich unchanged, so a wall absorbs every torque in the scene with no branch anywhere.

And then a sixth field that is redundant, and is there because §12 measured it: inertia_local, the forward tensor, stored beside its own inverse. Thirty-six bytes of duplication, with an invariant that nothing can enforce — which is exactly the cost of caching anything, and is paid because the alternative was a 3×3 inverse per body per step in two separate places. set_inertia is the only supported way to write either.

11.4 The step

The angular half is structurally the mirror of the linear one — a resistance, an accumulator, a step — with three differences that are the whole of this lesson:

  • The resistance is a tensor in body axes, so it is carried into world space by the sandwich before it can act on a world-space torque.
  • There is a term with no linear counterpart, and four things the body can do about it.
  • The orientation update is not an addition.

The orientation update is semi-implicit always, whatever rule_ says, and the inconsistency is deliberate rather than an oversight. rule_ chooses how a position update is paired with a velocity update; an orientation update is not a position update, and velocity Verlet's second half would need the tensor rebuilt at the new orientation and a second renormalisation, to correct by less than the renormalisation's own error. §7 measured both sides of that.

Kinematic bodies turn as well as travel, which is one line and is what a rotating platform, a swinging door and a fan blade all are. Fixed bodies are skipped entirely, and both accumulators are still cleared so that a system pushing on the floor does not leak a growing vector.

11.5 The report, and an instrument that measures somebody else

step_report gains angular_momentum, max_spin and max_unit_error.

The angular momentum is about the world origin and has two terms, the second of which people forget: I ω is the body's spin about its own centre, and r × (m v) is the angular momentum its linear motion carries about the origin. A body sailing past in a straight line without rotating at all has the second and not the first — which is how a planet's orbit conserves one.

max_unit_error is the interesting one, because it is deliberately not a measurement of the integrator. It reads | |q| − 1 | as the step finds each body, before touching anything — and since step renormalises on the way out, anything it finds was written by somebody else. An animation blend, a network packet, a hand-authored quat{0.7f, {0.7f, 0, 0}}: all of them leave a body slightly off the unit sphere, all of them render as a subtle shear that is very hard to see and impossible to grep for, and all of them show up here as a number that is not 1e−7.

12. The budget

8.2 measured a whole body update at a couple of nanoseconds. The angular half is considerably more than a couple, and the first version of this section did not find that out because it was measuring the wrong thing.

The first draft of §12 measured a memcpy. Each arm was written as body_world w = seed; … w.step(h), so that both arms would start from identical state — a reasonable instinct, and it put a 557 KB copy of the pool inside the timed region. Every arm reported 34–58 ns/body against 8.2's 1–2, every ratio came out near 1, and the conclusion would have been "the angular half is free". Each arm gets its own world now, built once, and the timed body is a single step.

12.1 Where the time actually went

With that fixed, the step came out at 33.793 ns/body — twenty times 8.2's, which is a lot for an update whose physics is one matrix-vector product and an integrator step. Breaking it down piece by piece found that most of it was not physics:

  • mat3_from_quat, 7.843 ns — and the step was calling it twice, once inside world_inverse_inertia and once twenty lines later inside angular_momentum, on the same quaternion.
  • angular_momentum, 13.418 ns — 40% of the whole step, for a diagnostic that is computed whether or not anybody reads it, and which carried a 3×3 inverse because the body stored only the inverse tensor.
  • The rest — the sandwich, the integrator, the report — accounting for the remainder.

Two changes followed, and both are visible in the listings. The forward tensor is now stored beside its inverse, which removes the 3×3 inverse from the report and from the gyroscopic term. And the step builds its rotation matrix once and uses it for both, which is why step spells the sandwich out rather than calling world_inverse_inertia.

The hoist is measurable on its own, inside one run, which is what makes it a fact rather than a before-and-after story: 

  I.3  mat3_from_quat, built twice or hoisted
       twice    14.577 ns/body
       hoisted  11.139 ns/body
       ratio hoisted/twice 0.764

  I.4  R I^-1 R^T rebuilt    8.545 ns/body
       read from a cache     0.621 ns/body
       ratio cached/rebuilt 0.073

Together the two changes took the step from 33.793 to 18.778 ns/body, a factor of 1.80, with no change to a single number this lesson reports. That is the whole of §12's optimisation: it is not clever, it is noticing that a rotation matrix was being built twice.

13. What this does not do

The engine can turn. Six things it deliberately cannot, each of which is somebody's later lesson or somebody's standing debt.

No centre-of-mass offset. A body's centre of mass is its state.position, always. inertia_assembly computes where a compound body balances so that you can place it there, rather than carrying an offset through every subsequent calculation. That is a real restriction — an authored mesh whose origin is at its feet needs its inertia and its position reconciled by the caller — and it removes an offset from every line of 8.9's solver, which is what it was traded for.

No diagonalisation. Every closed form here is diagonal because its shape is symmetric, but a compound body's tensor generally is not, and this engine never finds its principal axes. Doing so is an eigenvalue problem on a symmetric 3×3 — Jacobi's method, about fifty lines — and it is genuinely out of scope here rather than hard. What it would buy is the ability to store three floats instead of nine, which is what Bullet does. Exercise 4, and Golub & Van Loan §8.5 if you want it done properly.

L is not stored, so the momentum formulation's conservation is as good as a round trip through two rotation matrices, which is §9.4's counterintuitive column. Fixing it means adding angular_momentum to the struct and converting at every solver iteration. Named here so that it is a decision rather than an omission.

No angular tunnelling protection. max_spin measures how fast the fastest body is turning, which is the rotational twin of max_travel, and nothing does anything about it. A body turning more than about a radian per step cannot have its contacts tracked frame to frame, because the features that were touching are on the other side by the time the next test runs. 8.8's manifold persistence is where that comes due.

No collision, still. Four lessons away. The spin demo has no floor and no gravity, which is the experiment rather than a missing feature — every claim in it is about a body with nothing acting on it.

And the gyroscopic default is off, which means that out of the box this engine's bodies do not tumble. That is what Bullet and PhysX both ship (⚠ VERIFY the exact spellings against the headers: Bullet's BT_ENABLE_GYROSCOPIC_FORCE_IMPLICIT_BODY flag on btRigidBody, and PhysX's PxRigidBodyFlag::eENABLE_GYROSCOPIC_FORCES, both opt-in), and it is a defensible default — but it means the most interesting thing in this lesson is something you have to ask for.

16. Common pitfalls & debugging

Symptom: a body will not rotate about its own long axis, however much torque you apply. Cause: an inertia tensor built with the derived form |r|² 1 − r ⊗ r on a long thin body, where catastrophic cancellation returns an exact zero for the small principal moment (§4.4). Fix: build the diagonal from the terms that belong in it. Diagnosis: print inspect_inertia(...).diagonal and look for a zero where you expected a small number. inverse_inertia's relative test catches the near-singular cases, and it catching them is the signal.

Symptom: a tumble that looks plausible and is wrong, with no error anywhere. Cause: Rᵀ I R where R I Rᵀ was meant. It is symmetric, has the same trace, the same determinant and the same principal moments, and passes every test inspect_inertia can run (§6.2). Fix: read the sandwich right to left and check that the first thing applied to ω carries it into body axes. Diagnosis: put the body at a generic orientation — never a right angle, which passes either way — and check that the world Ixx matches the body moment you expect.

Symptom: a body shears, then flattens, then disappears. Cause: an orientation integrated without renormalising. The linearised update inflates |q| by (|ω|h)²/8 per step, 23% in a second at 10 rad/s, and mat3_from_quat on a non-unit quaternion produces a matrix with a scale in it (§7.3). Fix: advance_orientation renormalises; if you are writing orientation from outside the step, normalise it yourself. Diagnosis: step_report::max_unit_error exists exactly for this and reads about 1e−7 when everything is well.

Symptom: the rotation is right for a body at the origin facing forward and drifts for any other. Cause: an angular velocity used on the wrong side of the quaternion product — a body-space ω composed as though it were world-space, or vice versa. Both produce unit quaternions and smooth motion; they differ by 57.6° after one second in §7's F.5. Fix: world space multiplies from the left, body space from the right. Diagnosis: the bug vanishes when the body starts at the identity orientation, which is a strong tell and is why it survives testing.

Symptom: a torque-free body slowly speeds up until the simulation is unusable. Cause: an explicitly integrated gyroscopic term (§9.1). Fix: implicit_term or momentum. Diagnosis: plot |L| on a body with no torque on it. It must be flat, and a rising |L| means the integrator is inventing angular momentum — there is nothing else it can mean.

Symptom: a compound body's tensor is far too small and nothing complains. Cause: parts added without shifting them to the common centre of mass (§5.2) — 15.3× too small on the dumbbell, and it passes every validity test. Fix: inertia_assembly. Diagnosis: compare against a hand estimate of the dominant parallel-axis term, m d², which for a spread-out assembly is usually most of the answer.

Symptom: a measurement of a perturbation oscillates instead of growing, and a fitted rate is 20% low. Cause: measuring ω in world space when Euler's equations are body-frame equations (§10.1's callout). Fix: transpose(mat3_from_quat(q)) * ω. Diagnosis: the oscillation is at the spin rate, which is a strong tell — a perturbation has no reason to know about it.

Symptom: a benchmark says the angular half is free. Cause: a copy of the body pool inside the timed region (§12's callout). Fix: build the state once, outside. Diagnosis: the control arm. Two identical arms that come out at ratio 1.000 while every real comparison also comes out near 1.000 is the shape of a benchmark measuring something both arms have in common.

17. Exercises

1. The hollow box. Add inertia_hollow_box — a box of six thin plates rather than a solid volume, which is what a crate actually is. Build it with inertia_assembly from six rectangular plates, using the plate's own tensor (m(w² + h²)/12 about its normal, and the perpendicular-axis theorem for the other two) plus the parallel-axis shift to each face. Check it against inertia_of_points sampling only the surface. Hint: for a thin plate in the xz plane, Iy = Ix + Iz exactly — that is the perpendicular-axis theorem, and it is the equality case of the triangle inequality inspect_inertia checks.

2. The wheel that does not slip. Write a demo with a single body on which point_velocity at the lowest point is exactly zero, given a forward velocity v and a radius R. Derive the angular velocity that makes it so before you write it, then verify the contact patch's speed numerically at several points around the rim. Hint: the answer is ω = v/R about the correct axis, and getting the sign right the first time is the exercise.

3. The reaction wheel. Give a body a second, small body embedded in it, and spin the small one with add_torque. The big one must counter-rotate, and the total angular momentum of the pair must not change. Measure it. Hint: this is how a satellite points itself with no fuel, and it is Newton's third law applied to torques — apply the equal and opposite torque to the carrier.

4. (Engine challenge) Principal axes. Implement diagonalise(mat3) for a symmetric matrix using the cyclic Jacobi method: repeatedly find the largest off-diagonal element and apply the 2×2 rotation that zeroes it, accumulating the rotations. Return the three principal moments and the rotation to principal axes. Then use it to store a body's inertia as three floats and a quaternion, as Bullet does, and measure what that saves against §12's numbers. Solution sketch: each sweep drives the sum of squared off-diagonal elements down by the element you zeroed, so it converges quadratically; six to ten sweeps is plenty for a 3×3 in float. Golub & Van Loan §8.5 has it done properly, including the rotation angle that avoids cancellation.

5. (Engine challenge) Store the momentum. Add vec3 angular_momentum to rigid_body as a stored field, make the momentum mode use it instead of rebuilding L each step, and re-run §9's G.5 table. The third column should stop getting worse with a finer step and become flat at the float noise floor. Then find out what it cost: every place that writes angular_velocity — add_impulse_at, add_angular_impulse, damping, and every solver iteration in 8.10 — now has to write both, or convert. That is the trade the engine declined, and doing it is the only way to see its real size.

18. Recap & next

The engine can turn. A rigid_body carries an orientation, an angular velocity, a torque accumulator and an inertia tensor; add_force_at is the first function in eighty-six lessons that can make something tumble; and inertia.hpp knows what six shapes weigh, how to carry a tensor to a new point and how to carry it into a new basis.

The ideas worth keeping, in the order they arrived:

  • Resistance to turning is a tensor, because the two cross products in L = Σ m r × (ω × r) do not cancel into a scalar. The outer product is where a cross product becomes a matrix, and that is the step the whole derivation turns on.
  • A derivation tells you what to compute, not how. |r|² 1 − r ⊗ r returns exactly zero for a plank in float, and −[r]ₓ[r]ₓ — the same algebra — returns the right answer and is faster.
  • A tensor is about a point and in a basis. The parallel-axis theorem moves the point and only works from the centre of mass; R I Rᵀ changes the basis and has a plausible wrong twin that passes every structural test there is.
  • An orientation is not a vector, so it does not integrate by addition. The update that replaces it inflates the quaternion by (ωh)²/8 and turns short by (ωh)²/12, both predictable before measurement.
  • L is conserved and ω is not, which is a fact about I_world changing rather than about anything acting on the body — and the reachable range of |ω| is derivable by hand, because both conserved quantities are linear in Lᵢ².
  • The gyroscopic term is the price of a state variable. Store the momentum and it vanishes from the equations — and conserving the right quantity is still not enough, because the body can do the wrong thing with it.
  • Two of a body's three principal axes are stable and one is not, which is decided by nothing more than the ordering of three numbers, and is why a thrown book flips.

And the measurement worth remembering is §12's, because it is the shape of most real optimisations: the step was twenty times slower than 8.2's, and the largest single cause was a rotation matrix being built twice on the same quaternion, twenty lines apart, in two functions that were each perfectly reasonable on their own.

Next is Lesson 8.4, and the module turns a corner. Everything from 8.1 to here has been about a single body moving on its own; from 8.4 onward, bodies start to notice each other. It opens with the cheapest questions — does this sphere overlap that one, does this axis-aligned box overlap that one — and then the Separating Axis Theorem, which answers it for boxes that are not axis-aligned and is one of the most useful ideas in the module. The point_velocity and add_impulse_at we built today are what 8.9 will apply the answers with.

19. Further reading

  • Baraff, Physically Based Modeling: Rigid Body Simulation (SIGGRAPH course notes, freely available) — the canonical derivation of everything in §4 through §9, and notably the one that uses the momentum formulation of §9.3 as its primary state.   • Catto, Numerical Methods and Soft Constraints (GDC) — the practical side of §9, including why engines integrate the way they do and what they give up. Catto's Box2D writings are the clearest explanation anywhere of the gap between a correct integrator and a shippable one.

  • Golub & Van Loan, Matrix Computations, §8.5 — the symmetric eigenvalue problem, for exercise 4.
  • Higham, Accuracy and Stability of Numerical Algorithms, chapter 1 — catastrophic cancellation, which is §4.4's subject, done properly and with a great deal more rigour than one worked example.
  • Terzopoulos/Witkin's and Bullet's source — specifically btRigidBody::computeGyroscopicImpulseImplicit_Body, which is §9.2's Newton step as a shipping engine writes it (⚠ VERIFY the exact spelling against the current headers).
