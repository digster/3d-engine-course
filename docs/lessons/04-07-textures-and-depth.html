1The Problem

Lesson 4.6 gave the camera freedom, and freedom immediately exposed something that two lessons had been hiding. Orbit down towards the ring of tori until two of them overlap on screen, and whichever was drawn later wins — regardless of which is nearer.

That has been true since Lesson 4.4. It went unnoticed because Lesson 4.5 arranged its seven instances on a ring, at a scale and a camera angle where nothing overlapped, and Lesson 4.6 inherited that arrangement. This is worth naming as a general shape, because it will happen to you: a limitation you have designed around stops being visible, and starts being load-bearing. The scene was not built to hide the missing depth test — but it did, and the day the camera moved, two lessons' worth of quiet wrongness arrived at once.

The second thing is quieter still. The texture coordinate has been riding through the vertex buffer since Lesson 4.5, and its entire job so far has been to draw a diagnostic grid so we could tell it had arrived. Nothing has ever been painted.

So: a depth buffer and a texture. You wrote both, on the CPU, in Lessons 3.1 and 3.9 — and both of those lessons were written knowing this one was coming. Here is what depth_buffer.hpp said in Module 3, before any of Module 4 existed:

The hardware keeps them separate. SDL_GPU's SDL_BeginGPURenderPass takes an array of SDL_GPUColorTargetInfo and, as a separate parameter, a SDL_GPUDepthStencilTargetInfo* — which "may be NULL". […] Modelling that split now means Module 4 is a rename, not a redesign.

A promise made two modules early is a promise worth checking. Most of this lesson is that check — and it passes, which means the lesson can spend its length on the parts that are genuinely new: an image decoder, a sampler object, and the depth-precision question that nobody warns you about until a wall starts flickering at fifty metres.

2Building Intuition

2.1 Depth is an attachment, not a mode

On the CPU, turning depth testing on meant passing a depth_buffer* to draw_triangles instead of nullptr. On the GPU it is two separate decisions in two separate places, and keeping them straight is most of what goes wrong:

  • The render pass gets an attachment. A second image, the same size as the colour target, with its own load and store operations — cleared at the start of the pass exactly as the colour target is.
  • The pipeline gets three fields. Whether to test, whether to write, and what comparison to use. These have been sitting in SDL_GPUDepthStencilState, zero-filled, since Lesson 4.4.

Both are required and neither implies the other, which is exactly right: a shadow pass writes depth and no colour, and a transparent pass tests depth without writing it. Fuse them into one "depth on" switch and neither is expressible — which is the argument depth_buffer.hpp made in Lesson 3.1 for not putting the comparison inside the buffer class.

2.2 A sampler is an object

In Lesson 3.9, how to read a texture was an argument:

On the GPU it is an object, created once and bound alongside the texture. If that shape feels familiar, it should: it is the fourth time this module has made the same move. Render state became a pipeline object (4.4); the vertex layout became pipeline state (4.5); per-draw constants became a push (4.6); and now the rules for reading a texture become a sampler. Every one of them is a decision the hardware must specialise for, and a decision the hardware specialises for cannot be a parameter of the innermost loop.

The separation into two objects is worth a moment, because neither OpenGL nor our CPU path modelled it that way and it is better than both. A texture is the pixels; a sampler is the rules. Keeping them apart means one image can be read three ways in a single frame by binding it with three samplers, and one sampler serves every texture in a material system without being duplicated per image. They meet only at the binding — SDL_GPUTextureSamplerBinding, t0 paired with s0.

2.3 The one thing we do not write

This course has hand-rolled the maths library, the rasterizer, the OBJ parser, and will hand-roll the ECS, the allocators and the collision system. It is about to use somebody else's PNG decoder, and the reason is not difficulty.

A baseline PNG decoder is an afternoon. PNG in the wild is DEFLATE, plus five per-scanline filter modes, plus Adam7 interlacing, plus sixteen-bit channels, plus palettes, plus tRNS transparency, plus gamma and colour-profile chunks. A decoder that handles the files you happened to test is worse than no decoder at all, because it fails on a user's asset rather than on yours. And crucially: there is nothing about game engines in the fifth filter mode.

Compare with parse_obj, which we did write. OBJ is a line-based text format whose difficulty is entirely in the thing this course is about — the mismatch between how a file describes a vertex and how hardware fetches one. That is the test: is the hard part the subject? For OBJ, yes. For PNG, no.

The approved list, and why it is a list

The master prompt names five third-party libraries and no others: stb_image, stb_truetype, Dear ImGui (tooling only), cgltf, and SDL_shadercross. stb_image enters here; the rest arrive when their problem does. Each carries the same obligation this section just discharged — say out loud why you are not writing it — because a dependency added without that sentence is a dependency nobody can later argue with.

3The Theory

3.1 The depth test, as three fields

Here is the whole of it:

LESS, and the reason is a convention rather than a preference: SDL_GPU's NDC puts 0 at the near plane and 1 at the far plane (Conventions §4), so smaller is nearer and a nearer fragment must compare less-than to win. That is the same reason Lesson 3.1's software test was a < and its cleared buffer was full of 1.0.

enable_depth_write is documented as ignored while the test is off, so the two travel together — but they are genuinely independent when the test is on, and the pair that matters is test without write: that is how a transparent pass sorts itself against opaque geometry without occluding other transparent surfaces.

3.2 Why precision falls off as the square of distance

Now the part that is not a port. A depth buffer stores a number per pixel, and that number is not the distance — it is the distance run through Lesson 2.10's projection. That distinction is the entire subject of this section, and it has a cost you can compute.

Start from the projection matrix on the Conventions page. For a view-space point at depth z, the third and fourth clip components are zclip = A(z + n) and w = −z, with A = f/(n−f). Write d = −z for the positive distance in front of the eye, and the perspective divide gives:

\[ z_{\text{ndc}} = \frac{A(n - d)}{d} = \frac{f}{f-n}\left(1 - \frac{n}{d}\right) \]

In words: depth in the buffer is not proportional to distance — it is proportional to one minus near-over-distance, which is a hyperbola.

Check it at the two ends. At d = n the bracket is 1 − 1 = 0, so z = 0. At d = f the bracket is 1 − n/f = (f−n)/f, which cancels the leading factor exactly and gives 1. Near maps to 0 and far maps to 1, as SDL_GPU requires.

Now differentiate, because what we want to know is not where a surface lands but how far it must move before the buffer notices:

\[ \frac{dz}{dd} = \frac{f}{f-n} \cdot \frac{n}{d^{2}} \]

The rate at which depth changes with distance is proportional to one over distance squared. Double the distance and you get a quarter of the resolution.

Worked example, with our own frustum. Near 0.3, far 100. At one metre:

\[ z_{\text{ndc}}(1) = \frac{100}{99.7}\left(1 - \frac{0.3}{1}\right) = 1.00301 \times 0.7 = 0.7021 \]

Seventy per cent of the entire depth range is spent in the first metre. The remaining ninety-nine metres share the other thirty per cent. That single number is the whole of why depth buffers behave the way they do, and it follows from nothing more exotic than the perspective divide having a 1/d in it.

Turn that into the number an engine actually needs. If the format stores N evenly spaced codes across [0, 1], then one code is 1/N, and the smallest world-space separation that spans one code is:

\[ \Delta d = \frac{1}{N} \cdot \frac{dd}{dz} = \frac{(f-n)\,d^{2}}{f\,n\,N} \]

For D16 (N = 65,535) at ninety metres that is 0.41 metres. Two walls forty centimetres apart, ninety metres away, occupy the same depth code. Whichever is drawn second wins, per pixel, and as the camera moves the winner changes — which is z-fighting, with a number attached to it at last.

3.3 What each format can actually separate

A formula is a prediction. Here is the measurement: draw a far surface, then a near one, with the depth test on, and bisect on the gap until the near one stops winning.

The measurement tracks the prediction to within a few per cent across two orders of magnitude — 0.050 mm measured against 0.051 predicted at one metre, 412 mm against 411 at ninety. Theory and hardware agreeing that closely is worth pausing on: it means the model in §3.2 is not an approximation of what the hardware does, it is what the hardware does.

D24_UNORM does not exist here

Look at the support table in the figure. D24_UNORM — the format a desktop renderer would have hard-coded — is not available on this device. SDL guarantees exactly one depth format, D16_UNORM, and everything else is a query. This is the same discipline Lesson 4.2 established for the swapchain format and the present modes, arriving in the one place where getting it wrong is not a missing feature but a failure to create a texture at startup.

3.4 Reversed-Z

§3.2's derivation has a consequence that looks like a trick and is really just arithmetic.

A _UNORM depth format spreads its codes evenly across [0, 1]. A float does not: floating point stores its precision near zero, with roughly as many representable values between 0.001 and 0.002 as between 0.5 and 1.0. So a D32_FLOAT depth buffer has enormous precision near z = 0 and comparatively little near z = 1.

And where does the ordinary mapping put the far plane? At z = 1 — the coarse end. Which is precisely where §3.2 already told us 1/d² has thrown the resolution away. The two effects compound instead of cancelling.

So reverse it: map near to 1 and far to 0. Now the float's fine spacing near zero lands at the far plane, where the projection is at its coarsest, and the two effects very nearly cancel instead. Three changes and no new concepts — one subtraction in the vertex shader, COMPAREOP_GREATER instead of LESS, and clearing the buffer to 0 instead of 1.

180× at ninety metres, from 2.2 mm to 0.012 mm. And note the control: D16_UNORM gains nothing at all — 412.4 mm either way — because its codes are evenly spaced and reversing which end is which moves none of them. That the measurement shows a large effect on exactly the format the theory predicts and no effect on the format the theory predicts nothing for is what makes it believable.

Why this engine does not use it yet

Reversed-Z is standard practice in production renderers and this engine does not use it, which deserves a reason rather than an omission. It touches the projection matrix, the compare op, the clear value, and every later pass that reads depth — so it is a change to make once, deliberately, with the shadow maps and depth-based post-processing of Module 6 in view, rather than now for a scene whose far plane is a hundred metres and whose deepest object is at nine. Exercise 4.7.4 does it properly.

3.5 Which way up, and the one flag that matters

Two questions about an image, and they can both be answered with numbers rather than by squinting at a screenshot.

Which way up? Lesson 3.9 established that Wavefront OBJ counts v upwards from the bottom while every GPU texture counts it downwards from the top, and applied the flip at import. That was reasoned from documentation, because no image existed to test it against. assets/uv_grid.png now exists, and it carries a differently-coloured block in each corner precisely so that a program can read the four corners back and say which way up the picture arrived.

And what does _SRGB do? Lesson 3.9 spent a section on the fact that an albedo is a reflectance, a reflectance multiplies a quantity of light, and both sides of that multiply must be in linear space. It measured what skipping the decode costs: two texels blended in encoded space give 0.2139 where 0.5 is correct — 43% of the light. On the GPU that decode is one enum.

The _UNORM column matches the file byte for byte, in every corner. That is a stronger statement than "it looked right": the decoder, the upload, the sampler and the readback all agree about which way v runs and which channel is which, and any one of them being wrong would have shown here.

The _SRGB column is darker, and by the right amount: 222 becomes 186, and sRGB-decoding 222/255 = 0.871 gives 0.7305, which is 186.3 out of 255. The sampler did Lesson 3.9's per-texel arithmetic for free — and, crucially, before the filter rather than after, which is the ordering the software path had to construct by hand and the whole reason _SRGB texture formats exist.

4Implementation

4.1 A decoder behind our own interface

stb_image arrives the way every dependency in this project arrives: pinned, and at arm's length.

A commit SHA rather than a tag, because stb has no releases — the same situation Lesson 4.3 hit with SDL_shadercross, resolved the same way. A branch name would mean a fresh clone could decode differently from yours.

The library is header-only, which raises the one thing about stb that catches people:

STB_IMAGE_IMPLEMENTATION turns the header from declarations into definitions, and it must be defined in exactly one translation unit: two and the linker reports every function twice, none and it reports every function missing. Isolating it in one .cpp means no other file in the engine ever includes stb_image.h — they include image.hpp, which mentions no third-party type at all.

That isolation is the point of the file existing rather than the decode happening at the call site. A third-party library reaches exactly as far into a codebase as its types appear in headers, and stb's reach is one file. If it is ever replaced, one file changes.

STBI_NO_STDIO is worth a sentence too: it removes stb's FILE* API, so the decode takes bytes we already read with SDL_LoadFile. One notion of where files live (Lesson 3.5's asset_path), one error style, one place that knows how the memory is freed. Two file-opening paths in one program is two answers to "why can it not find my asset".

C++ — compiling somebody else's code with your warnings

image.cpp wraps the include in #pragma GCC diagnostic push and silences three warnings. That is deliberate and it is not laziness: the alternative is editing stb to satisfy -Wall -Wextra, and an edited dependency is one you can no longer update. Suppress at the boundary, never at the source — and suppress narrowly, so that your own code in the same file is still held to the standard.

Finally, the decode asks for four channels always, whatever the file held:

The 4 costs a byte per pixel on images that had no alpha and buys something worth more: nothing downstream ever branches on what shape a file happened to be. Our test image is three-channel PNG and arrives as RGBA; the engine never learns the difference.

4.2 The texture, and the depth target

Both are SDL_GPUTexture. They differ in usage, in format, and in whether anything is ever uploaded to them — so gpu_texture has two creation paths and one destructor.

The sampled path is Lesson 4.2's transfer chain with a different destination, and one argument that matters more than the rest of the function:

One enum, and it decides whether the lighting maths in the fragment shader is correct. §3.5 measured what it does; this is where it is chosen. An albedo texture is _SRGB. A normal map, a roughness map, a mask — anything whose values are numbers rather than colours — is _UNORM, because decoding them would corrupt data that was never encoded in the first place. That distinction is one of the commonest mistakes in a material system and Module 6 will lean on it hard.

The depth path creates and stops:

DEPTH_STENCIL_TARGET and nothing else. Adding SAMPLER would let a later pass read the depth buffer, which is exactly what Module 6's shadow maps need and this lesson does not — and asking for a usage you do not need can force the driver into a layout that is slower for the usage you do. Ask for what you use.

4.3 The sampler, and a cast with a test behind it

Here is the whole port of Lesson 3.9's sampler:

Two casts. Lesson 3.9 defined engine::filter and engine::address_mode to match SDL's enumerators one for one, wrote down that it was doing so and why, and verify_42 §G has asserted the correspondence on every run since Module 4 opened. So this is not a coincidence being relied upon: it is a rename with a regression test behind it, and if SDL ever inserts an enumerator the test fails there rather than this cast silently selecting the wrong mode.

That is what "written so the port is a rename" is worth, two modules later, and it is worth noticing how little it cost at the time — an ordering decision and a comment.

The interesting part of the create-info is the fields Lesson 3.9's function had no room for: separate minification and magnification filters, three address modes for three axes, the mipmap chain and its LOD clamps, anisotropy, and enable_compare. That last one is worth knowing about now even though we set it false: it makes the sampler perform the depth comparison itself and return a filtered occlusion value, which is how percentage-closer shadow filtering works and why it is nearly free.

4.4 The attachment, and the resize nobody remembers

The depth attachment goes on the render pass, and its fields mirror the colour target's:

clear_depth = 1.0f is the far plane, because SDL_GPU's NDC runs 0 at near to 1 at far and the test is LESS. Clear it to 0 and every fragment fails — a black screen with no error, and one of the two or three most common ways to lose an afternoon here.

STOREOP_DONT_CARE is the interesting one. Nothing reads this buffer after the pass ends, so telling the driver so lets it skip writing the depth buffer back to memory entirely. On a tile-based GPU — every phone, and Apple silicon — that is real bandwidth, and it is the sort of thing that is free to get right and expensive to notice later.

And then the piece that is not in any tutorial:

A depth attachment must match its colour target's dimensions, and the window is resizable. Create it once at startup and the first time somebody drags the window corner the pass fails. This is the one piece of bookkeeping a depth buffer costs, it is trivial, and it is forgotten constantly — because on the machine where the code was written, nobody resized the window.

4.5 Instruments for a question with no API

§3.3 needed to know the smallest gap a depth buffer can resolve, and there is no call that answers it. So, as in Lesson 4.6, the question gets turned into a picture and the picture gets counted.

depth_probe.vert.hlsl emits a full-target surface at an exact view distance, writing the clip position directly from Lesson 2.10's terms rather than through a camera:

After the divide that is xy = corner — the whole target — and z_ndc = z_clip / d, which is exactly the expression §3.2 derived. No camera, no matrix, no vertex buffer: the experiment measures the depth buffer and cannot accidentally be measuring anything else.

Then the test itself, which is binary by construction:

Draw the far surface first, then the near one. With LESS, the near one must beat what is already there. If the format can separate them, the near colour survives everywhere; if it cannot, the near draw fails its test and the far colour is left. Count orange pixels.

Bisect on the gap and you have the number. Except that the first version of this measurement was wrong, in a way worth keeping.

Two measurement bugs, and what they looked like

First: the answer at a single distance is not a property of the format at all. It depends on where that distance happens to fall between two representable depth codes — land just below a boundary and a nanometre crosses it, land just above and you need very nearly a whole code. Measured that way, D16 reported 1.4 mm at ten metres and 2.6 mm at twenty-five: a curve going the wrong way, which is the signal that the experiment rather than the hardware is being measured. The fix is to sample several nearby distances and take the maximum, which is also the honest number — it is what a renderer has to survive.

Second: those nearby distances were evenly spaced, and at twenty-five metres the step happened to be almost exactly one code wide. So sixteen samples measured one situation sixteen times and reported a quarter of the true spacing. That is aliasing — in a course that has already met it in a texture (3.9) and in a vsync measurement (4.2), now in a probe. The fix is the standard one: offsets at fractional multiples of the golden ratio, which never lock to any period.

With both fixed, the measurement matches §3.2's formula to a few per cent across two orders of magnitude and reproduces exactly between runs. That agreement is the reason to trust either.

7Common Pitfalls & Debugging

Nothing draws at all after adding depth

Symptom: black screen, no error.
Cause: clear_depth is 0 instead of 1. Every fragment fails a LESS test against a buffer already full of the nearest possible value.
Fix: 1.0 is far in SDL_GPU's NDC. If you are using reversed-Z, it is 0 — and the compare op is GREATER. The clear value and the compare op always change together.

The pass fails the first time the window is resized

Symptom: works perfectly until somebody drags a corner.
Cause: the depth attachment no longer matches the colour target's size.
Fix: recreate it when the swapchain dimensions change (§4.4). Note that this will never reproduce on a machine where you do not resize the window, which is the machine you are on.

Distant surfaces flicker as the camera moves

Symptom: z-fighting — two coplanar or near-coplanar surfaces swapping which is visible, per pixel, frame to frame.
Cause: they are closer together than the depth format can resolve at that distance. §3.2 gives you the number: Δd = (f−n)·d²/(f·n·N).
Fix, in order of how much they buy: move the near plane out (it is in the numerator of the resolution, so 0.3 → 1.0 is a 3× improvement everywhere); use reversed-Z with a float format; use a wider format; and only then consider moving the far plane in, which helps least.

Everything is washed out, or lighting looks flat and wrong

Symptom: the scene is too bright, and shading has less contrast than it should.
Cause: an albedo texture created with _UNORM instead of _SRGB. The sRGB-encoded bytes are being multiplied by light as though they were linear reflectances — Lesson 3.9 measured this as up to 43% of the light in the wrong place.
Fix: _SRGB for anything that is a colour. And _UNORM for anything that is a number — normal maps, roughness, masks — where decoding would corrupt data that was never encoded.

The texture is upside down

Symptom: the image is mirrored vertically on the surface.
Cause: one flip too many or too few between the file (top row first), the OBJ (v upwards), and the sampler (v downwards).
Fix: §3.5's probe is the way to find out which — read the four corners back and compare with the file. Fixing it by flipping something and seeing whether it looks better is how a codebase ends up with two flips that cancel.

A sheared or garbled texture

Symptom: the image is recognisable but skewed diagonally.
Cause: pixels_per_row given a byte pitch instead of a pixel count in SDL_GPUTextureTransferInfo.
Fix: pixels, not bytes. This is the third time this exact bug has appeared in this course — Lesson 1.5's framebuffer pitch, Lesson 4.5's vertex pitch, and now here — which is worth taking as a general warning about any field named for a row.

"Texture format is not supported" at startup

Symptom: SDL_CreateGPUTexture fails for a depth format that works on your other machine.
Cause: only D16_UNORM is guaranteed. This lesson's development machine does not support D24_UNORM at all.
Fix: SDL_GPUTextureSupportsFormat, in preference order, with a guaranteed fallback.

8Exercises

  1. 4.7.1 — Move the near plane and watch the table change. §3.2's formula has n in the denominator, so the near plane costs precision everywhere. Re-run the depth measurement with near at 0.1, 0.3 and 1.0 and confirm the 10× and 3× you predict.

    Hint: k_near in verify_47.cpp, and remember k_depth_a depends on it. The lesson to take away is a practical one: pushing the near plane out is the cheapest depth-precision fix there is, and it is usually available — most scenes have nothing within a metre of the camera.

  2. 4.7.2 — Make z-fighting happen on purpose. Add a second torus to the scene, offset from the first by a distance you compute from §3.2 to be just under one depth code at its distance. Predict what you will see before you build.

    Hint: the prediction is not "flicker" — a still camera gives a stable wrong answer. You need motion, and the interesting part is that the pattern of which surface wins is a map of the depth buffer's quantisation.

  3. 4.7.3 — An albedo in the wrong space. Change the texture to _UNORM and compare the two renders per pixel, as Lesson 3.9 compared its blends. Report the largest error as a fraction of full range.

    Hint: §E of the harness already renders both; the addition is a difference image and a maximum. Expect the largest errors in the midtones, not at the extremes — sRGB and linear agree at 0 and at 1 by construction.

  4. 4.7.4 — Reversed-Z, properly. §3.4 measured it with a probe. Do it in the engine: flip the projection's depth remap, the compare op, and the clear value, and confirm the scene is unchanged while the precision is not.

    Hint: the cleanest flip is in engine::perspective itself — swap the near and far terms — so the shader never learns about it. Check SDL_GPUTextureSupportsFormat for D32_FLOAT first and fall back to the ordinary mapping if it is absent, because reversed-Z on a UNORM format buys nothing (§3.4) and complicates everything.

  5. 4.7.5 — Mipmaps. The demo minifies badly — tile the texture with R and the surface boils. Generate a mip chain, set num_levels and mipmap_mode, and make it stop.

    Hint: SDL_GenerateMipmapsForGPUTexture does the generation; the texture must be created with num_levels > 1 and the COLOR_TARGET usage bit as well as SAMPLER. Compare against Lesson 3.9's shimmering floor, which is the same phenomenon and the reason that lesson named mipmaps and deferred them.

9Recap & Next

  • Depth is an attachment plus three pipeline fields, and both are needed. Lesson 3.1 modelled the split two modules early and the port is a rename, exactly as it promised.
  • Depth precision falls off as the square of distance, because the perspective divide puts 1/d into the stored value. Seventy per cent of our range is spent in the first metre; D16 cannot separate 41 cm at ninety metres. The formula is Δd = (f−n)·d²/(f·n·N) and the measurement matches it to a few per cent.
  • Reversed-Z is 180× on a float format and nothing on a UNORM one, and knowing which is which follows from knowing where a float keeps its precision.
  • Ask for a depth format; do not assume one. Only D16 is guaranteed, and this machine has no D24.
  • A sampler is an object, a texture is an object, and they meet at the binding. Lesson 3.9's enums were written to make the port two casts, and a test has been guarding that claim since Module 4 opened.
  • _SRGB is one enum and it decides whether your lighting is correct — decoding per read, for free, before the filter.

The engine now has every piece of a renderer: geometry on the device, a camera that moves, materials with an image on them, and hidden-surface removal. What it does not have is the scene — Module 3's demo, with its floor, its models, its lighting controls and its HUD, still runs entirely on the CPU beside all of this.

Next: 4.8 — Porting the Module-3 Scene. The payoff, and it should be undramatic: the same scene, the same maths, a different API. Every convention that made this possible was fixed deliberately in Module 2 and Module 3 — the NDC parity, the depth range, the winding, the column-major matrices, the sampler enums — and the honest test of whether that was worth it is how little of Module 3 has to change now.

  • Emil Persson, "Creating Vast Game Worlds" (SIGGRAPH 2012). Reversed-Z and logarithmic depth at planetary scale — where §3.2's 1/d² stops being an inconvenience and becomes the design constraint.
