Module 8 — Physics · Lesson 8.2

Forces, Gravity, and Linear Rigid Bodies

Lesson 8.1 taught this engine to advance a state through time, and left it unable to tell a crate from a pebble. You hand integrate an acceleration and it believes you — which is enough to drop a cube and enough for nothing else, because an acceleration cannot be added up and does not know what anything weighs. Four systems that each want to push the same body will overwrite one another, and a thruster that shoves a pebble will shove a truck exactly as hard. The fix is one equation old enough to be furniture, F = ma, and the interesting part is everything the engine has to decide to make it usable: an accumulator rather than a setter, because forces add and Newton's second law is linear in F; the reciprocal of the mass rather than the mass, because the common case in a real scene is immovable and that is a clean exact zero where infinity is a NaN waiting to happen; and an impulse as a separate entry point, because a jump written as a force for one step is 23× higher at 30 Hz than at 144 while the same jump written as an impulse varies by 6%. Then two questions that look like pedantry until they cost a week. Where “one unit is one metre” stops being a convention: 9.81 is in metres per second squared, a world modelled at scale s runs every duration in it by √s, and that single square root is why miniatures look like miniatures, why film crews overcrank by 2.83× for a 1/8 model, and why “the jumping feels floaty” is a measurement rather than an opinion. And where a scaled parent quietly stops meaning it: a body integrated under a parent scaled by 2 falls at 2 g, a body merely turned under a non-uniformly scaled one falls at 1.5811 g, 63.435° off vertical, and a spinning parent passes every test a matrix is capable of answering while putting a force-free body 3.7963 m from the straight line it was obliged to travel. Along the way: why a sweep of a million masses finds 16% of them fail a round trip that seven hand-picked ones all passed, and what that costs; why damping and drag sit next to each other in the header with different names and produce identical pictures for three masses 1000× apart until one of them does not; and a budget in which the honest arrangement of gravity turns out to cost a float divide worth 15% of the whole update, more than 8.1's entire integrator step.

1. The problem: an engine that cannot tell a crate from a pebble

Lesson 8.1 ended with a working stepper and a deliberately small type. A motion is a position and a velocity, and integrate advances one under an acceleration you supply. That is genuinely the heart of a physics engine, and everything in this module runs through it. It is also, right now, unusable for anything but a demo — and the reason is not that it is missing features. It is that the interface is wrong in two specific ways, and both of them are interesting.

An acceleration cannot be added up. Picture a crate in a real scene for one frame. Gravity wants to pull it down. A conveyor belt under it wants to drag it sideways. Wind from a designer-placed volume wants to push it east. A player has just shot it. Air resistance wants to slow it. Each of those is a separate system that knows nothing whatever about the others, written by different people, running in whatever order the frame happens to run them. Every one of them wants to tell the crate what its acceleration is.

With a single acceleration field, the last one to write it wins and the rest of the physics silently does not happen. You will not get a crash, or a warning, or a wrong-looking number in a debugger — you will get a crate that ignores gravity on the frames the wind system happened to run last. That is a bug with no symptom except “it feels wrong sometimes”, which is the worst kind there is.

And an acceleration does not know about mass. Put the same thruster on a pebble and on a truck and, in 8.1's world, both accelerate identically, because the only thing the stepper was told is how fast the velocity should change. That is not a small inaccuracy; it is the absence of the entire concept a player is using when they decide whether something looks heavy.

You might reasonably propose to fix the second one at the call site — have the wind system pass F/m instead of F — and that proposal is exactly what makes the first problem visible. The division can only happen once, after everything has been added, or it is not the same arithmetic. So the two problems have one answer, and it is the oldest equation in the subject.

Sum the forces. Divide by the mass. Once. Everything in this lesson is a consequence of taking that sentence literally.

And then two questions that sound like pedantry and are not. 9.81 is a number in metres per second squared, and conventions.html §3 has said “one unit is one metre” since Module 2, back when nothing in the engine could tell the difference. This is the lesson where something can. §8 derives what a mis-scaled world actually does — it is a square root, and it is why miniatures look like miniatures — and §9 asks where a body's position lives, which turns out to be a restriction rather than a feature and to have three separate ways of going wrong.

2. Building intuition: a shove is not an acceleration

Stand behind a shopping trolley and push it. Push exactly as hard on an empty one and on one full of bricks. What is the same in those two pushes, and what is different?

What is the same is you. Your arms are doing the same thing; the sensation in your shoulders is the same; if there were a scale between your hands and the handle it would read the same number. What is different is everything that happens next. The empty trolley leaps away; the full one grudgingly creeps. The quantity that was the same is the force, and the quantity that differed is the acceleration, and the thing that converted one into the other is the mass.

That is genuinely all Newton's second law says, and stated this way it is almost a tautology: we define force as the thing that is the same in the two pushes. What makes it a law rather than a definition is the second half — that forces add. If a friend pushes the trolley from the side while you push from behind, the trolley does not choose one of you. It goes in the direction of the sum of the two pushes, and the sum is the ordinary vector sum you would draw with a ruler. Nothing about that is obvious in advance; a universe in which the second push had half its effect while the first was already acting would be perfectly imaginable, and would make physics engines far harder to write.

It is worth sitting with how much the engine gets for free from that one property. Because forces add, gravity does not need to know that wind exists. Because forces add, the order of the four systems above does not matter. Because forces add, there is no interface to implement, no registry to sign up for, no visitor to write — the crate has a vec3, and everything that wants to push it does +=. An engine's simplest subsystems are the ones whose physics is linear, and this is the cleanest example in the whole course.

Now the other half of the picture, and it is the one people find genuinely strange the first time. Drop a hammer and a feather. On Earth the hammer wins, which everybody knows; on the Moon, famously, they land together, which David Scott demonstrated on camera in 1971 for exactly this reason. If mass converts force into acceleration, and the hammer has more mass, why does more mass not mean less acceleration under gravity?

Because gravity is the one force that scales with the mass it is pulling on. The hammer is a hundred times heavier, so gravity pulls on it a hundred times harder, so the two hundreds cancel and every object falls at the same rate. It is the only force in this lesson with that property, and it means gravity is simultaneously the most familiar force and the least representative one — the intuition you have built over a lifetime of watching things fall is intuition about the one case where mass does not matter. §5 makes that a design decision and §6 shows what happens when a second force turns up and the cancellation stops.

3. F = ma, and the accumulator that makes it usable

Write the law down, in the direction the engine actually needs it:

\[ \mathbf{F} = m\,\mathbf{a} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \mathbf{a} = \frac{\mathbf{F}}{m} = \mathbf{F}\cdot\frac{1}{m} \]

Force equals mass times acceleration; so acceleration is the total force divided by the mass, or — as the engine will actually spell it — the total force multiplied by the reciprocal of the mass.

The total force. That word is doing all the work, and it is what turns one equation into a piece of architecture. A body carries a vec3 that starts each step at zero; every system that wants to push adds to it; and at the moment the step runs, that vector is divided by the mass exactly once. Nothing else in the design has to exist.

3.1 A worked sum, with four real systems

Take a 2 kg body and let four systems have their say, in whatever order the frame runs them. Gravity contributes its weight, m·g — 2 kg at 9.81 m/s² is 19.62 N, downward. A thruster pushes 14 N along +x. A wind volume contributes (−4.5, 1.25, 3.0) N. A crude drag model contributes (−2.25, 0.5, −1.5) N. Add them componentwise:

\[ \mathbf{F} = (0, -19.62, 0) + (14, 0, 0) + (-4.5, 1.25, 3) + (-2.25, 0.5, -1.5) = (7.25,\; -17.87,\; 1.5)\ \text{N} \]

and divide once by 2 kg:

\[ \mathbf{a} = (7.25,\; -17.87,\; 1.5) \times 0.5 = (3.625,\; -8.935,\; 0.75)\ \text{m/s}^2 \]

The four forces sum to 7.25 newtons east, 17.87 down and 1.5 forward; dividing by the two-kilogram mass gives an acceleration of 3.625, −8.935 and 0.75 metres per second squared.

Notice the y component: −8.935, not −9.81. The wind and the drag both had a small upward contribution, and the body is now falling slightly slower than gravity alone would have it. That number is the one thing you could not have got from a design where each system sets the acceleration, and it is what the accumulator buys.

Figure 1 draws the same four forces tip to tail, at one scale on both axes, with the sum spanning from the body to the final tip. It is worth pausing on the right-hand half, because the failure mode is so quiet. Replace the four += with four = and the harness reports F = (14.000, 0.000, 0.000) N: gravity, wind and drag are simply gone. No error, no warning, no nan. Just a body that this frame has decided is in space.

3.2 Why the accumulator is cleared after the step, not before

A force is an input to a step, not a property of a body. Next frame every one of those systems will speak again; what the crate “has” is a velocity and a position, and what it was handed was a force. So the accumulator has to be emptied once per step, and the single most common bug in a first physics engine is forgetting — which presents as objects that accelerate away forever after being touched once, because a thruster fired for one frame keeps firing for the rest of the level.

Less obvious is when. Clearing at the start of the step is the arrangement that first occurs to you and it is wrong in a way you will not notice for months: every tool that wants to ask “what was pushing on this body?” then reads zero. A debug overlay drawing force arrows draws nothing. An assertion checking that nothing exceeded a sane magnitude has nothing to check. Lesson 8.10's solver, which reads what the contact generator asked for, reads an empty vector. step therefore clears the accumulator last, after the integration it fed, so that anything looking at a body between two steps sees the forces that produced the state it is looking at.

3.3 The measurement: a is proportional to 1/m, exactly

Section A of the harness pushes three bodies with the same 50 N and steps them once at 60 Hz: 

    mass      inv_mass        a       v after 1 step
      1.0 kg  1.000000   50.0000      0.833333 m/s
     10.0 kg  0.100000    5.0000      0.083333 m/s
   1000.0 kg  0.001000    0.0500      0.000833 m/s

  A.1  m*a recovers the force at every mass:
           1.0 kg -> m*a =    50.0000 N   (F = 50 N)
          10.0 kg -> m*a =    50.0000 N   (F = 50 N)
        1000.0 kg -> m*a =    50.0000 N   (F = 50 N)

  A.2  v(1 kg) / v(1000 kg) = 999.9999  (expect 1000)

  A.3  CONTROL same mass, same push: identical  yes

A.1 is the check worth having: multiplying the acceleration back by the mass must return the force you put in, at every mass, and it does. A.2 is the same statement as a ratio, and it reads 999.9999 rather than 1000 for the reason §5 is about — 1/1000 is not exactly representable in float, so neither is the number you get back by dividing into it. That is a one-part-in-ten-million discrepancy and it is not nothing; hold on to it.

3.4 …and the one way in which forces do not commute

Vector addition is commutative and associative. Floating-point addition is commutative and not associative, and since an accumulator is a running sum, the order in which systems call add_force can in principle change the answer. Section B tries all 24 orderings of four forces chosen to be awkward: 

  B.2  same four forces, 24 orders, x component only
         [1e6, -1e6, 0.125, 0.0037] N
    24 orders   lo 0.125000000   hi 0.128700003
    spread 3.7000e-03 = 2.9% of the answer
    the smallest of the four forces is 3.7000e-03

  B.3  CONTROL exactly representable, partial sums exact
         [4, 2, 1, 0.5] N
    24 orders   lo 7.500000000   hi 7.500000000
    spread 0.0000e+00 = 0.0% of the answer

Read the two numbers in B.2 together: the spread across orderings is 3.7000e−03, and the smallest of the four forces is 3.7000e−03. They are not similar, they are the same number. What happened is not a gradual loss of precision; it is that in some orderings the fourth force was added to a partial sum of a million, where one ulp is 0.0625, and it vanished completely. Whether a whole force applies at all came down to which system ran first.

B.3 is the control, and it is written against the second half of 7.8's rule — ask what the check would say if the thing were completely fine. Four forces that are exact powers of two have exact partial sums in every order, and the spread is exactly zero. So B.2's spread is a fact about those particular values and not a fact about addition, which is the distinction that makes it actionable.

Two honest conclusions, and they point in different directions. For a normal game this is a non-issue: real forces on a real body are within a few orders of magnitude of each other and the spread is a last-bit affair. For a deterministic game — a replay, a lockstep multiplayer session, a golden-image test — it is fatal, because bit-identical is the requirement and a differing last bit compounds. The mitigation is not a smarter sum; it is that the accumulator is a field written by systems in a fixed order fixed by the code, not a list summed in arrival order. Our design already has that property, and it is worth knowing that it is a property rather than an accident.

4. Inverse mass, and the question it does not answer

Every physics engine you will ever read stores 1/m rather than m, and the reason usually given — “it turns a divide into a multiply” — is the least of it. A divide is about four times a multiply on modern hardware and it happens once per body per step; nobody restructures a public API for that. There are three real reasons, and they get better as they go.

4.1 Immovable is representable, and it is the common case

Count the bodies in a real level. The floor is immovable. So is every wall, every staircase, every rock, every piece of static geometry an artist placed — which is to say, most of the bodies. Their mass is not large. It is infinite, in exactly the sense that no force you can apply will accelerate them, and inv_mass = 0 says so precisely, in a float, with no sentinel value and no branch anywhere.

Store the mass instead and you need either a magic value or a parallel flag. The magic value is HUGE_VALF, and it works right up until two of them meet: 

  F.2  two immovable bodies:
       mass_of  = inf and inf
       m_a - m_b        = nan   (NaN: yes)
       inv_a + inv_b    = 0.000000   (the solver's term)
       1/(inv_a+inv_b)  = inf   (reduced mass)

inf − inf is NaN, and a NaN in a physics engine is not a local problem — it propagates through every subsequent operation, out through the position, into the transform, and onto the screen as an object that has disappeared. The inverse masses subtract to a clean zero.

4.2 The solver wants this quantity, and not mass

This is the reason that will look like foresight in eight lessons' time. Every contact in 8.9 and every joint in 8.11 computes an effective mass for the pair, and it is built from the sum of the inverse masses:

\[ m_{\text{eff}} = \frac{1}{\frac{1}{m_a} + \frac{1}{m_b}} = \frac{m_a m_b}{m_a + m_b} \]

The effective mass of a colliding pair is one over the sum of their inverse masses, which is also the product of the masses over their sum.

That quantity is the reduced mass, and it has a physical meaning: it is the mass a single body would need to have for the pair's relative motion to look the same. Written in terms of masses it needs a special case at every infinity; written in terms of inverse masses it is 1/(w_a + w_b) and a fixed body simply contributes nothing to the sum, which gives exactly the right answer — a crate bouncing off a wall behaves as though only the crate has a mass, which is what happens. The storage is chosen to match the arithmetic the hot loop will actually perform, and the hot loop has not been written yet.

4.3 The degenerate case moves somewhere harmless

With mass stored, mass = 0 is a divide by zero on the hot path. With the reciprocal stored, a massless body is inv_mass = ∞: still nonsense, but nonsense that does not divide, and nonsense that can be refused at the one place it can be created. 

  F.3  set_mass(0) refused   set_mass(-2) refused
       set_mass(inf) accepted
       inv_mass after inf = 0.0

set_mass is written as if (!(kilograms > 0.0f)) rather than if (kilograms <= 0.0f), and that is the one place in the file where the negated comparison is load-bearing: NaN <= 0 is false, so the second form lets a NaN mass straight through, while !(NaN > 0) is true and catches it. A negative mass is refused too, and not out of fastidiousness — a body with negative mass accelerates towards a push, which breaks assumptions the solver will make from 8.9 onward in ways that are essentially impossible to trace back to here.

4.4 …and now the question inverse mass does not answer

“Can a force move it?” is answered by inv_mass, completely. There is a second question that looks like the same one and is not: “does it move at all?”

A lift is immovable by forces — a crate landing on it must not push it down, however heavy the crate is — and it also moves, because that is its entire job. A floor is immovable by forces and does not move. Both have inv_mass = 0 and they are not the same kind of object, so the bottom half of figure 2 is a 2×2 rather than a switch, and three of its four cells are the three values of body_kind:

does not movemoves
a force cannot move it fixed — the ground, a wall kinematic — a lift, a door
a force can move it nothing is this dynamic — a crate, a ragdoll

Conflate the two and you get the two classic bugs, one from each direction. Make the lift dynamic and very heavy, and it sags under load and — as we are about to measure — drifts downward under gravity anyway. Make it static and teleport it every frame, and anything standing on it is left behind, because a body with no velocity imparts none to whatever it is carrying.

A C++ note in passing, because the enumerator name is odd. Every other engine calls the first of those static, and static is a keyword — it cannot be an enumerator even inside an enum class, where the name is otherwise scoped and cannot collide with anything. Ours is fixed, and the doc comment says the other word so that somebody searching for it lands in the right place.

4.5 “Just make the floor very heavy”

This is the proposal that every engine receives, and it deserves a measurement rather than an argument. Section F drops a dynamic body of one million kilograms beside a ten-gram pebble for one second: 

  F.4  CONTROL a 1,000,000 kg DYNAMIC 'floor', 1 s:
       y = -4.986749 m   a 10 g pebble: y = -4.986749 m

  F.1  fixed body, 10 s of gravity + 10 kN:
       position 0.0  velocity 0.0   moved: no

The two numbers are identical to the last decimal place, and figure 3 draws them as two arrows of exactly the same length. A hundred million times the mass buys precisely nothing, because gravity does not ask what anything weighs — which is §2's hammer and feather arriving as a line in a test log. Making something heavy makes it hard to push, and the floor's problem was never that somebody was pushing it.

inv_mass = 0 is not a very large number. It is a different kind of answer, and F.1 is what it buys: ten seconds of gravity plus a ten-kilonewton shove, and the position and velocity are still exactly zero. Not “small”. Zero, because x · 0 is zero and there is nothing for the arithmetic to round.

5. Gravity, and the round trip that is not exact

Gravity is a force like any other, and it is the one force in this lesson that is proportional to the mass it acts on:

\[ \mathbf{F}_{\text{gravity}} = m\,\mathbf{g} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \mathbf{a} = \frac{m\,\mathbf{g}}{m} = \mathbf{g} \]

The force of gravity is the mass times g, so the acceleration it produces is g, with the mass cancelling out entirely.

This is §2's hammer and feather written down, and it is the whole of why Galileo needed a tower. It also presents the engine with a small, genuinely interesting decision, because there are two correct places to put it.

5.1 Two arrangements of the same physics

Weight is a force, so put it in the accumulator. This is the honest reading of the physics and it is what the first draft of this engine did. A kitchen scale under a crate reads a force; the crate's weight is one of the things pushing on it; so step adds m·g·gravity_scale alongside whatever the caller contributed, and divides the sum by the mass at the end like everything else. One code path, and `force` contains everything acting on the body.

Or: the mass was always going to cancel, so decline to compute it. Divide the accumulator by the mass, then add g·gravity_scale to the acceleration that comes out. This is what Box2D does, in the same place, for the same reason.

Figure 4 puts the two side by side as the three steps each one actually performs. Both are correct. They differ in two things, and it took a measurement to find out which of the two mattered.

5.2 The cost: a divide the body does not have the number for

The body stores 1/m, for all three of §4's reasons. The weight needs m. So the first arrangement contains, on the hot path, once per body per step, a floating-point divide — gravity * (gravity_scale / inv_mass) — that exists solely to undo the storage decision two lines later.

§11 times it. The two arrangements are otherwise identical loops over the same table, timed alternately one rep each so that both see the same thermal state, and the ratio across three runs of the binary is 0.845, 0.848, 0.847. The divide is 15% of the entire per-body update — which is more than 8.1 measured for a whole semi-implicit Euler step (0.963 ns). A line that computes a number in order to cancel it out costs more than the physics.

5.3 …and the exactness, which was the surprise

The other difference is whether it is even the same answer. The first arrangement computes (g · (1/w)) · w where w is the stored inverse mass, which involves a reciprocal, a multiply and a multiply — three roundings where the second arrangement has none, because g goes in untouched.

The first version of this section tested seven hand-picked masses — 0.001, 0.1, 1, 7, 100, 1000 and 106 kg — and every single one recovered −9.810000420 exactly, which is the correctly-rounded float value of −9.81. That looked like a proof and was nothing of the kind, because those are the masses a person picks, and a person picks powers of ten. So the harness sweeps a million of them instead, log-uniform from one gram to a thousand tonnes: 

  C.1  a = (g / inv_mass) * inv_mass, against g = -9.81
       1000001 masses from 1 g to 1000 t
       inexact 159937 (15.9937%)   worst 9.537e-07 at 0.001 kg
       one ulp of g is 9.537e-07, so that is 1.00 ulp

Sixteen per cent of masses do not survive the round trip, and the seven that were tried all did. The first failing mass is not exotic either — the sweep reaches it at 1.000145 kg. This is the clearest example in the course so far of test data chosen by a human being agreeing with the code by construction.

5.4 Does one ulp matter? Two answers, and they disagree

A single ulp of an acceleration sounds like nothing, so the harness drops that 1.000145 kg body beside a 1 kg one and watches the velocities for ten seconds. Through the force route: 

  C.2  the FORCE route: 1 kg against 1.000145 kg
      steps        v(1 kg)        v(odd)     apart
          1      -0.163500     -0.163500  1.490e-08
         60      -9.809996     -9.809995  9.537e-07
        600     -98.099884    -98.099884  0.000e+00
       one ulp of v after one step is 1.490e-08

  C.3  the ACCELERATION route, through body_world
      steps        v(1 kg)        v(odd)     apart
          1      -0.163500     -0.163500  0.000e+00
         60      -9.809996     -9.809996  0.000e+00
        600     -98.099884    -98.099884  0.000e+00

  C.4  CONTROL same mass, force route: identical  yes

Read the apart column carefully, because it does something more interesting than growing. After one step the two bodies differ by 1.490e−08, which is exactly one ulp of the velocity at that magnitude. After sixty steps they differ by one ulp of the larger velocity. After six hundred they are identical again. The error does not accumulate; it wanders, because each accumulation rounds and the rounding sometimes absorbs it and sometimes does not.

So: does it matter? For a falling crate, no, and it is important to say so plainly rather than manufacture alarm — you could not measure this difference with any instrument in the game. For anything that requires bit-identical results it is fatal, and the list of those is longer than it looks: a replay that has to play back the same way, a lockstep multiplayer session where two machines must not diverge, and the golden-image tests this course has been running since Module 4. Two identical crates of different mass must fall identically, and through the force route they do not.

C.4 is the control that makes any of that mean anything: the same mass through the same route, twice, must be identical, and is — so C.2's divergence is a fact about the mass rather than about the code path being nondeterministic.

Two findings, one cheaper and one more correct, both pointing the same way. The engine adds gravity as an acceleration, after the division. What that costs is worth naming rather than hiding, and §12 names it: rigid_body::force no longer contains the body's weight, so a tool drawing force arrows draws every push on a body except the one that is always there. That is a one-line problem with a one-line answer (mass_of(b) * world.gravity()) and it is the right side of the trade.

5.5 A sanity check against the closed form

Neither route should be graded against the other, so C.5 puts both against the answer from first-year mechanics. Ten seconds of free fall from rest is ½gt² = 490.5 m, and the simulation reports 491.318207 m. The difference is 0.8175 m, and it is not this lesson's — it is 8.1's, the ½·a·h·t overshoot that semi-implicit Euler has under constant acceleration. Plug the numbers in: 0.5 × 9.81 × (1/60) × 10 = 0.8175. Exactly.

6. Damping is not drag

Every engine ships a per-body knob that bleeds off speed, and every engine gets asked why it does not behave like air. The answer is a genuinely sharp distinction, and this engine puts the two functions next to each other in the header so it is one line of reading rather than an afternoon.

6.1 The two models, written down

Velocity damping multiplies the velocity by a factor every step. 8.1 derived the correct form of it — the exact solution of dv/dt = −kv, which is v ×= exp(−kh), frame-rate independent and incapable of reversing the velocity the way v ×= (1 − kh) does past kh = 1. Under gravity it settles where the losses balance the gain:

\[ g = k\,v_{\infty} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad v_{\infty} = \frac{g}{k} \]

There is no mass in that equation, and there never was one, because k was defined as acting on the velocity directly.

A drag force is a force: F = −b·v, pushed through the accumulator like anything else, which means step divides it by the mass on the way past. Its balance is between two forces:

\[ m\,g = b\,v_{\infty} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad v_{\infty} = \frac{m\,g}{b} \]

Under velocity damping the terminal speed is g over k, with no mass in it. Under a drag force it is mass times g over b, directly proportional to the mass.

That is the entire difference, and it is the difference between a feather and a cannonball. A cannonball is not more aerodynamic than a feather; it has more mass for the same air to work against, so the air has proportionally less effect, so it falls faster.

6.2 The measurement, and a prediction that was wrong on purpose

  DAMPING  v *= exp(-k h),  k = 0.5 /s

       0.10 kg    19.5383 m/s  19.6200    19.5384
       1.00 kg    19.5383 m/s  19.6200    19.5384
     100.00 kg    19.5383 m/s  19.6200    19.5384

A thousandfold range of mass produces one number under damping and a thousandfold range under drag, which is figure 5 in a sentence. But look at the g/k column: it says 19.6200 and the simulation says 19.5383, a 0.42% disagreement that is far too consistent to be noise. A harness that printed only the continuous formula would have sent somebody looking for a bug in apply_drag.

There is no bug. g/k is the terminal speed of the continuous system, and we are not running the continuous system. Our step applies gravity and then damps, so the velocity the loop settles on is the fixed point of that particular pair of lines:

\[ (v + g h)\,e^{-kh} = v \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad v_{\infty}^{\text{discrete}} = \frac{g\,h\,e^{-kh}}{1 - e^{-kh}} \]

At 60 Hz with k = 0.5 that evaluates to 19.5384, against a measured 19.5383. It tends to g/k as h → 0, which is the sense in which the continuous formula is right, and it is 0.42% below it at the step size we actually run. Print the discrete prediction next to the continuous one whenever they differ by more than the thing you are trying to measure, or the constant offset will be read as an error.

The drag rows carry their own honesty: each is run for seven time constants, and the durations differ by a factor of a thousand, because τ = m/b is 0.2 s for the light body and 200 s for the heavy one. That is the other half of the finding — a heavy body does not merely end up faster, it takes proportionally longer to get there — and it is why the drag panel of figure 5 has one curve levelling off almost immediately and one still climbing off the top.

The control is D.3, and it is written against the “what if everything is fine” question: with neither damping nor drag there should be no terminal speed at all, and after sixty seconds the body is doing 588.62 m/s and still accelerating. g·t is 588.60. If that row had reported something finite and stable, every row above it would have been suspect.

6.3 So why does the engine ship the wrong one?

Because nine times in ten, what gameplay wants is not air. It wants “bleed off speed so this feels controllable”, or “stop this thing vibrating”, and those are stability and feel controls with no physical content whatsoever. A designer tuning a damping of 0.4 on a camera boom is not modelling anything and should not have to think about the boom's mass.

So the field exists, and it is called damping rather than drag, and the two terminal-speed functions sit adjacent in the header with their formulas in the doc comments. When you want the physics, it is one line at the call site: 

add_force(b, b.state.velocity * -drag_coefficient);

…and the mass does its job, because that line goes into the accumulator and the accumulator gets divided.

7. An impulse has no h in it

A force acts over time. Integrate one over the interval it acts for and you get an impulse, which is a change in momentum:

\[ \mathbf{J} = \int \mathbf{F}\,dt = \Delta(m\mathbf{v}) = m\,\Delta\mathbf{v} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \Delta\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{J}\cdot\frac{1}{m} \]

An impulse is a force integrated over time, which equals the change in momentum; so the change in velocity it produces is the impulse times the inverse mass.

Read the right-hand side and notice what is absent: there is no h anywhere. That absence is the entire reason add_impulse is a separate entry point rather than a convenience wrapper.

7.1 Why a jump is not a force

Some things really do act over a duration you know: a rocket burns for three seconds, a conveyor belt pushes continuously, a magnet pulls while you are inside it. Those are forces, and the accumulator is the right home for them.

Other things act over a duration nobody knows, and shorter than one step. A bat hits a ball. A bullet lands. A character jumps. The contact between bat and ball lasts perhaps a millisecond; at 60 Hz that is a sixteenth of a step, and the question “what force was it?” has no useful answer. What is well-defined, and what the gameplay programmer actually knows, is the total change in momentum.

Write the jump as a force anyway — add_force(b, up * F) for one step, which is the natural-looking thing to type — and the velocity you get is F·h/m. There is an h in it. Tune F until the jump feels right on your 60 Hz monitor, ship it, and every player on a different refresh rate gets a different game.

7.2 The measurement: four monitors, one jump

A 70 kg character wants to reach 1.00 m. That needs a takeoff speed of √(2gH) = 4.4294 m/s, so an impulse of J = m·v₀ = 310.06 N·s, or — tuned at 60 Hz — a one-step force of J/h = 18,604 N. Both are then run at four common refresh rates:

     120 Hz      0.9816 m       0.2408 m

That is not a subtle regression — it is a different game, and it is twenty-three times over. The scaling is exactly what the algebra said: Δv ∝ h and height goes as v², so height goes as h², and halving the frame time quarters the jump. 0.9632 ÷ 4 = 0.2408, which is the 120 Hz row to the digit.

7.3 The impulse column is not flat either, and that is somebody else's fault

6.16% is small next to 2204%, but it is not zero, and a lesson that waved at it would be doing the thing this course exists not to do. Where does it come from? 

  E.3  the impulse column is not flat, and the residue is
       8.1's: peak = v0^2/2g - v0*h/2
     rate    predicted      measured
      30 Hz    0.926176 m    0.927527 m

     120 Hz    0.981544 m    0.981596 m
     144 Hz    0.984620 m    0.984625 m

It is 8.1's, not this lesson's. Semi-implicit Euler subtracts g·h from the velocity before moving the position, so the body spends each step travelling slightly slower than the continuous solution would have it, and the peak falls short by exactly v₀h/2. Predicted and measured agree to four decimal places at every rate. The impulse removed the first-order rate dependence and left the second-order one, which is a term you can write down, correct for if you care, and shrink by raising the rate.

E.4 is the control, and it took two attempts. The idea is to apply the same total impulse as a force spread over however many whole steps fit, which should recover the impulse behaviour because that is what an impulse is. The first version held the force for round(rate/60) steps and reported a third bug — at 144 Hz, two steps is 13.9 ms rather than 16.7, so the control was measuring how evenly 60 divides into the refresh rate. With the force rescaled to the duration it is actually held for, the spread drops to the same order as the impulse column's. A control with a bug in it does not fail loudly; it produces a third number and invites you to explain it.

Every contact response in 8.9 and every constraint in 8.10 is an impulse, and this is why. A collision does not last a step; pretending it does makes the bounce depend on the frame rate, which is the same bug as the jump, arriving somewhere much harder to see.

8. Units: the square root under every miniature

k_gravity is 9.81f. That number is in metres per second squared, and it is the first line of code in this course for which the unit is not a matter of taste. conventions.html §3 has said “one unit is one metre” since Module 2, when nothing in the engine could have told the difference — a rasterizer does not care what a unit means, and neither does a shadow map or a BRDF. A constant does.

So what actually happens if your art is authored at some other scale? Not “things look wrong”: something specific and derivable.

8.1 The derivation, in two lines

Start from the only equation involved:

\[ d = \tfrac{1}{2} g t^{2} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad t = \sqrt{\frac{2d}{g}} \]

Now multiply every length in the world by s and leave g alone. The d under the square root scales by s, so the time scales by √s:

\[ t' = \sqrt{\frac{2\,(s\,d)}{g}} = \sqrt{s}\;\sqrt{\frac{2d}{g}} = \sqrt{s}\;t \]

Scaling every length by s multiplies every duration by the square root of s, because the time to fall a distance goes as the square root of that distance.

That is the whole law, and it is not restricted to free fall: any motion driven by a fixed acceleration has time going as the square root of distance, so everything in a rescaled world is slow or fast by the same √s. Splashes, tumbling, collapse, the arc of a thrown object, the settling of a stack. 

  G.2  a world where 1 unit depicts s metres, g left alone

      s     fall of a 1.8 m figure   ratio   sqrt(s)
   0.25             1.211565 s   2.0000  2.0000
   0.50             0.856706 s   1.4142  1.4142
   1.00             0.605783 s   1.0000  1.0000
   2.00             0.428353 s   0.7071  0.7071
   4.00             0.302891 s   0.5000  0.5000

Figure 7 plots those five measured points against the curve 1/√s, and they sit on it. Read the table the way a developer would hit it: a world modelled twice as large as it depicts (a “1.8 m” character built 3.6 units tall, which is s = 0.5) takes 1.4142× too long for everything. A world modelled half as large is 1.4142× too fast.

8.2 The symptom names the bug

“Everything feels floaty” and “everything feels like a toy” are not vague aesthetic complaints. They are the two directions of this square root, and once you know the law you can read the scale error off a playtest:

What it feels likeWhat it isThe number
floaty, moon-like, slow to land the world is modelled bigger than it depicts s < 1, times ×1/√s
snappy, twitchy, toy-like the world is modelled smaller than it depicts s > 1, times ×1/√s

Film has known this for a century and pays for it in the other currency. A model ship at 1/8 scale sits in water pulled by the same 9.81 m/s² the real one is, so every wave, every roll and every collapse happens √8 = 2.8284 times too fast — which an audience reads instantly as “that is a toy” without being able to say why. The fix is to overcrank the camera by exactly that factor, shooting at 67.9 fps and playing back at 24, which slows the model's world by √8 and puts it back on the right clock. Same equation; you simply cannot change g on a soundstage, so you change the frame rate instead.

8.3 “Just multiply g” — which works, and is not the fix

The reflex when things feel floaty is to raise gravity until they do not, and this section is going to defend that reflex further than you might expect before taking it away.

It works. Exactly. Scaling g by 1/s restores the fall time to the last digit the simulation can produce, and G.4 measures it by actually running the integrator at each scale rather than evaluating a formula in which s cancels algebraically:

   1.00    9.8100      36   0.600000 s  +0.0000 s
   2.00    4.9050      36   0.600000 s  +0.0000 s

Thirty-six steps at every scale. Not approximately — the same integer. And that is not luck: what you have actually done by scaling g is change the unit of length, and a consistent change of units cannot change the physics. If every length, every velocity and every acceleration in your program is expressed in the same units, then working in half-metres with a gravity of 19.62 is exactly as correct as working in metres with 9.81.

8.4 …until a length turns up that did not get the memo

“Every length in your program” is doing heroic work in that sentence, and it is where the argument turns. A rescale is only consistent if it reaches everything, and physics engines are full of absolute lengths that nobody thinks of as content:

  • Contact slop — the small overlap a solver tolerates so that resting bodies do not jitter. Box2D's default is 0.005 m, and its manual is explicit that the library is tuned for objects between 0.1 and 10 metres. ⚠ VERIFY: the b2_linearSlop value and the recommended size range, against the Box2D manual's “Units” section — the principle does not depend on the constant, but the constant is quoted here and should be checked before it is repeated.
  • Sleep thresholds — how slowly a body must be moving, and for how long, before 8.10 stops simulating it.
  • Penetration recovery limits, restitution velocity cutoffs, broadphase cell sizes, character step-up heights, the radius below which a shape is considered degenerate.

None of those is authored by an artist, none is in a file anybody rescales, and every one of them is in metres. G.5 puts a single number on it: 

  G.5  what a rescaled g does NOT carry with it
       a solver tolerance of 0.005 UNITS, in a world at s =
        0.25  ->     1.25 mm of real slop
        0.50  ->     2.50 mm of real slop
        1.00  ->     5.00 mm of real slop
        2.00  ->    10.00 mm of real slop
        4.00  ->    20.00 mm of real slop

At s = 4 the solver's five-millimetre tolerance is two centimetres of visible interpenetration, and no amount of tuning g touches it. At s = 0.25 it is 1.25 mm, which sounds harmless and is worse: every stack now jitters, because the tolerance that was keeping it quiet has become smaller than the float error in the positions.

So the engine picks metres, once, and says so. Not because metres are special but because one choice has to be written down where the tolerances can be written down beside it. That is what conventions.html §3 has been for since Module 2, and 8.2 is the lesson where it stops being a convention and starts being a constant.

9. Frames: where a scaled parent stops meaning it

Here is a question with a boring-sounding answer and three interesting failure modes. When a rigid_body says its position is (3, 0, 4), in what space?

The engine has had a transform hierarchy since Module 5. An entity's transform is expressed in its parent's space, and a chain of them composes down to world space. That is the right design for a scene: a lamp bolted to a moving truck should be authored relative to the truck. So the natural-looking thing to do is put a body on a node and integrate its local transform, and the natural-looking thing is wrong in three separate ways.

9.1 Gravity is a direction, and a frame transforms directions

A parent's world matrix has a linear part M. Whatever the body does in local space, the world sees M applied to it — and because an acceleration is a direction rather than a position, it is the 3×3 linear part that acts on it and not the full 4×4. That is Lesson 2.7's w doing its job: a translation must not change an acceleration, or sliding a platform sideways would make everything on it fall harder.

So a local gravity g produces a world acceleration M·g, and inspect_frame measures exactly that, for four frames:

Figure 8 draws all four as arrows against a dashed reference pointing straight down, and the control is first this time, deliberately: a frame that is a rotation and a translation must report a gain of exactly 1 and a square of exactly 0, because a rotation cannot change a length and cannot change an angle. If H.1 had reported anything else, nothing below it would mean a thing.

H.2 is the one everybody hits. A parent scaled by 2 — an artist scaled a group node, or a prefab was authored at half size and scaled up — and the body falls at 19.62 m/s². Two g. The scene looks entirely normal; the object simply falls wrong, and it falls wrong by an amount that depends on which node it happens to be parented to.

9.2 The trap in column three

H.3 is the row worth stopping on, because it is a test passing for the wrong reason. The frame has a non-uniform scale of (2, 1, 1) — thoroughly broken — and the two numbers you would naturally check both come back clean: gain 1.0000, tilt 0.000°.

Of course they do. Gravity points along y, and the scale is on x. This particular vector never touches the broken axis. A diagnostic built only from “where does gravity end up” would sign this frame off, and the first body to be rotated in it would fall sideways.

Which is H.4, and it is the same frame: put a 45° turn between the body and the parent — a crate that is not axis-aligned, which is most crates — and gravity comes out at 1.5811 g, 63.435° off vertical. Work it through by hand, because the arithmetic is short. The turn sends local down (0, −1, 0) to (0.7071, −0.7071, 0), and then the scale stretches the x component and leaves y:

\[ \begin{bmatrix}2&0\\0&1\end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}0.7071\\-0.7071\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}1.4142\\-0.7071\end{bmatrix}, \qquad |{\cdot}| = \sqrt{2 + 0.5} = 1.5811, \qquad \arccos\!\frac{0.7071}{1.5811} = 63.435^\circ \]

A non-uniform scale does not preserve angles, so the direction that comes out depends on the direction that went in — which is precisely why gain and tilt cannot catch it and why frame_report carries out_of_square and uniform as well. out_of_square reads 0.6000 here, and it is a cosine, so it means the frame's own axes are arccos(0.6) = 53.13° away from perpendicular.

That number is Lesson 7.5's. It was built for a completely different purpose — to report what a quat could not hold when transform::rotation was narrowed from a mat3 — and it has been sitting in math/transform.hpp for three lessons with no caller other than the decomposition that produces it. It is the exact diagnostic a physics frame needs, because “does this frame preserve angles” is the whole question, and it is the same question in both cases. A number that measures shear does not care why you wanted to know.

9.3 The same thing as a fall

  H.5  1 s of falling, integrated in a frame scaled by 2
       local  y = -4.986749 units
       world  y = -9.973498 m
       a world-space body falls -4.986749 m -> ratio 2.0000

Figure 9 is H.2 as something you could watch: the integrator ran once, correctly, with g = 9.81, and produced a body that fell 9.97 metres in a second. Nothing reported anything. There was nothing to report — every line of arithmetic was right.

9.4 And the one a matrix cannot see

All of the above is about a fixed frame, and there is a failure mode underneath it that no amount of inspecting a matrix can find. Put a body on a spinning platform. Give it no forces at all — nothing, not even gravity — so that Newton's first law obliges it to travel in a straight line at constant speed. Integrate it in the platform's local space, where a body with no acceleration also travels in a straight line, and map the result out to the world: 

  H.6  no forces at all, 1 s, in a frame spinning at 2 rad/s
       straight line   (1.0000, 0.0000, 2.0333)
       integrated local, mapped to world
                       (1.4024, 0.0000, -1.7416)
       apart by 3.7963 m after one second

Nearly four metres, in one second, from a body that nothing was pushing. A rotating frame is non-inertial: in it, Newton's laws do not hold as written, and the missing terms are the ones physics calls centrifugal and Coriolis. They are real in the sense that matters — something has to supply them or the answer is wrong — and nothing in a 4×4 matrix can tell you they are needed, because a matrix has no time derivative. inspect_frame reports this frame as perfectly inertial at every instant, and it is right at every instant, and the body is still four metres away.

9.5 Which is why the rule is absolute

A rigid_body's position and velocity are in world space. There is no way to express anything else. body_world has no concept of a parent, a hierarchy or a transform, and the absence is the design.

The bridge to the scene is one function, place_in_parent, which converts a body's world position into a local transform for a node whose parent's world matrix you hand it. It converts at the boundary, once per frame, rather than letting either space leak into the other. Its implementation is worth a glance for a reason that has nothing to do with physics: it needs the inverse of a general affine matrix, and it gets it by decomposing to a transform and calling 7.6's local_from_parent, which inverts by construction — transpose the rotation, reciprocate the scale, negate the translation — rather than by cofactors. Forty operations saved is not the reason. The reason is that a decomposition reports what it could not represent and a determinant does not.

And inspect_frame exists so that the rule is a measurable claim rather than an assertion. Nothing in the engine calls it. It is there so a tool can point at a scene node and say why physics must not happen there, and so that §9 could be written with numbers in it.

10. The implementation

Everything above is one header and one source file. They are short, and the interesting content is in the decisions rather than the arithmetic, so this section walks the four that are not obvious from reading the code.

10.1 A body has a motion; it is not one

struct rigid_body
{
    motion state{};          // 8.1's position + velocity, in WORLD space
    vec3 force{};            // newtons, cleared by step()
    float inv_mass = 1.0f;   // 1/kg; 0 means immovable
    float damping = 0.0f;    // 1/s, velocity-space, MASS-INDEPENDENT
    float gravity_scale = 1.0f;
    body_kind kind = body_kind::dynamic;
};

8.1's header promised this in as many words, and it is composition doing a real job rather than an aesthetic preference. Every function in integrate.hpp — all three rules, both drag helpers, all four diagnostics — takes a motion&, and none of them has to learn what a body is. That is what lets §5's harness step a bare motion beside a rigid_body and compare the two bit for bit, which would have been impossible if mass had been added by widening the struct.

gravity_scale is the one field with no physics in it, and it is here because every engine ships it: a balloon rises, a boss's hammer falls harder than it should because that is what makes it feel heavy, a projectile arcs on a curve an artist chose. It multiplies the world gravity and nothing else, which is what distinguishes it from the mistake of editing the mass to get the same visual result — editing the mass also changes how the body responds to every other force, and to every contact from 8.9 onward.

10.2 The order of operations inside step

const vec3 acceleration = (b.inv_mass > 0.0f)
                        ? (b.force * b.inv_mass + gravity_ * b.gravity_scale)
                        : vec3{};

integrate(b.state, acceleration, h, rule_);
if (b.damping > 0.0f) { apply_drag(b.state, b.damping, h); }

b.force = vec3{};

Four things in four lines, each settled by a section above. The accumulator is divided by the mass once (§3). Gravity is added afterwards, as the acceleration it already is, which is 15% cheaper and exact by construction (§5). The body is advanced by 8.1's rule, unchanged and unlitigated — 8.1 settled that semi-implicit Euler is the default and why, and this lesson does not reopen it. And the force is cleared last, so a tool between two steps can still see what produced the state it is looking at.

The damping is applied after the integration rather than folded into the acceleration, and that is 8.1 §8 rather than a preference: a = −kv is not a force derived from a potential, so feeding it through a symplectic rule gets you plain explicit Euler's stability limit on it — h < 2/k — which your simulation will find for you the first time somebody raises the coefficient. apply_drag solves it exactly instead.

The inv_mass > 0 guard is not about the multiply. It is about gravity_scale: a body with kind == dynamic and inv_mass == 0 is a legal and useful thing — immovable, but participating in everything — and without the guard gravity would be added to its acceleration and it would fall.

10.3 The three kinds, and what each one skips

A fixed body is skipped entirely, not integrated with a zero acceleration. It does not pay the loads, and 8.6's broadphase will rely on these never moving in order to keep a structure it never rebuilds. §11 measures the difference at 0.23× — a table of fixed bodies costs a quarter of what the same table of dynamic ones costs, and in a real level most bodies are fixed.

A kinematic body gets exactly one line of the integrator: 

b.state.position += b.state.velocity * h;

…written out rather than routed through integrate, so that it is obvious the velocity is not being changed. No gravity, no forces, no damping. It travels at whatever gameplay set, and it has a velocity — which is the thing that distinguishes it from teleporting the body every frame, and which 8.9 will need in order to carry a crate standing on it.

Both still have their force accumulators cleared, so that a system pushing on the floor does not leak a vector that grows without bound for the lifetime of the level.

10.4 The table is pool<rigid_body>, and that is the whole storage design

body_world is a pool<rigid_body> and about eight lines of policy. Lesson 5.4 built generational handles precisely so that subsystems would not each invent their own storage, and a body table is the textbook case: bodies are created and destroyed constantly, everything else in the engine wants to refer to them, and the solver in 8.10 wants them in a flat array it can walk without chasing a pointer.

What that buys, stated rather than assumed. Bodies live in a dense array with no holes, so bodies() is a contiguous span and step is a linear walk with perfect prefetching. A removal swaps the last body into the gap and bumps the slot's generation, so a stale body_id fails its lookup loudly instead of silently naming whichever body moved in. And the whole table serialises as 32-bit integers, which Module 9's scene format needs.

The one thing a caller must not do is depend on the order of that span — a removal reorders it. That is not a defect to work around; it is what keeps the walk contiguous, and 5.4 §6 measured what the alternative costs.

bodies, dynamic, kinematic, fixed A count per kind, which is the first thing you want when a scene is slow.
max_speed The fastest body at the end of the step.
max_travel The tunnelling number. A body that moves further in one step than the thickness of the wall it is heading for passes through it without any test ever seeing an overlap, because every test from 8.4 on is asked about a position and never about the segment between two of them. At 60 Hz a bullet at 400 m/s covers 6.67 m per step. Nothing here fixes it; reporting it is how you find out that you need to.
momentum The conservation law this module will be judged by. Gravity and thrusters change it — they are external forces, which is what an external force is — but a collision between two bodies must not, to the last bit the arithmetic allows. 8.9 checks its impulse response against this field, so the instrument predates the thing it measures by seven lessons.

Immovable bodies are excluded from the momentum sum, because theirs is either zero or infinite and neither belongs in a total that is about to be compared across a collision.

11. The budget

Four thousand and ninety-six bodies, stepped two thousand times, with the two arms of each comparison run alternately, one rep each so that both see the same thermal state within microseconds of each other. That machinery is Lesson 5.6's bench_compare, and it is the only reason the numbers below are worth quoting.

The first version of this section did not use it. 11.1 The optimisation that paid

b/a across three runs: 0.845, 0.848, 0.847. Adding gravity after the division rather than as a weight before it is 15% of the whole per-body update, and the ratio repeats to the third decimal place across separate runs of the binary — which is what makes it a finding rather than a fluctuation.

Fifteen per cent for removing one divss is a larger number than it sounds, and the comparison that makes it vivid is 8.1's: a complete semi-implicit Euler step — two vector multiply-adds, six loads, six stores — measured 0.963 ns. The divide that existed only to undo the storage decision cost about a third of that again.

11.2 The optimisation that did not

max_speed needs a length() per body, which is a square root, and the reflex fix is to compare length_squared and take one root at the end. Measured: 1.036, 0.968, 0.969. Once slower, twice marginally faster, and the spread between runs is larger than the effect. It is a tie.

This is worth reporting precisely because it is the more famous optimisation of the two. sqrtss is a single pipelined instruction on every target this engine builds for, and it is not on the loop's critical path — the next iteration's loads have already started. A divss is neither. So the engine keeps the sqrt, because when a measurement is a wash the version that reads as what it means wins by default, and length(v) reads as a speed where length_squared(v) reads as a puzzle.

Two optimisations, one obvious and one not, and the obvious one was the tie. That is the whole argument for measuring rather than reasoning about instruction counts — and note that both were measured with a control (answers agree yes), because an arm that is faster because it did less has not won anything.

One expf costs more than the whole rest of the update — damping adds 1.5 ns per body to a 2.7 ns step — which is not an argument against damping but is an argument for the branch that skips it when k is zero, and for knowing that turning it on for every body in a scene is a real decision rather than a free toggle.

And a table of fixed bodies costs 0.23× what the same table of dynamic ones does, which matters more than it looks: in a real level most bodies are the level.

For scale, 4,096 dynamic bodies at 2.706 ns is 11 µs, against a 16,667 µs frame at 60 Hz — 0.066%. Linear rigid-body integration is not where a physics engine's time goes, and it is worth establishing that now so that when 8.6's broadphase and 8.10's solver arrive, nobody optimises the wrong loop.

11.4 The demo

Figure 10's two screenshots are demos/bodies under two of its three resistance modes, and they are the reason the demo exists. Three bodies of 0.1, 1 and 10 kg are launched on identical trajectories. Under damping the three trails lie exactly on top of one another; under a drag force they separate completely, the light one stopping almost dead while the heavy one carries nearly as far as it would through vacuum.

One drawing decision in there is worth naming, because it is the same problem the figures have. Three curves drawn solid on top of one another are indistinguishable from one curve — so the picture that proves the point would look exactly like a picture of a bug that lost two bodies. Each body therefore draws one dash in three: a single line cycling amber, green, blue is three trajectories in exact agreement, and three separate dashed lines are three trajectories that parted company. The figures use the same convention, with the same phases.

The right-hand panel is §9 as something you can watch. Four bodies are dropped from the same world point in four different parent frames — and starting them at the same world point rather than the same local one is the whole experiment, because a body at local (0, 10) under a parent scaled by 2 starts at world (0, 20), and a panel that gave them all the same local position would be comparing four different drops. Only the green one is falling. The red one, under a uniform scale of 2, drops twice as far in the same time. The amber one goes sideways. The purple one, on a spinning parent, curves away entirely.

12. What this does not do

Stated as a list rather than implied by omission, because the gaps are the next eleven lessons.

  • No rotation. A rigid_body here is a point with a mass: it translates and it does not spin, add_force has no application point, and there is no torque and no inertia tensor. That is Lesson 8.3, and it needs 7.4's quaternions. The linear half is genuinely separable — momentum and angular momentum do not mix — so nothing in this file will have to be rewritten for it.
  • force does not contain the body's weight. §5's trade, named here so it is not a surprise: a debug overlay drawing force arrows draws every push on a body except the one that is always there. Draw it as mass_of(b) * world.gravity().
  • No collision, of any kind. The demo's ground line is drawn and then ignored, and the bodies fall through it. Shapes arrive in 8.4, the broadphase in 8.6, manifolds in 8.7, and response in 8.9.
  • No continuous collision detection. step_report::max_travel measures the number that decides whether a body will tunnel, and nothing acts on it. 1.8's swept test is the shape of the answer; 8.13 is where it stops being optional.
  • No sleeping and no islands. Every body is stepped every step, even one that has been motionless for a minute. 8.10.
  • No constraints and no joints. 8.10 and 8.11 — and note that this lesson has already made the argument for why they will be solved with impulses rather than with springs: §7, and before that 8.1 §6's measurement that a 60 Hz step admits a spring of about 3.8 Hz while a contact is hundreds.
  • No angular or non-linear drag. Real aerodynamic drag goes as v², not v; the linear model is the one whose terminal speed has a closed form, which is what made §6 measurable. Exercise 8.2.4 is the quadratic version.
  • Nothing threaded. The walk is embarrassingly parallel and there is exactly one place it will stop being so, which is the moment bodies start affecting each other. Module 9's job system.

15. Common pitfalls & debugging

  1. Symptom: an object drifts away forever after being pushed once.

    Cause: the force accumulator is never cleared. A force is an input to a step, not a property of a body, and a thruster fired for one frame keeps firing for the rest of the level.

    Fix: clear it in step, after the integration. Diagnosis: a body's force should be zero every time you look at it from outside step. If it is growing, count how many systems += to it per frame and compare against the number of times it is zeroed — the answer is usually zero.

  2. Symptom: a debug overlay says a resting crate has no forces on it.

    Cause: §5. This engine adds gravity as an acceleration, after the division, so rigid_body::force genuinely does not contain the weight. The overlay is correct and is showing you something true that is not what you asked.

    Fix: draw the weight separately as mass_of(b) * world.gravity(). It is worth knowing which side of this trade your engine is on before you spend an hour looking for a missing add_force.

  3. Symptom: the jump height changed when you got a new monitor.

    Cause: §7. Something that should be an impulse was written as a force applied for one step, so the velocity it produces is F·h/m and the height goes as h².

    Fix: add_impulse. Diagnosis: run the same input at two rates and compare. A ratio near 4 between 30 and 60 Hz is this bug and nothing else, because nothing else in a fixed-step simulation scales as the square of the step.

  4. Symptom: a heavy object and a light one fall identically, and that is wrong for your game.

    Cause: it is right for gravity, and you want air. You have reached for damping, which has no mass in it.

    Fix: a drag force, through the accumulator: add_force(b, b.state.velocity * -b_coeff). Diagnosis: compare the two terminal speeds. If a 0.1 kg body and a 100 kg body settle at the same speed, you are damping.

  5. Symptom: a body under a particular scene node falls at the wrong rate — and only under that node.

    Cause: §9. Something is integrating in a parent's local space, and the parent is scaled.

    Fix: integrate in world space and convert at the boundary with place_in_parent. Diagnosis: inspect_frame(parent_world, gravity) and read gain. A gain of 2 is a parent scaled by 2; a nonzero out_of_square is shear, and a uniform of false with a gain of 1 is §9.2's trap — a frame that is broken along an axis this particular vector does not touch.

  6. Symptom: the whole game feels floaty, or like a toy, and no amount of tuning fixes it.

    Cause: §8. One unit is not one metre.

    Fix: measure it rather than tune it. Put a body at a known height, time the fall, and compare against free_fall_time(d, 9.81). The ratio is 1/√s, so square it and you have the scale error. Then decide whether to rescale the art or rescale g — and if you rescale g, write down every absolute length in the project that has to move with it, because the solver tolerances will not.

  7. Symptom: a “very heavy” floor sinks slowly out of the level.

    Cause: §4.5. Gravity does not care what anything weighs; a million kilograms falls exactly as fast as ten grams.

    Fix: make_fixed, or inv_mass = 0. Diagnosis: if raising a mass made a problem smaller rather than removing it, you have the wrong tool — mass resists pushes, and gravity is not a push in this sense.

  8. Symptom: a fast object passes through a wall.

    Cause: nothing in this lesson, and nothing in the next five either. It is tunnelling, and it will remain possible until 8.13.

    Fix: not yet. Diagnosis: step_report::max_travel against the thinnest wall in the level. A bullet at 400 m/s covers 6.67 m per step at 60 Hz, and the number being larger than the wall is the whole explanation.

  9. Symptom: a replay diverges, or two machines in a lockstep session disagree.

    Cause: could be many things, and two of them are in this lesson. §3.4: the order in which systems call add_force changes the sum's last bits, and can drop a small force entirely. §5.4: a round trip through the mass can differ by an ulp between two bodies that should be identical.

    Fix: for the first, a fixed system order — which our design has, because the accumulator is a field rather than a list. For the second, gravity as an acceleration, which is what the engine now does. Diagnosis: hash the whole body table after each step and bisect on the first step at which two runs differ.

16. Exercises

  1. 8.2.1 — Draw the weight. §12 names the cost of the acceleration route: a force overlay no longer shows the one force that is always present. Add a weight_of(const rigid_body&, vec3 gravity) helper and use it in demos/bodies' ImGui panel to show total force including weight. Then answer the design question it raises: should step_report carry the total weight it applied, so the tool does not have to recompute it?

    Hint: the helper is one line. The interesting half is that a report field costs every caller and a helper costs only the one that asks — and §11 measured what a report field costs at 27% of the step.

  2. 8.2.2 — Find the mass that breaks it. §5.3 found that 16% of masses fail the round trip and that the first one is 1.000145 kg. Write a short program that, for a given mass, prints (g/w)·w against g in hex float (%a), and use it to find the largest mass under 10 kg that fails. Then answer: is the set of failing masses dense, or does it have structure?

    Hint: print the failures in a narrow band and look at their spacing in ulps of the mass rather than in kilograms. The reciprocal is a monotone map, so the structure you are looking for is about where 1/m lands relative to the boundaries between exponents.

  3. 8.2.3 — A kinematic platform, and the crate it does not carry. Add a kinematic body to demos/bodies that oscillates horizontally, and a dynamic one resting on it — resting in the sense of being placed at the right height, since nothing collides yet. Watch the crate fail to be carried. Then write down precisely what information 8.9 will need in order to carry it, and check that rigid_body already stores it.

    Hint: the answer is the platform's velocity, which is exactly why a kinematic body has one and why teleporting it every frame instead is the bug this exercise is about. The relative velocity at a contact point is the quantity 8.9 solves against.

  4. 8.2.4 — Quadratic drag. Real aerodynamic drag goes as v²: F = −½ρCdA|v|v. Implement it as a force in the demo and measure the terminal speed against the closed form √(2mg / ρCdA). Then find out what it costs you: the linear model's exact solution made apply_drag possible, and the quadratic one has no such step. At what h does an explicit quadratic drag step become unstable, and how does that limit compare to §6's?

    Hint: linearise about the current speed — the effective coefficient is 2·(½ρCdA|v|)/m, which grows with speed, so the stability limit tightens as the body speeds up. That is a different and nastier shape of bug than 8.1's, because the simulation is stable until it is fast.

  5. 8.2.5 — An engine challenge: make the frame rule enforceable. inspect_frame can tell you a frame is unsafe and nothing calls it. Design — and then build — a debug-only check that catches a body being integrated in a non-inertial frame. You will find there is no place to put it, because body_world deliberately has no idea a hierarchy exists. Resolve that, and defend your resolution.

    Hint: there are at least three defensible answers and they are not equivalent. A validation pass over the scene that runs on load and reports every node whose world matrix is non-inertial and has a body under it; an assertion inside place_in_parent, which is the one function that sees both spaces; or an ECS-side rule that a body component may not be attached below a scaled node. Ask of each: does it catch the spinning case? (§9.4 says nothing can.)

17. Recap & next

The engine has bodies. A rigid_body is 8.1's motion plus a mass, a force accumulator and two knobs, and body_world is 5.4's pool plus a walk. In one lesson this engine has gone from “a thing that can advance a state” to “a thing that can be told what is pushing on what”, and every one of the remaining eleven lessons in this module adds forces or impulses to that same accumulator.

What the lesson actually turned on, in four sentences. Forces add, which is why an accumulator and not a setter, and which is the reason four independent systems need no interface between them. The reciprocal is the quantity the arithmetic wants — not because of the divide, but because immovable is the common case, it is an exact zero, and 8.9's effective mass is literally a sum of inverse masses. An impulse has no h in it, which is a 23× difference in a jump and the reason every contact from 8.9 onward is an impulse. And the units and the frame are physics, not bookkeeping: a world at the wrong scale runs its clock at √s, and a body integrated under a scaled parent falls at the wrong rate with nothing anywhere reporting an error.

Two measurements were worth the harness on their own. A sweep of a million masses found that 16% of them fail a round trip that seven hand-picked ones all passed — the clearest case yet of test data chosen by a person agreeing with the code by construction. And of two candidate optimisations, the famous one (avoid a sqrt) was a tie at 1.036, 0.968, 0.969, while the one nobody talks about (do not compute a mass in order to cancel it) was worth 15% of the whole update.

Next: Lesson 8.3 — Angular Dynamics: Torque and the Inertia Tensor. Everything here has been the linear half, and it has been separable because momentum and angular momentum do not mix. 8.3 takes the other half, and the first thing it changes is the signature of add_force: a force applied at a point produces a torque r × F, whose sign is fixed by this course's right-handed convention rather than by taste. Then the quantity that plays the role mass played here — the inertia tensor, which is a 3×3 matrix rather than a scalar because a body resists being spun differently about different axes, and which therefore has to be carried into world space by a basis change R I Rᵀ every step. mat3 will gain the three operators an outer product needs. And the pleasing part: inv_mass = 0 has an exact analogue — a zero inverse inertia tensor is precisely what an immovable body wants, for exactly the reasons §4 gave, which is the second time this lesson's storage decision will pay for itself.
