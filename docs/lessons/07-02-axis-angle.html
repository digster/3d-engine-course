Axis-Angle and Rodrigues' Rotation Formula

Lesson 7.1 asked "which three turns compose to this orientation?" and got twenty-four different answers, a singularity that no choice of answer escapes, and an interpolation that performs 14% more turning than it needs on an ordinary pair of poses and 209% more near gimbal lock. This lesson asks a different question — what single turn is this? — and the difference between the two questions is the whole arc of Module 7. Euler's rotation theorem says the second question always has an answer: every rotation of space about a fixed origin is a turn of some angle about some axis. Not "can be decomposed into". Is. We prove it in four lines of determinant algebra using only functions mat3 has had since Lesson 2.6, and then we look hard at the one step that fails in four dimensions — because the theorem is false there, and knowing why is what turns it from a fact you memorised into one you understand. Then we derive Rodrigues' rotation formula from a picture rather than quoting it, build the matrix it names, and go backwards: given a rotation, find its turn. That inverse has two holes, at θ = 0 and at θ = 180°, and they are not equally dangerous — an error φ in the axis produces 2 sin(θ/2) · φ of error in the orientation, which is zero exactly where the axis becomes unrecoverable and maximal where it becomes hard to find. That one formula explains both ends, sets both thresholds, and is measured against a brute-force sweep to two parts in a hundred thousand. And then the payoff. Scaling the angle of the single turn that separates two orientations traces the geodesic between them — the shortest path, at constant speed — so 7.1's +14% and +209% become 0.00% at every pose, on the same pairs, measured with the same instrument. That is spherical linear interpolation, three lines long, and it is also where this representation runs out: every call spends an atan2, a sqrt and a sin/cos pair on a quantity a quaternion carries for free, and axis-angle has no formula at all for composing two turns. Lessons 7.3 and 7.4 are about fixing that.

1. The problem: three numbers, twenty-four meanings, one hole

Lesson 7.1 gave the engine an interface: three numbers a human can type, converted to the mat3 the renderer multiplies by. It works, it is written down, and the Conventions page now names it. It also ended with three indictments, and they are worth restating precisely because this lesson answers all three.

First, the convention is load-bearing and invisible. There are twenty-four self-consistent ways to read three angles as an orientation. They are all in use. The same triple (30°, 40°, 50°) read under the wrong one lands anywhere from 16° to 83° away from where it belongs, and the 16° case is the dangerous one, because 16° reads as a tuning problem rather than as a wrong answer. A file that contains three floats and does not say which of the twenty-four it means does not contain an orientation.

Second, there is a hole. At pitch ±90° the yaw and roll knobs turn about the same world axis, one degree of freedom of the parameterisation disappears, and the matrix that converts knob rates into angular velocity drops rank. That is not a defect of the y-x-z order; every three-angle scheme has such a hole somewhere, and the only thing an axis order decides is where to put it.

Third — and this is the one that actually costs a game frames and looks wrong on screen — interpolating three angles does not interpolate the rotation. Lesson 7.1 measured a generic blend performing 204.29° of turning for a journey that needs 179.05°: a 25.24° detour, with the angular speed varying by a factor of 1.56 along the way. Run the identical angle deltas near gimbal lock and the detour becomes 209.5% — the blend performs three times the turning the move requires.

Every one of those three is a property of the way we wrote the rotation down, not of rotation. So the honest response is not to patch the notation. It is to ask whether there is a different question to ask about a rotation whose answer does not have these problems.

The question this lesson asks

Euler angles answer: which three turns, about which axes, in which order, compose to this orientation? That question has twenty-four readings and a singularity in each.

Axis-angle answers: what single turn is this? That question has exactly one reading, and — as §4 proves — it always has an answer.

2. Building intuition: the line that stays still

Put your hand flat on the desk, palm down, fingers pointing away from you. Now turn it into any other orientation you like: twist it, tip it, roll it, take as many separate movements as you want. Stop somewhere awkward.

Here is the claim. However you got there, there is a single straight line through your wrist such that one clean turn about that line — one movement, one axis — would have taken your hand from flat-on-the-desk to exactly where it is now. Not approximately. Exactly. And you did not choose that line; it was decided the moment you stopped moving.

That is a strong claim and it should not feel obvious. You performed three or four separate turns about three or four different axes, and the assertion is that the net effect of all of them is always expressible as one turn about one axis. Most compositions of operations do not collapse like that. Two translations compose into a translation, fine — but two scalings about different centres do not generally compose into a scaling, and two shears do not compose into a shear. Rotations are special, and the reason they are special is a fact about the number three.

Before the algebra, though, look at what the claim means for the points of the object. If there is a single axis, then every point on that axis has not moved at all — a rotation about a line leaves the line fixed. And every point off the axis has travelled along a circle centred on the axis, through exactly the same angle as every other point. So the entire motion of an entire rigid body, however many separate movements it took, is described by a line and one number.

Figure 1 draws that for three sample vectors. The teal one lies along the axis and is in precisely the same place in both panels — the arrowhead has not moved a pixel. The amber and blue ones have each swung round it by the same 58°, and the curves in the right-hand panel are the paths they took. The dashed circle is the orbit: it is drawn once, and every direction at that angle from the axis lives on it.

There is a second way to see it that some people find more convincing, and it is worth having both. Take any rotation and ask what it does to a sphere centred at the origin. It maps the sphere onto itself — rotations preserve length, so no point leaves the sphere and no point on the sphere fails to be hit. So we have a continuous map from a sphere to itself that preserves distances and does not turn it inside out. Now think about what such a map can do to the directions on the sphere, and the question becomes: must something be left alone? In two dimensions, no — spin a circle by 37° and nothing on it stays put. In three, the answer turns out to be yes, and the difference between those two answers is exactly the difference between an even dimension and an odd one.

Where this is going

The two-dimensional case leaving nothing fixed is not a failure of intuition, it is the right answer: a 2-D rotation's axis points out of the plane, which is a direction the plane does not contain. That is the first hint that "rotation happens in a plane, and in 3-D a plane happens to have a unique normal" is the deeper statement, and "rotation happens about an axis" is a coincidence of three dimensions. Lesson 7.3 picks that thread up; §4.3 shows what happens when you pull it in 4-D.

3. What axis-angle is, and the two ambiguities it admits

The representation is exactly what the last section described: a unit vector n naming the line, and an angle θ saying how far to turn about it, right-hand rule (Conventions §2). Four numbers, for a thing with three degrees of freedom, with one constraint — |n| = 1 — soaking up the extra.

Before we build anything, it is worth being precise about when two different (n, θ) pairs are the same rotation, because every difficulty in this lesson's second half is one of these three facts arriving somewhere inconvenient.

  1. Reversing both reverses nothing. (n, θ) and (−n, −θ) are the same rotation: turning the other way about the opposite axis gets you to the same place. We remove this one by convention, taking θ ≥ 0 always, and it costs nothing.
  2. The identity has no axis. (n, 0) is the identity for every unit n. "Every axis is correct" and "no axis is determined" are the same sentence, and no implementation can do better than pick one and say so.
  3. A half-turn has two axes. (n, 180°) and (−n, 180°) are the same rotation — turning 180° one way about a line and 180° the other way about the same line land in the same place. This is not a numerical difficulty; it is a genuine two-valued answer, and the measurement in §8.6 shows the two differing by 1.0e-05°, which is float noise and not disagreement.

With θ ≥ 0 fixed by convention, we also want an upper bound, and [0, 180°] is the natural one: a turn of 200° about n is a turn of 160° about −n, so restricting to half-turns or less loses nothing and gains the property that the angle is the distance. That is not a small gain. It means θ is exactly the quantity Lesson 7.1 had to build a separate instrument to measure, and it falls out of the representation for free.

Canonical form, used everywhere in this lesson

axis is unit, angle is in [0, π]. Every rotation has exactly one such spelling, except the identity (infinitely many, because the axis is free) and the half-turn (two, because the sign is free). Both exceptions are real, both are handled, and neither is swept under a clamp.

4. Euler's rotation theorem

The claim, stated properly: every rotation of three-dimensional space about a fixed origin is a rotation about some axis through that origin. Euler published it in 1775, and the modern proof is short enough to do here in full — which matters, because the short proof is also the one that tells you why it is a fact about odd dimensions.

4.1 "There is an axis" and "something does not move" are the same sentence

First, translate the claim into something we can compute with. "There is an axis" means there is a direction n that the rotation leaves alone:

\[ R\,\mathbf{n} = \mathbf{n}, \qquad \mathbf{n} \neq \mathbf{0} \] R n = n, for some non-zero n

In the language of Lesson 2.5 — a matrix is where the basis vectors land — this says there is some direction that lands exactly where it started. Rearranging, R n − n = 0, that is (R − I) n = 0: the matrix R − I sends a non-zero vector to zero. A matrix that flattens a non-zero vector to nothing has collapsed space, and the determinant is exactly the number that reports how much space survived (Lesson 2.6). So the whole theorem reduces to one arithmetic claim:

\[ \det(R - I) = 0 \] det(R - I) = 0

That is now a statement we can check, and one the engine already has every function for. Section A.1 of the harness checks it on twenty thousand random rotations.

4.2 The proof, in four lines

We need det(R − I) = 0 for every rotation. A rotation is an orthogonal matrix (RᵀR = I, so Rᵀ = R⁻¹) with det R = +1 — those two conditions are the definition, and the second is what separates a rotation from a reflection. Three determinant facts do the rest, all of which Lesson 2.6 established: det(AB) = det(A)·det(B), det(Aᵀ) = det(A), and det(−A) = (−1)ⁿ·det(A) for an n×n matrix.

\[ \begin{aligned} \det(R - I) &= \det(R^{\mathsf T})\,\det(R - I) && \text{since } \det(R^{\mathsf T}) = \det(R) = 1 \\ &= \det\!\big(R^{\mathsf T}(R - I)\big) && \text{product rule} \\ &= \det(I - R^{\mathsf T}) && \text{since } R^{\mathsf T}R = I \\ &= \det\!\big((I - R)^{\mathsf T}\big) \;=\; \det(I - R) \\ &= (-1)^3 \det(R - I) \;=\; -\det(R - I) \end{aligned} \] det(R - I) = det(R^T) det(R - I) = det(R^T R - R^T) = det(I - R^T) = det(I - R) = (-1)^3 det(R - I) = -det(R - I)

The first line multiplies by det(Rᵀ), which is 1, so nothing has changed. The second folds the product into one determinant. The third uses RᵀR = I. The fourth notices that I − Rᵀ is the transpose of I − R, and transposing does not change a determinant. The fifth pulls a minus sign out of a 3×3 matrix, which costs (−1)³ = −1.

So x = −x, where x = det(R − I). The only number equal to its own negative is zero. Therefore det(R − I) = 0, therefore R − I collapses some non-zero direction to nothing, therefore some direction satisfies R n = n, therefore there is an axis. □

Two loose ends, named rather than glossed

The argument gives a fixed direction; it does not by itself say the rotation is a turn about that direction by some angle. That last step is short: build a basis whose first vector is n, write R in it, and orthogonality plus R n = n force the other two columns to be a 2×2 rotation in the remaining plane — which is a turn by an angle. We use the consequence of that in §8.1 (the trace is 1 + 2cos θ) and do not re-derive it here; Lesson 7.1's angle_between_rotations already spells it out in full.

And the determinant proof shows a fixed direction exists without telling you which. That is normal for an existence proof and it is why §8 is a separate section: finding the axis is a different job from knowing there is one, and it is the harder of the two.

4.3 Why three, and what happens in four

Exactly one line of that proof mentions the dimension: (−1)³ = −1. In an even dimension it would be (−1)⁴ = +1, the last line would read x = x, and the argument would prove nothing whatsoever. That is not a technicality to skip past. It is the theorem's entire content, and the cleanest way to see it is to watch the theorem fail.

Figure 2 is the eigenvalue picture, which is the same proof from the other side. A rotation preserves lengths, so every eigenvalue has magnitude 1 — they all sit on the unit circle in the complex plane. And a matrix with real entries has eigenvalues that come in conjugate pairs: if λ is one, so is λ̄. A 3×3 matrix has three eigenvalues, and three is odd, so after pairing them up one is always left over — and an unpaired eigenvalue must be its own conjugate, hence real, hence (being on the unit circle) either +1 or −1. The determinant is the product of the eigenvalues, and for a rotation it is +1; the conjugate pair multiplies to |λ|² = 1, so the leftover one must be +1. An eigenvalue of +1 is a fixed direction. That is the axis.

In four dimensions nothing is left over. A 4-D rotation can turn in two completely independent planes at once — the xy plane by one angle and the zw plane by another — and its four eigenvalues form two conjugate pairs with none spare. Nothing is fixed. There is no axis, because there is nothing for an axis to be.

Not zero — and it agrees with the eigenvalue prediction to five decimal places, so the determinant and the eigenvalue story are the same story. In four dimensions the sentence "every rotation is a rotation about an axis" is simply false, and this number is the counterexample. The harness computes it with its own 4×4 determinant, twenty lines of cofactor expansion, because mat3 cannot hold a disproof of a claim about mat3.

Ninety percent of the picture

The honest general statement is that rotation happens in a plane, not about an axis. In n dimensions a rotation decomposes into turns in ⌊n/2⌋ mutually perpendicular planes. Three dimensions is the special case where ⌊3/2⌋ = 1 — one plane — and a plane in 3-D has a unique normal direction, so you can name the plane by its normal and call it "the axis". Both coincidences are needed, and both fail in 4-D. The missing ten percent is the general spectral theorem for orthogonal matrices; Artin's Algebra §5.1 finishes it.

5. Rodrigues' rotation formula, derived

We know a single turn exists. Now we need to perform it: given a unit axis n, an angle θ, and a vector v, where does v go?

5.1 Split the vector, and one half does nothing

The whole derivation is one idea: separate the part of v that the rotation cannot touch from the part it acts on. Decompose v into a component along the axis and a component across it:

\[ \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v}_\parallel + \mathbf{v}_\perp, \qquad \mathbf{v}_\parallel = (\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{v})\,\mathbf{n} \] v = v_parallel + v_perp, with v_parallel = (n . v) n

The parallel part is the projection of v onto the axis — Lesson 1.7's dot product, doing the job it was introduced for. It lies on the axis, and the axis does not move, so v∥ does not move. Half the problem is already solved and we have not done any trigonometry.

What remains is v⊥ = v − v∥, which is perpendicular to the axis and therefore lives in the plane the rotation turns. Inside that plane, this is a two-dimensional rotation of a vector by θ, which Lesson 1.7 already solved — but to use that solution we need a pair of axes for the plane, and we need them to be perpendicular and the same length, or "rotate by θ" is not what the sines and cosines will do.

One of them is v⊥ itself. The other is the one place a cross product is the obvious tool: n × v is perpendicular to both n and v, so it lies in the plane and is perpendicular to v⊥. And its length is |n||v| sin α where α is the angle between n and v — which, with n unit, is exactly |v| sin α = |v⊥|. Same length. So {v⊥, n × v} is a perfectly good pair of axes for the disc, and the 2-D rotation is the one everybody knows:

\[ \mathbf{v}_\perp' = \mathbf{v}_\perp\cos\theta + (\mathbf{n}\times\mathbf{v})\sin\theta \] v_perp' = v_perp cos(theta) + (n x v) sin(theta)

Figure 3 is that sentence, drawn. The dashed disc is the plane v⊥ turns in, centred on the tip of v∥; the two in-plane arrows are the pair of axes; the blue arc is the sweep. Everything about the formula is visible in it, including the thing that is easiest to forget — that n × v and n × v⊥ are the same vector, because the parallel part of v contributes nothing to a cross product with n. That is why the formula can take the cross product of the axis with the whole of v and never has to compute v⊥ at all.

Add the fixed part back and substitute v⊥ = v − (n·v)n:

\[ \mathbf{v}' \;=\; \mathbf{v}\cos\theta \;+\; (\mathbf{n}\times\mathbf{v})\sin\theta \;+\; \mathbf{n}\,(\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{v})(1-\cos\theta) \] v' = v cos(theta) + (n x v) sin(theta) + n (n . v) (1 - cos(theta))

That is Rodrigues' rotation formula, and the three terms have three readings you can keep rather than memorise. The first is most of the vector just leans over. The second is and it swings sideways, along the one direction perpendicular to both. The third is the correction that puts back the along-axis part the cosine took away — which is why it carries (1 − cos θ) and vanishes at θ = 0, where nothing should be corrected.

A sanity check worth doing once

Set v = n. Then n × n = 0 and n·n = 1, so the formula gives n cos θ + 0 + n(1 − cos θ) = n. The axis does not move. A formula that failed this check would be wrong in the one way that matters most, and it takes ten seconds.

Take the axis through the corner of a cube — the body diagonal (1, 1, 1), normalised — and turn by 120°. A cube has three-fold symmetry about that diagonal, so we should expect something clean, and what we get is the cleanest possible answer: the coordinate axes cycle. x̂ → ŷ → ẑ → x̂.

5.3 The matrix form, and why we do not write it

A renderer does not rotate one vector, it rotates thousands, and it wants a matrix so the trigonometry is paid for once. Lesson 2.5 gives the construction directly: a matrix is where the basis vectors land. Push x̂, ŷ and ẑ through Rodrigues, and the three answers are the three columns. The worked example above already computed the first column of the 120°-about-the-diagonal matrix without anybody calling it that.

Every reference states the result in closed form instead, and it is worth writing down once so that you recognise it in a paper:

\[ R \;=\; I\cos\theta \;+\; \sin\theta\,[\mathbf{n}]_\times \;+\; (1-\cos\theta)\,\mathbf{n}\mathbf{n}^{\mathsf T} \] R = I cos(theta) + sin(theta) [n]_x + (1 - cos(theta)) n n^T

where [n]ₓ is the cross-product matrix — the matrix that does what crossing with n does — and n nᵀ is the outer product, the matrix that projects onto n. Read the three terms and it is the same three sentences as before: scale everything by the cosine, add the sideways swing, restore the along-axis part.

\[ [\mathbf{n}]_\times = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -n_z & n_y \\ n_z & 0 & -n_x \\ -n_y & n_x & 0 \end{bmatrix} \] [n]_x = [[0, -nz, ny], [nz, 0, -nx], [-ny, nx, 0]] -- antisymmetric, zero diagonal

We implement the basis-vector version, not this one, and the reasons are worth separating because one of them is a matter of taste and the other is not.

The small reason is that mat3 has no operator+, no scalar multiply and no outer product. It has never needed them, and adding three operators to a core type so that one function can be spelled differently is the wrong trade at this point in the course. Module 8's inertia tensor genuinely needs an outer product — that is what an inertia tensor is — and that is the right moment to add them, at the point of need rather than in advance.

The large reason is that the basis-vector version is checkable by a reader who has the derivation in their head, and nine transcribed products of sines are not. You can look at three calls to rotate_about_axis and see the definition of a matrix. You cannot look at m21 = n.z * n.y * k + n.x * s and see anything at all — which is exactly why the harness transcribes the closed form separately and checks the two against each other across fifty thousand turns. A second, independent route is the only thing that makes a transcription trustworthy.

6. The exponential map, and the rotation vector

There is a second road to Rodrigues' formula. It is longer, it needs one idea we have not used before, and it is worth the detour for two reasons: it explains a spelling you will meet in every physics paper, and it produces a representation with three numbers instead of four and no constraint to maintain — the one Module 8 will integrate rigid bodies with.

6.1 Turning a little, many times

Start from angular velocity rather than from angle. Suppose a body spins about the unit axis n at one radian per second. Over a very small time dt, the velocity of the point at v is n × v — that is what the cross product means for rotation, and Lesson 7.1 §9b of the Conventions page fixed it. So over that small step, v moves to v + (n × v) dt, which in matrix terms is

\[ v \;\mapsto\; \big(I + [\mathbf{n}]_\times \,dt\big)\,v \] v -> (I + [n]_x dt) v

That is not a rotation — it is a rotation's first-order approximation, and applied once it makes the vector slightly too long. But apply it N times with dt = θ/N and let N grow:

\[ R(\mathbf{n}, \theta) \;=\; \lim_{N\to\infty}\Big(I + \tfrac{\theta}{N}[\mathbf{n}]_\times\Big)^{\!N} \;=\; \exp\!\big(\theta\,[\mathbf{n}]_\times\big) \] R(n, theta) = limit as N grows of (I + (theta/N)[n]_x)^N = exp(theta [n]_x)

The limit on the left is the definition of ex you met for numbers, applied to a matrix. So a rotation is the exponential of an antisymmetric matrix, and the name "exponential map" is not a metaphor.

Now expand the exponential as a series, and watch it collapse. The only fact needed is what happens when you cross with n twice. Crossing with n kills the along-axis part and rotates the rest by 90° in the plane; do it again and you have rotated by 180° in the plane, which is a negation of the perpendicular part:

\[ [\mathbf{n}]_\times^2 = \mathbf{n}\mathbf{n}^{\mathsf T} - I, \qquad\text{and therefore}\qquad [\mathbf{n}]_\times^3 = -[\mathbf{n}]_\times \] [n]_x squared = n n^T - I, and [n]_x cubed = -[n]_x

The second identity is the one that does the work: every third power brings you back to where you started with a minus sign, so the infinite series only ever contains two distinct matrices, [n]ₓ and [n]ₓ². Group the terms by which one they carry, and the odd powers assemble into sin θ and the even ones (past the first) into 1 − cos θ:

\[ \exp\big(\theta[\mathbf{n}]_\times\big) = I + \Big(\theta - \tfrac{\theta^3}{3!} + \cdots\Big)[\mathbf{n}]_\times + \Big(\tfrac{\theta^2}{2!} - \tfrac{\theta^4}{4!} + \cdots\Big)[\mathbf{n}]_\times^2 = I + \sin\theta\,[\mathbf{n}]_\times + (1-\cos\theta)[\mathbf{n}]_\times^2 \] exp(theta [n]_x) = I + sin(theta) [n]_x + (1 - cos(theta)) [n]_x^2

Substitute [n]ₓ² = n nᵀ − I and it is §5.3's closed form, term for term. Two completely different arguments — one a picture of a disc, one a power series — land on the same nine numbers. That is the kind of agreement worth noticing.

6.2 Three numbers, no constraint, no hole at the identity

Look at what the exponential takes as input: θ[n]ₓ, a single antisymmetric matrix, which has exactly three independent entries. Those three numbers are the vector r = θn — the rotation vector, whose direction is the axis and whose length is the angle.

This is a better-behaved object than the (n, θ) pair, and the reason is the representation's first ambiguity from §3. (n, 0) is the identity for every n, so the pair has an entire sphere's worth of spellings for one rotation — a genuine defect, a pinch point in the parameterisation. The rotation vector has r = 0 and nothing else. The hole at the identity closes.

Rewrite Rodrigues in terms of r by substituting n = r/θ, and the angle survives only inside two coefficients:

\[ \mathbf{v}' = \mathbf{v} + \frac{\sin\theta}{\theta}\,(\mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{v}) + \frac{1-\cos\theta}{\theta^2}\,\big(\mathbf{r}\times(\mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{v})\big), \qquad \theta = |\mathbf{r}| \] v' = v + (sin(theta)/theta)(r x v) + ((1 - cos(theta))/theta^2)(r x (r x v)), where theta = |r|

Both coefficients are finite at θ = 0 — sin θ/θ → 1 and (1 − cos θ)/θ² → 1/2 — so this expression has no singularity at the identity at all, only a removable 0/0 at the single point where θ is exactly zero. That is the formula the engine implements, and it is why rotation_from_rotation_vector needs no special case for a small turn.

It does not remove every hole, only that one

The rotation vector is only unique on the ball |r| ≤ π, because turning by θ + 2π is turning by θ. And on the ball's surface, r and −r are the same rotation — §3's half-turn ambiguity, arriving by another route. So the rotation space is a solid ball with antipodal surface points glued together, which is a genuinely strange shape and is exactly why rotation is hard. What the rotation vector fixes is the pinch at the centre. The seam on the surface is still there, and Lesson 7.4 will meet it again as the quaternion double cover.

6.3 One cancellation that turns out not to matter, and why to measure anyway

Lesson 7.1 found the same numerical bug three times: a quantity that should be small, computed by subtracting two nearly-equal numbers near 1.0, destroyed before it reached the caller. The coefficient (1 − cos θ)/θ² above has exactly that shape. At θ = 1e-4 a float cannot represent 1 − cos θ ≈ 5e-9 as a difference from 1.0 at all, and the expression returns zero instead of one half.

So the engine spells it the other way, using the half-angle identity 1 − cos θ = 2sin²(θ/2), which never subtracts anything:

\[ \frac{1-\cos\theta}{\theta^2} \;=\; \frac{2\sin^2(\theta/2)}{\theta^2} \;=\; \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\sin(\theta/2)}{\theta/2}\right)^{\!2} \] (1 - cos(theta))/theta^2 = 2 sin^2(theta/2)/theta^2 = 0.5 * (sin(theta/2)/(theta/2))^2

That is the right habit. But here is the finding, and it is the opposite of 7.1's: measured, it makes essentially no difference, and the reason it makes no difference is worth more than the fix.

The explanation is in the multiplication. That coefficient scales r × (r × v), whose magnitude is θ²|v|. The coefficient's absolute error is about ε/θ², because ε of absolute error in 1 − cos θ divided by θ² is ε/θ². Multiply the two and the θ² cancels exactly: the term's contribution to the matrix is wrong by ε, one ULP, at every angle.

The transferable rule, and it is not "always use the half-angle form"

7.1's rule was: if a formula's job is to report something near zero, look at what it computes just before it returns and ask whether that is ever small. This section adds the other half. Ask what happens to the damaged quantity on its way to the output. In 7.1 the damaged quantity was the output, so the damage was the answer. Here it is multiplied by something that shrinks at precisely the rate the error grows, and the product is clean. The way to tell the two apart is not to recognise the pattern — the pattern is identical — it is to measure the number that reaches the caller.

We keep the half-angle spelling anyway. It costs one extra multiply, it is not harder to read, and "this is fine because the error cancels downstream" is a property of the current call sites rather than of the function.

7.2 Rodrigues, in code

The vector form is the formula with the names changed, and nothing else: 

[[nodiscard]] inline vec3 rotate_about_axis(vec3 v, vec3 unit_axis, float angle)
{
    const float c = std::cos(angle);
    const float s = std::sin(angle);
    return v * c + cross(unit_axis, v) * s + unit_axis * (dot(unit_axis, v) * (1.0f - c));
}

Three terms, in the order §5.1 derived them. A reader who has the picture can check this line against the picture, which is the entire argument for writing it this way.

And the matrix is Lesson 2.5's definition, applied: 

[[nodiscard]] inline mat3 rotation_from_axis_angle(axis_angle r)
{
    return mat3{rotate_about_axis(vec3{1.0f, 0.0f, 0.0f}, r.axis, r.angle),
                rotate_about_axis(vec3{0.0f, 1.0f, 0.0f}, r.axis, r.angle),
                rotate_about_axis(vec3{0.0f, 0.0f, 1.0f}, r.axis, r.angle)};
}

Column 0 is where x̂ goes, column 1 where ŷ goes, column 2 where ẑ goes, and mat3 stores columns (Lesson 2.6 §3.3), so there is no rearrangement at all. This is the same function body a student could write from the figure.

7.3 Why the axis is not normalised for you

rotation_from_axis_angle requires r.axis to be unit and does not enforce it. That is a decision, not an omission, and it is worth defending because the obvious alternative — a quiet normalised() inside the struct or the function — is what most libraries do.

A non-unit axis does not produce a slightly wrong rotation. Look at the three terms: the cross product scales with |n| and the projection term scales with |n|², so the result shears — it is not a rotation at all, and everything downstream that assumes RᵀR = I is now quietly wrong. Silently fixing that at the bottom of the stack hides a real mistake at the top of it.

And there is a better answer available to the caller who has a non-unit direction in hand — the output of a cross, say. What they actually have is a rotation vector: a direction whose length means something. §6.2's rotation_from_rotation_vector takes exactly that, has no unit requirement to violate, and is the function they wanted. The API's shape says so.

7.4 What the readable spelling costs, measured twice

The basis-vector form does about three times the arithmetic of the transcribed closed form: it computes three cross products and three dot products where the closed form computes nine entries directly. So it should be three times slower. Count the multiplies and that is the prediction.

The third row is the interesting one. Because it could not be shown to win: it came out 1.5% faster here and 3% slower in a standalone harness with the same four spellings, which is to say identical. sin and cos are pure functions, so common-subexpression elimination hoists them whether or not you ask, and what remains is a difference in how well a lambda folds against literal basis vectors — which does not survive a change of surrounding code.

The rule that came out of it

An optimisation whose sign flips between measurements is not an optimisation, and paying for it in readability is a straight loss. The version that ships is the one a reader can check against Figure 3.

8. Implementation: recovering one

Given a rotation matrix, find the turn. This is the harder half, it is where the two ambiguities of §3 arrive as real code, and it is the part most implementations get subtly wrong — usually by clamping something and hoping.

8.1 The angle, which 7.1 already computed

Split any matrix into a symmetric part and an antisymmetric part; it is the matrix version of splitting a function into even and odd halves, and for a rotation both halves turn out to carry exactly one thing we want. Take the closed form from §5.3 and read it:

\[ \operatorname{tr}(R) = 1 + 2\cos\theta, \qquad R - R^{\mathsf T} = 2\sin\theta\,[\mathbf{n}]_\times \] trace(R) = 1 + 2 cos(theta), and R - R^T = 2 sin(theta) [n]_x

The trace comes from the diagonal: I cos θ contributes 3cos θ, [n]ₓ has a zero diagonal and contributes nothing, and (1 − cos θ)n nᵀ contributes (1 − cos θ)(nx² + ny² + nz²) = 1 − cos θ because n is unit. Total 3cos θ + 1 − cos θ = 1 + 2cos θ.

The antisymmetric part is even easier: I and n nᵀ are both symmetric, so they cancel completely in R − Rᵀ, and only the sin θ [n]ₓ term survives — doubled, because subtracting an antisymmetric matrix's transpose is adding it.

So we have the cosine and the sine of the angle, and Lesson 7.1 §8 established at length why atan2 of both beats acos of one: the trace alone cannot resolve a small turn, because near θ = 0 the trace is 3 − θ² and a float at 3.0 cannot hold a change of 1.6e-05. The measured relative error of the textbook acos((tr R − 1)/2) at a 0.004° turn is 1.00 — it returns zero.

\[ \theta \;=\; \operatorname{atan2}\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\big\|R - R^{\mathsf T}\big\|,\; \tfrac{1}{2}\big(\operatorname{tr}(R) - 1\big)\right) \] theta = atan2( |R - R^T| / 2 , (trace(R) - 1) / 2 )

The first argument is a magnitude, so atan2 returns a value in [0, π] — which is §3's canonical range, arriving for free rather than being clamped into place. This is the same four lines Lesson 7.1 wrote as angle_between_rotations, and the only thing 7.2 changes is that it stops throwing away the vector it takes the length of.

8.2 The axis, for free, almost everywhere

That vector is the axis. R − Rᵀ = 2 sin θ [n]ₓ, and an antisymmetric matrix has three independent entries which are exactly the components of the vector it crosses with. In written notation they are (r21 − r12, r02 − r20, r10 − r01), and that vector is 2 sin θ · n: direction the axis, length twice the sine.

So the general case is one division. Normalise the skew vector and you have the axis, with the correct sign — because sin θ > 0 for every θ strictly between 0 and π, so normalising cannot flip it. 

const vec3 skew{m.at(2, 1) - m.at(1, 2),
                m.at(0, 2) - m.at(2, 0),
                m.at(1, 0) - m.at(0, 1)};
// ... and after computing the angle from atan2 of its length and the trace:
out.value.axis = skew / two_sin;

8.3 What an axis error actually costs — the formula that explains both ends

Before handling either degenerate case, it is worth knowing what is at stake in each, and there is one formula that answers for both. Suppose the axis you recover is off by a small angle φ. How wrong is the resulting orientation?

Two turns of the same angle θ about axes φ apart differ by a rotation of Θ, where

\[ \cos(\Theta/2) = \cos^2(\theta/2) + \sin^2(\theta/2)\cos\varphi \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; \Theta \approx 2\sin(\theta/2)\,\varphi \quad\text{for small }\varphi \] cos(Theta/2) = cos^2(theta/2) + sin^2(theta/2) cos(phi), so for small phi, Theta is about 2 sin(theta/2) phi

Read the consequence rather than the derivation. An error in the axis is multiplied by 2 sin(θ/2) on its way into the orientation. At θ = 0 that factor is zero. At θ = 180° it is two — the error is doubled.

That single curve settles the design of the whole extraction, and it says something that is not obvious: the two hard ends are not equally dangerous. At the identity the axis becomes unrecoverable, and it does not matter, because whatever nonsense you return is multiplied by essentially zero. At a half-turn the axis becomes hard to find, and it matters more than anywhere else, because that is exactly where the multiplier peaks. A library that treats the two ends symmetrically — the same epsilon, the same fallback — has misunderstood its own problem.

8.4 The half-turn, where the easy route runs out

As θ approaches 180°, sin θ approaches zero, the skew vector's length 2 sin θ approaches zero with it, and normalising a vanishing vector amplifies whatever error it carries. A float's ε of absolute error in the matrix entries arrives in the axis direction as roughly ε / (2 sin θ), which grows without bound. At exactly θ = 180° the matrix is symmetric, the skew vector is identically zero, and there is nothing at all to normalise.

The information has not been destroyed, though — it has moved. At a half-turn, everything about the rotation is in the symmetric part, which is where we have not looked. Take the closed form again and read the diagonal and the off-diagonal separately:

\[ r_{ii} = \cos\theta + (1-\cos\theta)\,n_i^2, \qquad r_{ij} + r_{ji} = 2(1-\cos\theta)\,n_i n_j \quad (i \neq j) \] r_ii = cos(theta) + (1 - cos(theta)) n_i^2, and r_ij + r_ji = 2(1 - cos(theta)) n_i n_j for i not equal to j

The [n]ₓ term is antisymmetric, so it cancels exactly in the second line — which is precisely why this route is immune to whatever is happening to the part the first route depends on. Rearranged, with cos θ already known from the trace:

\[ n_i^2 = \frac{r_{ii} - \cos\theta}{1 - \cos\theta}, \qquad n_j = \frac{r_{ij} + r_{ji}}{2(1-\cos\theta)\,n_i} \] n_i^2 = (r_ii - cos(theta)) / (1 - cos(theta)), and n_j = (r_ij + r_ji) / (2 (1 - cos(theta)) n_i)

Two details make this work rather than merely exist. Pivot on the largest diagonal. Every other component is obtained by dividing by ni, and the three squares sum to 1, so the largest is at least 1/3 and the divisor at least 1/√3. Pivot on the smallest instead and an axis lying close to a coordinate plane divides by something near zero — trading one catastrophe for another.

And squares have no sign. The formula fixes the axis only up to an overall ±. Whatever is left of the antisymmetric part still knows the answer: skew is 2 sin θ · n with sin θ > 0 below 180°, so a positive dot product with the candidate means we guessed right. At exactly 180° the dot product is zero and we keep what we have — correctly, because both answers name the same rotation.

Where to switch, derived and then measured

Two routes, each bad at one end. The switch belongs where their errors are equal. The first route divides by 2 sin θ; the second divides by 1 − cos θ, with its pivot at least 1/√3, so its error is about √3 ε / (2(1 − cos θ)). Setting the two equal:

\[ \frac{\varepsilon}{2\sin\theta} = \frac{\sqrt{3}\,\varepsilon}{2(1-\cos\theta)} \;\Longrightarrow\; \sqrt{3}\sin\theta = 1-\cos\theta \;\Longrightarrow\; \tan(\theta/2) = \sqrt{3} \;\Longrightarrow\; \theta = 120° \] eps/(2 sin theta) = sqrt(3) eps / (2 (1 - cos theta)) gives sqrt(3) sin theta = 1 - cos theta, i.e. tan(theta/2) = sqrt(3), i.e. theta = 120 degrees

The last step uses the half-angle identities sin θ = 2sin(θ/2)cos(θ/2) and 1 − cos θ = 2sin²(θ/2); the 2sin(θ/2) cancels off both sides and leaves √3 cos(θ/2) = sin(θ/2). So the threshold is 2π/3.

And now the measurement, which says something slightly better than "the derivation was right".

Why the harness has its own copy of both routes

The engine runs exactly one route per call, chosen by the threshold — so the engine cannot make this measurement about itself. The perturbation is ABSOLUTE, and that is the whole experiment

Perturbing by a fixed 1e-7 per entry is how this harness builds one.

9. The geodesic: 7.1's indictment, answered

Everything so far has been machinery. This is what it was for.

9.1 Three lines

To blend from orientation A to orientation B: find the single turn that separates them, perform t of it, and apply that after A.

\[ \operatorname{slerp}(A, B, t) \;=\; A \cdot R(\mathbf{n},\, t\theta), \qquad (\mathbf{n}, \theta) = \text{axis-angle of } A^{\mathsf T}B \] slerp(A, B, t) = A * R(n, t theta), where (n, theta) is the axis-angle of A^T B

AᵀB is "undo A, then do B" — the relative rotation, in A's own frame. Both endpoints are exact by construction: at t = 0 it is A·I = A, and at t = 1 it is A·AᵀB = B. In between it is the geodesic, because turning steadily about one fixed axis is what "straight line" means in the space of rotations. 

[[nodiscard]] inline mat3 rotation_slerp(const mat3& a, const mat3& b, float t)
{
    const axis_angle_extraction e = axis_angle_from_rotation(transpose(a) * b);
    return a * rotation_from_axis_angle({e.value.axis, e.value.angle * t});
}

And it takes the short way round for free. axis_angle_from_rotation returns an angle in [0, π], so there is no long-way-round branch to forget — where Lesson 7.1 needed shortest_angle_delta applied per angle, three times, because a raw lerp from yaw 170° to −170° performs 340° backwards.

10. What it costs, and why this is not the end

Axis-angle has answered all three of Lesson 7.1's indictments. It has one convention rather than twenty-four; its angle is the distance between orientations; and it interpolates exactly. It would be a strange lesson that then went on to build two more representations, so it is worth being precise about what is still wrong.

10.1 The bill

verify_72 §F — measured as cumulative stages, each one the previous plus the next step of rotation_slerp:

rotation_slerp               31.46 ns/call, and where it goes:
  transpose(a) * b            3.76 ns
  + the extraction           24.95 ns   (+21.18)
  + rebuilding the turn      29.55 ns   (+4.60)
  + composing it onto a      31.46 ns   (+1.91)
the two trig-bearing stages are 82% of the call.

Stages rather than isolated timings, and for a specific reason: the extraction needs a relative matrix, and building one is the first stage, so an "extraction only" loop either measures the product as well or measures it on a fixed input the compiler can hoist out of the repeat loop entirely. The first draft of this section timed a 3×3 product at 0.41 ns — about one cycle for 27 multiplies and 18 adds, which is not a fast matrix multiply, it is five of the forty-five operations it should have done, because reading one element of the result let the compiler discard the other eight.

Thirty-one nanoseconds is not much. A hundred and twenty bones blended between two animation clips is 3.8 µs a frame, which nobody would notice. But a real animation graph blends three or four clips rather than two, and it does it for several characters, and the number that matters is that 82% of the call is trigonometry spent recovering a quantity the representation did not keep. A quaternion carries the half-angle's sine and cosine in its four components; slerping two of them is a dot product, two trig calls and a four-component interpolation, with no matrix and no extraction anywhere.

10.2 The thing it cannot do at all

The cost is an argument about constants. This one is an argument about the representation.

Given (n₁, θ₁) and (n₂, θ₂), what is the axis and angle of doing one and then the other? There is no pleasant formula. There is a formula — the Rodrigues composition formula — and it is a page of half-angle tangents that is numerically poor near θ = π and that nobody uses. In practice you convert both to matrices, multiply, and extract: trigonometry out, trigonometry back in, every time two rotations meet.

That is fatal for the thing engines do most. A transform hierarchy composes rotations at every level, every frame. A physics solver composes an orientation with an angular velocity step thousands of times a second. A skeleton composes a bone's local rotation with its parent's for every bone in the chain. Axis-angle is a fine interface and a fine interpolator, and it is a bad thing to hold a rotation in while you work.

What we are looking for, stated before we go and find it

A representation that composes without trigonometry, interpolates along the geodesic the way this one does, stores in four floats or fewer, and can be renormalised cheaply when it drifts. Lesson 7.3 finds the answer in two dimensions first, where it is a piece of school algebra and not a mystery: a complex number cos θ + i sin θ composes rotations by multiplication, with no trigonometry at the point of use. Lesson 7.4 asks what the three-dimensional version of that object is, and the answer is a quaternion — which will turn out to store exactly the half-angle quantities §8.3's 2 sin(θ/2) has been hinting at for this entire section.

13. Common pitfalls & debugging

Objects shear instead of rotating

Symptom. Meshes stretch and skew as they turn; normals stop being unit; lighting crawls. Cause. A non-unit axis passed to rotation_from_axis_angle — almost always the raw output of a cross. Rodrigues scales the in-plane part by |n| and the along-axis part by |n|², so the result is not a rotation at all. Fix. Normalise at the call site, or notice that what you are holding is a rotation vector and call rotation_from_rotation_vector, which has no unit requirement to violate. Confirm it. max|RᵀR − I| should be under 1e-6; if it is 0.3 you have found this.

An object flips to a wild orientation at one specific pose

Symptom. A blend or an IK solver is stable everywhere except near one configuration, where the object snaps through a large angle and back. Cause. Code comparing two recovered axes near a half-turn, where the sign is genuinely free (§8.6). Two extractions of nearly the same rotation can return n and −n. Fix. Never compare axes. Compare rotations, with angle_between_rotations, which does not care.

A NaN appears somewhere unrelated, three systems downstream

Symptom. An object vanishes; a bounding box becomes NaN; the renderer culls everything. Cause. An extraction that normalised a zero-length skew vector and returned a zero axis, which built a degenerate matrix that has been propagating quietly ever since. Fix. It is why axis_route::no_axis exists — the engine returns a valid placeholder unit axis rather than a zero one. If you write your own extraction, this is the branch to write first, not last. Confirm it. Print sin_angle and route at the site that produced the value.

Round-tripping through axis-angle in a loop drifts

Symptom. An orientation stored as (axis, angle), converted to a matrix each frame and back, wanders over minutes. Cause. Each round trip costs about ε / (2 cos(θ/2)) of orientation, which is fine once and accumulates forever. Fix. The same rule Lesson 7.1 gave for Euler angles: do not round-trip through a representation in a loop. Pick one storage format and convert at the edges. Lesson 7.4's quaternion is the one this engine will pick.

A micro-benchmark reports an impossible number

Symptom. A 3×3 matrix product times at 0.41 ns — about one cycle for 45 floating-point operations. Cause. Two different eliminations, and §10.1 hit both. Reading one element of the returned matrix let the compiler compute only that element; and feeding the loop a fixed input array let it compute all 256 answers once and replay them across every repetition. Fix. Consume the whole result (the harness sums all nine entries), and make each repetition genuinely different work by indexing with the repetition counter. Confirm it. Divide the reported time by the operation count and ask whether the answer is physically possible.

A structural argument built on grep is wrong

Symptom. "Nothing the renderer compiles includes this file" — asserted from a grep, and false. Cause. grep -rln "math/euler.hpp" matches the path wherever it appears, including inside the doc comments that this codebase is full of. Two of the four hits for that string are prose. Fix. Match ^#include <…>, and better, walk the graph transitively from the actual roots — §12.2 does it in fifteen lines of Python. A one-level grep also misses a header reached through two others, which is the more dangerous error of the two.

14. Exercises

  1. Rotate a vector without building a matrix, and find out when it pays. rotation_from_axis_angle amortises two trig calls across every vector you rotate; rotate_about_axis pays them per vector. Write a benchmark that rotates N vectors by one turn, both ways, and find the N at which building the matrix wins. Then predict the answer before you run it, from §7.4's numbers alone.

    Hint

    §7.4 says one sin plus one cos is about 2.1 ns and the whole matrix is about 5.5 ns. A matrix–vector product is 9 multiplies and 6 adds. Read every lesson of §10.1's pitfall before trusting your own timing loop — consume the whole result and vary the input per repetition, or you will measure the compiler.

  2. Move the threshold and watch it cost something. Set k_axis_angle_reversal_angle to 179° (effectively disabling the symmetric route) and re-run verify_72. Which checks fail, and by how much? Then set it to 30° and answer the same question. Report the two failure modes in one sentence each — they are not the same failure.

    Solution sketch

    At 179° the skew route is used up to a half-turn, where §8.4's table says its worst axis error is 6.9e-03° — about 570× worse than the alternative — so the round-trip check degrades at the top of its range while everything else passes. At 30° the symmetric route is used from 30° upward, where it divides by a small 1 − cos θ; §8.4 measures 1.19e-04° there. Neither setting produces a catastrophe, and that is the honest finding: both routes are usable over a wide middle, which is why the band in Figure 6 is wide. The threshold is a choice about the tails.

  3. Give the engine mat3's missing operators, and write Rodrigues the way the books do. Add operator+, operator-, scalar multiply, and outer_product(vec3, vec3) to mat3.hpp, plus skew_matrix(vec3) for [n]ₓ. Then write rotation_from_axis_angle as I·cos θ + sin θ·[n]ₓ + (1 − cos θ)·n nᵀ and check it against the shipped version with the test in §B.2, which already exists. Finally: measure it, and decide whether you would ship it.

    Hint

    Module 8 needs all five of those for the inertia tensor, so this is not throwaway work — but notice that adding them changes mat3.hpp, which puts it inside the reference render's include closure (§12.2). The golden stops being a null instrument the moment you do this, which is itself worth seeing: run it before and after.

    Solution sketch

    The expression version is likely to be slower than both, because each operator returns a temporary mat3 and there are four of them. That is the standard argument against expression-style linear algebra in a hot path and the standard argument for expression templates, which are a large amount of machinery to avoid a copy the optimiser often elides anyway. The defensible conclusion is to add the operators for Module 8, which needs them for readability in code that runs once per body per frame, and leave this function alone.

  4. Find the worst round trip. §8.2 reports a worst orientation error of 2.755e-05° over 50,000 random rotations, on the reversal branch. Search harder — a few million, biased toward the angles and axis orientations you expect to be worst — and report the worst case you can find, the (axis, angle) that produced it, and whether it is where the theory of §8.3 and §8.4 says it should be. If it is not, explain the discrepancy; that is the valuable outcome.

    Hint

    The theory predicts trouble near θ = π for the skew route and near θ = 0 for the symmetric one, and predicts that the pivot choice matters most when two diagonal entries are nearly equal — that is, when the axis lies near a plane bisecting two coordinate axes, such as (1, 1, 0)/√2. Sample those deliberately rather than uniformly; a uniform sweep of 50,000 spends almost none of its budget there.

  5. Make the blend lie, then catch it. Change rotation_slerp to interpolate the axis as well as the angle — lerp from the identity's placeholder axis to the relative rotation's axis while scaling the angle. This is a plausible-looking mistake and the endpoints are still exact. Measure the excess turning with §9.2's instrument, and then explain, in one sentence, why the endpoints being right told you nothing.

    Solution sketch

    The path is no longer a rotation about a fixed axis, so it is no longer a geodesic, and the excess becomes non-zero — how non-zero depends on how far the placeholder axis is from the real one, which is arbitrary. The sentence is the general one: endpoint checks test two points out of a continuum, and every wrong interpolation in this lesson and the last one gets both endpoints right. That is exactly why §9 measures a path length and not a pair of poses.

15. Recap & next

The engine can now answer "what single turn is this?" for any rotation, and perform the answer. That sentence hides a theorem, two derivations of one formula, an inverse with two genuinely different failure modes, and the first interpolation in this course that is provably optimal.

What you can do now that you could not before, stated as capabilities rather than facts:

  • Blend two orientations correctly. rotation_slerp performs exactly the turning a move requires, at constant speed, at every pose — 0.00% excess against Lesson 7.1's 14.10% and 209.5%, measured with 7.1's own instrument on 7.1's own pairs.
  • Measure how far apart two orientations are without building a separate instrument for it, because the angle of the single turn is that distance.
  • Recognise which of a representation's singularities matter. 2 sin(θ/2) is the whole answer: the hole at the identity is multiplied by zero and the hole at the half-turn is multiplied by two. That reasoning transfers — every parameterisation has this question, and most treatments do not ask it.
  • Set a threshold by deriving a crossover and then measuring it, and recognise when the measurement says something better than the derivation did: here, that the crossover is a 45°-wide band rather than a point, and that 120° sits in the middle of it.
  • Integrate a rotation. rotation_from_rotation_vector(ω · dt) is one step of a rigid body's spin, with no singularity at rest, which is where Module 8 starts.
  • Tell a real optimisation from a coincidence — and tell a real benchmark from one the compiler has quietly deleted.

And what is still wrong, because it is the reason Module 7 has two more lessons in it. Axis-angle does not compose: there is no usable formula for the single turn of "do this, then that", so every composition goes out to a matrix and back. And it is expensive to interpolate — 82% of a slerp is trigonometry recovering a quantity the representation declined to store. Both problems have the same shape: the numbers we keep are not the numbers the operations want.

Next: 7.3 — Complex Numbers Rotate the Plane. Before attacking three dimensions, we solve two, where the answer is school algebra rather than a mystery. A complex number cos θ + i sin θ composes rotations by multiplication — no trigonometry at the point of use, no matrix, no extraction — and it interpolates along the geodesic by a formula two lines long. Understanding exactly why that works in the plane is the only honest route to the four-dimensional object that does the same job in space, and it is the difference between deriving a quaternion in 7.4 and being handed one.
