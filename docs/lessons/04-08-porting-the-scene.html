Module 4 — To the GPU: SDL_GPU · Lesson 4.8

Porting the Module-3 Scene

Every piece exists. Geometry, a camera, textures, a depth buffer — all built, all measured. What is missing is the scene: Module 3's floor, its models and its lighting are still running on the CPU beside the GPU path. This lesson moves them, and then asks the only question worth asking about a port — how much actually had to change? Two modules of convention work were done on the promise that the answer would be "almost nothing". A promise nobody checks is a wish, so we count.

1The Problem

Run the engine today and you get a ring of tori. Run it with --software and you get a floor running to the horizon, an icosahedron, two boxes, a loaded model, four lighting controls and a HUD narrating a vertex through six coordinate spaces. The second one is the engine. The first one is a probe — an instrument built to exercise one API feature at a time — and it has been standing in for the engine since Lesson 4.4.

That was the right way to build. Lesson 4.5 could measure the cost of an index buffer because nothing else in the frame was moving; Lesson 4.7 could measure a depth format's resolution because the scene was two flat surfaces at known distances. Every one of those numbers is trustworthy because the probe was small. But a probe is not an engine, and Module 4 now has every piece the scene needs: geometry (4.5), a camera (4.6), a texture and a depth attachment (4.7).

So the work of this lesson is not difficult, and it should not feel difficult. It is bookkeeping — walk the objects, push their matrices, issue their draws. The interesting part is the audit that comes with it.

The claim under test

Modules 2 and 3 kept making decisions on the stated grounds that they would pay off here. NDC ranges and the +y flip (2.11). Depth running 0 at the near plane, so a LESS comparison means "nearer" (3.1). Counter-clockwise front faces (3.4). Column-major matrices, chosen in 2.6 with a claim about what HLSL constant buffers expect. Sampler enums written in 3.9 to match SDL's enumerator for enumerator. Each of those was a cheque written against this lesson.

This lesson cashes them, and counts what bounces. That number is Modules 2 and 3's report card, and reporting it honestly matters more than it being good.

There is a second reason to do this carefully, and it outlives the lesson. Right now this project has two complete renderers that can draw the same scene. That is a rare and temporary luxury — nobody keeps a software rasterizer around forever — and while it lasts it gives us something almost no graphics course has: a reference implementation. Every claim Modules 2 and 3 made was measured against code you wrote and can read. If the GPU disagrees with it, one of them is wrong, and we can find out which. From Module 5 onward the software path becomes exactly that — a reference, kept because a port whose reference has been deleted is a port nobody can check.

2Building Intuition

2.1The bill from Lesson 3.8

Open src/main.cpp and look at collect_triangles. It walks every object in the scene, transforms every vertex, and appends the results to one array. Then draw_triangles fills that array in a single loop. In the middle of that loop is this line:

Lesson 3.8 wrote that line and immediately flagged it, in the field's own documentation, as something that was not going to survive:

"This field is a cheat, and the cheat is instructive. … a GPU cannot do that, because a material is pipeline state and changing it means ending the draw call and starting another. Real renderers therefore batch by material, and a scene with three materials is three draws. … the moment this engine reaches Module 4, this field stops being possible."

Here is the moment. And it is worth being precise about why it stops being possible, because "the GPU is less flexible" is the wrong explanation and leads to the wrong intuition.

A software rasterizer is one thread walking one loop. Between two iterations of that loop it can do anything at all — read a different material, take a different branch, change its mind about culling — because "between two iterations" is a moment that exists. A GPU draw call is not a loop. It is a launch: thousands of fragments enter the machine at once, spread across dozens of cores, in an order nobody controls and with no synchronisation between them. There is no moment between triangle 7 and triangle 8, because triangle 8 may well have finished first.

So anything that has to be the same for every fragment in flight has to be fixed before the launch. That is what "pipeline state" means, and it is Lesson 4.1's argument arriving with a concrete bill: the cull mode, the fill mode, the depth comparison, the blend equation and the shaders themselves are frozen into an immutable object; the material's numbers are frozen into a uniform push. Both are decided before the draw, and a draw is the unit at which you get to change your mind.

Four objects with different materials is therefore four draws. Not four draws because we wrote it badly — four draws because that is the shape of the machine, and the reason every renderer you will ever read has a step that sorts a list of things to draw.

2.2A third rate of change

Lesson 4.5 gave the engine two rates at which data changes: per vertex (slot 0) and per instance (slot 1). Lesson 4.6 found a third that fitted neither — the camera matrix, which is the same for every vertex of every instance of a frame — and introduced the uniform push for it: per frame.

A scene needs a fourth, and noticing that is most of what this lesson does to the renderer's shape. Consider what an object's model matrix actually is:

  • It is not per vertex — every vertex of the crate shares it.
  • It is not per frame — the crate and the barrel have different ones.
  • It could be per instance, if the crate and the barrel were the same mesh. They are not.

It is per draw, and SDL_GPU supports that directly, in one sentence of documentation for SDL_PushGPUVertexUniformData: "Subsequent draw calls in this command buffer will use this uniform data." A push is not bound to a pass and not bound to a pipeline; it is a value written into the command stream at the point you write it, and every draw recorded after it sees it. So the loop becomes: push this object's matrices, push this object's material, bind its texture, bind the pipeline its surface style needs, draw.

Why "rate of change" is the right axis to group by

It would feel more natural to group by subject — a "transform block", a "lighting block", a "material block". Resist it. The camera and the lamp have nothing to do with each other conceptually and they belong in the same push, because both change once a frame; the model matrix and the albedo have nothing to do with each other either, and they belong together because both change once a draw.

The reason is cost. Data pushed at the wrong rate is not wrong, it is repeated — and Lesson 4.5 measured what repetition costs when the camera was hard-coded into a shader because there was no other way to send it. Grouping by rate is how you never pay that again. It is also why Lesson 4.7 put two texture controls into the light block rather than giving them one of their own: they were per frame, and that was the only property that mattered.

2.3A vertex shader sees one vertex

Here is the one that is not bookkeeping, and it announced itself the first time the ported scene drew a black icosahedron.

Three of the four built-in meshes carry no normals at all. cube_mesh(), quad_mesh() and icosahedron_mesh() are positions and indices and nothing else, and Lesson 3.2's ground plane is the same. That never mattered, because collect_triangles has this fallback, inside its per-triangle loop:

A vertex shader cannot do that. It is handed one vertex. It does not know which triangle that vertex belongs to, cannot see the other two corners, and has no way to take a cross product of things it cannot see. (A geometry shader could — it receives whole primitives — and this is one of about three legitimate uses for one. SDL_GPU has no geometry stage, and geometry shaders are slow enough on modern hardware that their absence is closer to a feature than a limitation.)

So the fallback has to move: out of the renderer, and into the geometry. That is not a workaround. It is what every engine you will ever use already does — normals are authored in the modelling package and written into the file, and an importer that finds none generates them once, at import, and never thinks about it again. Assimp calls it aiProcess_GenNormals. Our version is engine::with_normals, and §3.3 derives it.

Watch what that does to a decision Lesson 3.8 had on a keypress. Flat shading and smooth shading were Q — a runtime toggle, because the CPU rasterizer could evaluate a normal per triangle or per vertex as it pleased. Once normals are data, the choice is baked into the vertex buffer before anything is drawn, and the two options no longer have the same vertex count. A runtime toggle became a build-time decision. That is the honest cost of the port, and it is the first time in this course that moving code to the GPU has taken something away.

3The Theory

3.1Fifteen stages, and where each one went

Before writing anything, it is worth laying the two pipelines side by side and being specific about each stage's fate. There are only five possible fates, and naming them is most of the lesson.

Read the right-hand column and the shape of a port becomes clear. Eight of the fifteen stages simply became the hardware's job — the perspective divide, near clipping, the viewport transform, coverage, interpolation, perspective correction, the depth test, the texture sample and the sRGB encode. We wrote every one of those by hand across Modules 2 and 3, and we now delete none of them and use none of them. That is not wasted work; it is the difference between calling SDL_BeginGPURenderPass and knowing what happens inside it. When §3.6 finds sixty pixels the two renderers disagree about, you will know that the disagreement is about a sub-pixel snapping rule, because you wrote the rule.

Two stages are literally the same C++ function, called from both sides. engine::parent_from_local builds the model matrix for the software path and for the GPU path; engine::normal_matrix builds the inverse transpose for both. Neither knows a GPU exists. That is worth pausing on, because it is the strongest possible evidence for a claim Module 5 is about to make at length: a scene description and a renderer are different things, and code that computes where an object is has no business knowing who is going to draw it.

Two were folded into one. The software path went world → view → clip in two matrix multiplies per vertex; the GPU path multiplies projection * view once per frame on the CPU and sends the product. For our 1,225-vertex torus that is 1,224 matrix multiplies saved per frame, and it is available to the software path too — Lesson 3.7 had it and gave it up, because a specular highlight needs the world position and the composed matrix could not produce one. On the GPU we get both, because the vertex shader can output the world position as a varying and the composed matrix separately. The cost has moved from arithmetic to bandwidth: three more interpolated floats per fragment, forever.

One was translated line for line — the shading equation — and §3.6 measures how faithfully.

And two could not cross at all. The cull mode and the material, for the reason §2.1 gave. Everything structural about this lesson follows from those two rows.

3.2What one draw per object costs

Let us put numbers on it, because "N draws" sounds expensive and mostly is not.

The per-draw blocks are object_uniforms (a 4×4 model matrix plus three padded columns of the normal matrix — 112 bytes) and material_uniforms (an albedo, a highlight colour, an exponent and a flag — 32 bytes). That is 144 bytes per object. The per-frame blocks are the camera (64) and the lighting environment (64), 128 bytes, pushed once.

So a three-object scene moves 128 + 3 × 144 = 560 bytes of uniform traffic per frame, which is what the harness reports, and it would move the same 560 bytes if each of those objects had a million triangles.

That is the shape of the cost and it is worth internalising: per-draw overhead scales with the number of objects, not with their size. A thousand crates is a thousand draws and 144 KB of pushes — which is when it starts to hurt, and which is why Module 6 reaches for instancing (one draw, many crates) and Module 9 for a storage buffer holding every object's transform at once. Three objects is not that day.

What sorting the list is worth

The other cost is the pipeline binds. Our scene has three surface styles — a closed solid culls its back faces, a sheet cannot, and wireframe is a third fill mode — which means three pipeline objects, created once at startup. A draw list that alternates between styles binds a pipeline for each; a list sorted by style binds one per style present.

Measured on a three-object scene with two styles: 3 binds unsorted, 2 sorted. Which is a saving of one bind, and is not worth doing for the saving. It is worth doing because the number scales with the scene and the sort does not, and because a renderer that has a place to put the sort can improve its policy later without restructuring. Our gpu_scene_renderer::render therefore does not sort — it draws in the order given and counts what that order cost, next to what a perfect order would have cost. A policy you can measure is a policy you can argue about.

Why the renderer refuses to sort for you

Sorting a draw list has more than one right answer and they conflict. By pipeline, to minimise state changes. Front-to-back, so early-z rejects occluded fragments before they shade. Back-to-front, which is the only correct order for transparency. By distance, for level-of-detail. A renderer that picks one has quietly made a decision that belongs to whoever knows what the frame is for — and a frame usually wants several of them at once, applied to different buckets.

So render() takes the list as given. Module 6's frame organisation is where buckets and their policies appear; this is the version of that idea small enough to see all of.

3.3Normals as data, and why flat costs vertices

§2.3 established that the geometry has to carry normals. Now: which normals?

Start with the picture. Three faces meet at a cube's corner. Each face is flat, so each has exactly one normal, and the three point in three different directions — −x, +y, +z. A vertex carries one normal. There is no way to satisfy all three faces from one vertex, and no amount of cleverness makes there be one: the surface genuinely has three different orientations at that point, because it has a corner there.

So flat shading requires unshared vertices. Each triangle gets its own three corners, each carrying that triangle's face normal, and the index buffer that comes out is 0, 1, 2, 3, … — every vertex used exactly once, no sharing left to do. A cube's eight positions become 36 vertices (12 triangles × 3). An icosahedron's twelve become 60.

You have seen 36 before. Lesson 3.5 loaded a cube from an OBJ file and measured that it produced 24 unified vertices rather than 8, because a file that authors per-face normals forces exactly this split. Here we produce it ourselves, and the two numbers differ only because the file shares corners between the two triangles of each face and our generator does not bother.

Smooth normals, and area weighting for free

The alternative keeps the sharing and asks a different question: what normal should this position have, given all the faces that meet there? The standard answer is the average of the face normals — and the useful subtlety is weighted by what.

Consider a mesh where one corner is touched by one enormous triangle and by twenty tiny slivers left over from a bad tessellation. An unweighted average lets the twenty slivers outvote the one large face by twenty to one, even though the slivers describe almost none of the surface. The result is lighting that ripples along seams, which is a real artifact with a real diagnosis and a one-word fix.

Weight each face by its area. And here is the pleasing part: you get that for nothing, because the cross product you already computed is the weight.

\[ \|\,\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v}\,\| = 2\,\text{area} \]

where u = b − a and v = c − a are two edges of the triangle: the length of their cross product is twice the triangle's area.

The length of the cross product of two triangle edges is exactly twice the triangle's area (Lesson 2.3 derived this when it built barycentric coordinates from signed areas). So if you accumulate the un-normalised cross products and normalise once at the end, every face has already contributed in proportion to its area. Normalising each face normal first — the obvious thing — is strictly more work and throws the weighting away.

A worked example: the corner of a cube

Take the unit cube's corner at (−½, −½, −½). Three faces meet there: the −x face, the −y face and the −z face, each a unit square, each split into two triangles, and the corner touches one triangle of each face plus, depending on the triangulation, a second. Rather than track the triangulation, note what each face contributes: a vector along its own outward axis, with magnitude proportional to the area it contributes. The three faces are congruent and the corner's share of each is the same, so the three contributions are equal in magnitude:

k(−1, 0, 0) + k(0, −1, 0) + k(0, 0, −1) = k(−1, −1, −1)

Normalise: (−1, −1, −1) / √3 = (−0.5774, −0.5774, −0.5774) — the corner direction itself, which is the answer symmetry demands. The harness computes the real thing from the real geometry and dots it against that prediction: 1.000000. Not "close to 1": the two agree to six decimal places, which is what you should expect from a construction that is exact rather than approximate.

Smooth is not "better"

It is tempting to treat smooth normals as the good ones. They are correct for anything meant to look curved and wrong for anything with real edges: an averaged corner normal on a crate rounds off every edge into a soft smear, which is Lesson 3.8's picture arriving from the other direction. Our scene uses flat for the boxes and the icosahedron — which is a faceted solid, and averaging its twenty face normals turns it into a ball — and smooth for loaded models, which mostly ship their own normals anyway and are left alone.

with_normals therefore has one hard rule: geometry that already has normals is returned unchanged, whatever style you asked for. A file's normals are authorship, and a loader that invents over them is the lie Lesson 3.5 forbade.

3.4A 3×3 through a constant buffer

The normal matrix has to reach the vertex shader, and there is a decision hiding in how you spell it that is worth twenty minutes now and two days later.

The obvious spelling is float3x3. It reads well, and it costs exactly what the alternative costs: a matrix in a constant buffer is laid out one column (or row) per 16-byte register, so a 3×3 occupies 48 bytes and wastes 12 of them whichever way you write it.

The problem is the parenthesis. Column or row is a compiler default — column_major for DXC, and row_major if a #pragma pack_matrix anywhere upstream in your include chain says so — and nothing diagnoses a mismatch. A transposed normal matrix does not crash and does not warn. It lights every non-symmetric object in the scene from a slightly wrong direction, which people mistake for a modelling problem and chase for days.

So the engine ships the columns explicitly, as three float4, and the shader rebuilds the product by its definition:

That is Lesson 2.5's sentence — a matrix is where the basis vectors land — written out as three multiply-adds, and it cannot be transposed by a compiler flag because there is no matrix for a flag to have an opinion about.

Having made that choice, we then measure whether it was necessary, because a precaution nobody checks is superstition. shaders/matrix_probe.frag.hlsl declares the same 48 bytes twice — once as a float3x3 and once as three loose columns at a second slot — multiplies a known vector by each, and returns the answers into a float render target. With

M = columns (1, 2, 3), (40, 50, 60), (700, 800, 900) and v = (1, 0.1, 0.01),

the two possible answers are far apart and cannot be confused:

The matrix probe's four results
computedresult
CPU, m * v(12.000, 15.000, 18.000)
CPU, transpose(m) * v(1.230, 45.600, 789.000)
GPU, mul(float3x3, v)(12.000, 15.000, 18.000)
GPU, c0*x + c1*y + c2*z(12.000, 15.000, 18.000)

On this toolchain float3x3 would have worked. DXC packs constant-buffer matrices column-major by default, which is what our storage assumes, and the matrix-typed reading agrees exactly. That is a genuinely useful thing to know — and it is still not what the engine ships, because a default that happens to be right on the machine you tested is the most expensive kind of correctness there is. The measurement cost one twenty-five-line shader; the alternative was finding out on somebody else's machine.

3.5The bug that is invisible on boxes

Lesson 3.6 put the normal matrix on a key, J, so that the difference between the correct inverse transpose and the naive model matrix could be watched rather than described. Porting that toggle to the GPU produced a result the harness flatly refused to accept at first: zero pixels changed.

Two of the three objects in the scene are non-uniformly scaled boxes, which is exactly the configuration in which the two matrices are supposed to differ. The experiment looked broken. It was not.

Here is the reasoning, and it generalises well beyond this bug. Write the object's linear transform as R S with S = diag(sx, sy, sz). Then:

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{naive} &= R\,S \\[2pt] \text{correct} &= (R\,S)^{-\mathsf{T}} = R\,S^{-1} \end{aligned} \]

(using R−T = R for a rotation, and S−T = S−1 for a diagonal.)

Now feed each an axis-aligned normal, say ex = (1, 0, 0):

naive: R S ex = sx R ex   ·   correct: R S−1ex = (1 / sx) R ex

The two results are parallel. They differ by a factor of sx² in length and not at all in direction — and the fragment shader's first line is normalize(input.normal), which throws length away. A box's model-space normals are its own axes, so on a box the bug is not merely hard to see; it is exactly, provably, zero.

Now a normal that is not an axis

Take S = diag(1.7, 0.5, 1.0), R = I, and a normal at 45° in the xy plane, n = (0.7071, 0.7071, 0):

The two normal matrices applied to a 45-degree normal
before normalisingafter normalising
naive, Sn (1.2021, 0.3536, 0) (0.9594, 0.2822, 0)
correct, S−1n (0.4159, 1.4142, 0) (0.2822, 0.9594, 0)

Their dot product is 0.9594 × 0.2822 + 0.2822 × 0.9594 = 0.5416, so the two normals are 57.2° apart. That is not a subtle shading difference; that is a surface facing somewhere else entirely.

Give the scene one object whose normals are not its axes — squash the icosahedron to (1.7, 0.5, 1.0) — and the harness reports 2,160 pixels changing by up to 143 codes. Figure 4 shows both renders: the naive version blows a whole facet out to white.

The general lesson, which is about testing, not about normals

Lesson 3.6 said "two thirds of a typical scene looks perfect with this bug, which is why it survives in codebases". The sharper statement, which the port forced out, is that on axis-aligned geometry the bug is invisible by construction — not usually, not mostly, but as a theorem.

A test scene made of crates therefore proves nothing about your normal handling, and would have reported a clean pass while the renderer was broken. Choose test geometry that is able to fail. This is the same discipline as Lesson 3.1's cyclic planks and Lesson 4.5's deliberately wrong vertex pitch: build the scene that can expose the mistake, then look at it.

3.6How close can two rasterizers get?

Now the audit. Same scene, same camera, same light, same geometry — literally the same mesh_data, imported once and handed to both renderers — at 320×180, compared per pixel.

Three numbers, and each has a different cause.

Coverage: 42 and 60 pixels

Forty-two pixels the software rasterizer drew and the GPU did not; sixty the other way. They form a sliver one pixel wide around each object's silhouette, and they are not a bug in either renderer. Both use the same fill rule — a pixel belongs to a triangle when its centre is inside, with the top-left tie-break Lesson 2.2 derived — but they disagree about where the triangle's corners are. Our fill rounds each projected corner to a whole pixel; the GPU snaps them to a fixed sub-pixel grid (typically 1/256 of a pixel). Same rule, different resolution, and the disagreement is confined to the boundary, which is precisely what you should predict.

Colour: 87% byte-identical

Of the 2,729 pixels both renderers drew, 2,374 are identical byte for byte, 215 differ by one code, 64 by two. That is a strong result for two completely independent implementations of perspective-correct interpolation and a lighting equation, and it is strong because of the convention work: had the depth comparison, the winding, the NDC range or the matrix layout disagreed anywhere, this number would not be 87%, it would be close to zero.

Added after Lesson 6.1: what this 87% did not test

The number above is sound for what it measures, and the sentence before this box still stands: geometry, winding, depth and perspective-correct interpolation genuinely agreed, and a convention error anywhere in that chain would have collapsed the figure. Keep the result. What it cannot support is the broader reading — “the GPU path is correct” — and it is worth knowing why now rather than in two modules.

This harness built its own render target, and gave it an _SRGB format. The shipped demo did not: it returned linear light into a swapchain whose header says the values are sRGB-encoded. So the comparison ran against a configuration the program never used. Lesson 6.1 opens by measuring what that cost — every GPU pixel drawn since Module 4 is too dark, by a factor of 2.3 at mid grey and by thirteen in shadow — and the error is a ratio, savage in the darks and absent near white, which is much of the answer to how it survived four modules and this very comparison.

The transferable lesson is not about colour. A harness that constructs its own configuration tests that configuration, not your program. This one was written to isolate the rasterizers from presentation, which is a reasonable instinct and is why the shape is easy to miss — the isolation removed the exact surface where the bug lived. When a test builds its own setup, ask which part of the shipped path it just stopped covering. 6.1 also gives the fix, which is one call, asked and not assumed.

The 2.5% that are badly wrong

Sixty-seven pixels differ by more than sixteen codes, up to 134. Every one of them is on a silhouette — look at the difference image and they trace the outlines. These are the pixels where the two rasterizers made opposite coverage decisions on adjacent triangles, so one renderer shows the object and the other shows the background, or one shows the front face and the other the one behind it. A large disagreement at a boundary is one pixel of coverage difference wearing a large number.

And the shading equation itself: one float ULP

The comparison above mixes rasterization and shading. To separate them, the harness renders one triangle covering a 64×64 float target with all three corner normals equal, so the interpolated normal is exact and the interpolated world position is computable in closed form. Then it evaluates engine::shade() on the CPU for each of those 4,096 fragments and compares against what scene.frag.hlsl wrote:

A float has 24 bits of mantissa, so one unit in the last place near 1.0 is about 1.19 × 10⁻⁷. The worst disagreement across 12,288 comparisons — including pow, normalize, a reflection and a halfway vector — is exactly one ULP. Two implementations written three modules apart, in two languages, running on two entirely different processors, agree to the last bit a float has.

That number is the answer to "was the software rasterizer a detour?". It was not. It was the specification.

The one-code floor, and a hypothesis that was wrong

One case in the audit looked alarming. Render the ground plane untextured — one flat quad, one uniform colour — and 100% of its 39,202 pixels differ. Every single one. By one code, in blue only.

The obvious explanation is the last step: turning a linear float into an eight-bit sRGB code, which is engine::to_encoded on our side and the render target's _SRGB format on the hardware's. So: measure it. Draw a surface of known linear colour into the same _SRGB target, read the byte back, and compare.

The first sweep refuted the hypothesis — fourteen probes across the whole range, including the floor's own value, and the two encoders agreed on every one. Which meant either the encode was innocent or the sweep was in the wrong place. A finer sweep across the 14/15 boundary found it:

0.004600014.5471515
0.005000015.5571615

Our powf puts the 14/15 boundary at linear 0.004580, where the exact curve puts it. This hardware's converter puts it somewhere between 0.0045148 and 0.0045186 — and then holds 15 all the way through 0.0050, where the exact answer is 16. Everything above linear 0.006 agrees exactly. Every disagreement is in the darks.

Which is what the curve predicts. The sRGB transfer function is linear below 0.0031308 with slope 12.92 and a power law above it, and its slope near zero is more than twelve times its slope near white — so a linear value crosses a code boundary twelve times faster down there. Both sides are approximations of the same curve, and neither is wrong; sRGB is specified as a function, not as a table of 256 bytes, and a fixed-function converter in a ROP is entitled to a tolerance.

And now the floor makes sense. One flat quad, lit only by ambient (its generated normal points down, which §4.6 explains), so its blue is a single number: 0.0045186 — a value that lands inside the gap. One colour, one disagreement, 39,202 times.

What to take from this

Three things. First: "100% of pixels differ" can mean nothing at all, and a percentage without a magnitude is not a measurement. Second: a per-pixel comparison across the CPU/GPU boundary has a floor of one code, the floor is worst in the shadows, and asserting bit-identity across it would be asserting something about the hardware rather than about your maths. Third, and most useful: the first hypothesis was wrong, and the sweep that refuted it took ten minutes. Measure the thing you are about to blame.

What the texture does to all this

One more row, because it is the one that will surprise you. The textured floor — same quad, same camera, one bound sampler — is far worse: 66.8% exact, and 14.4% of pixels differing by more than sixteen codes.

Look at Figure 5’s third panel again and then imagine the same for a checkerboard running to the horizon: the disagreements sit on the checker edges and get denser towards the horizon. That is not the sampler being wrong on either side. It is undersampling — the same phenomenon Lesson 3.9 §7 named when the floor sparkled and deferred to mipmaps. Where one screen pixel covers many texels, the answer depends on exactly which texel you land in, and a sub-pixel difference in the sample position gives a completely different colour. Both renderers are computing a defensible answer to a question that does not have a stable one.

The control is what makes that claim rather than a story: the untextured version of the identical geometry differs by at most one code. Turn one thing off, and the difference goes with it.

4Implementation

Six steps, in dependency order: the geometry gains normals, the uniform blocks gain two members, the two shaders arrive, a renderer turns a list into draws, the demo stops being a probe, and the harness counts what it all cost.

4.1Normals, at import

§2.3 established that the fallback has to leave the renderer. It goes into src/gfx/mesh.hpp, next to flip_uv_v — which is the other function in this engine whose entire job is to reconcile somebody else's data with ours at the import boundary, and which is exactly the right neighbour.

The flat branch writes three vertices per triangle and an index buffer of 0, 1, 2, 3, … — every vertex used once. Note that this is exactly Lesson 4.5's expanded form, arriving for a reason other than a comparison: there, it existed so the cost of not having an index buffer could be measured; here it is forced by the geometry, because there is no sharing left to express.

The smooth branch is four lines of accumulation and the area weighting from §3.3, and the whole of it is this:

Then one normalised_or pass at the end. A degenerate triangle contributes a zero cross product and therefore contributes nothing — the right answer arriving for free rather than as a special case. A vertex no triangle references accumulates nothing and takes the fallback, which is Lesson 1.7's rule about never producing a NaN, and the reason an unused vertex cannot poison a frame.

C++ — why this returns mesh_data and takes mesh

mesh is a bundle of std::span: a view that owns nothing, so this function can be handed the inline constexpr arrays behind cube_mesh(), a loaded model, or the demo's generated floor, without any of them being copied to get here. mesh_data owns four vectors, because a function that creates geometry has to hand the ownership to somebody.

That asymmetry — take a view, return an owner — is the rule this engine follows everywhere and it is worth stating once. Functions that only read take mesh and therefore work on both kinds. Only the ones that produce geometry deal in mesh_data. It is also why view() is never called on a temporary anywhere in this codebase: the spans would outlive the vectors and it would compile.

4.2Two more blocks, and their offsets

gpu_uniform.hpp gains three structs: object_uniforms (vertex slot 1), scene_light_uniforms (fragment slot 0) and material_uniforms (fragment slot 1). Each states its byte offsets and asserts them with packed_offset, which is Lesson 4.6's executable version of HLSL's packing rule — a rule you can run cannot drift away from the code it describes.

Two details worth defending. First, scene_light_uniforms replaces 4.7's light_uniforms for the scene pipelines rather than extending it. The two describe different shading models, and a block that served both would carry fields one of them ignores. The probe's block stays exactly as 4.7 left it, and nothing that worked stops working — which is a rule this codebase has kept since Lesson 2.12.

Second, look at what gets multiplied on the CPU before it is pushed:

directional_light keeps colour and intensity apart because an artist wants to adjust a lamp's hue and its brightness independently — light.hpp makes that argument. Nothing downstream of authoring needs them apart, so they arrive as one product. The same reasoning decodes the object's Uint32 tint to linear once per draw rather than per pixel. Work that is constant over a draw belongs on the side that runs once, and a uniform block is the natural place to notice it.

scene.frag.hlsl is the same exercise against src/gfx/light.hpp, and it is the stronger of the two because those two files were written three modules apart without either being adjusted to suit the other. normalised_or becomes normalize; std::max(0, dot(n,l)) becomes saturate; mirror_direction, halfway and the clamp before pow transcribe unchanged. §3.6 measured the result at one float ULP.

The clamp before pow is not defensive tidying

Both files write pow(min(1, alignment), shininess). Two unit vectors' dot product is a cosine and cannot exceed 1 — but it can round above it, and pow amplifies the excess instead of absorbing it. Lesson 3.7 measured an un-clamped peak of 1.00001. The GPU spells the same clamp saturate or min and pays nothing for it, which is a small illustration of a general point: the things a CPU implementation had to be careful about are usually still worth being careful about, and are usually cheaper.

One line in the fragment shader deserves a look because it is the only place the port genuinely simplified something:

lerp, not if. Both sides cost one instruction, and a branch on a value that is uniform across the whole draw is not free enough to be worth the words. Note also that the sample happens unconditionally — which is fine, and is why the renderer binds a 1×1 white texture for objects that have no image. A draw with nothing bound at a sampler slot the shader declares produces no pixels and no error message; that failure has now cost this project two debugging sessions (Lessons 4.6 and 4.7 each hit it once), and the fix both times was the same: hand the shader the identity element of the feature it is missing.

4.4A renderer that takes a list

src/gfx/gpu_scene.hpp introduces three things and deliberately not a fourth.

surface_style is the part of a material that cannot be a number in a buffer: solid (cull back), two_sided (cull none), wireframe (fill LINE). Three values, three pipeline objects, created at startup. Three and not more, because every additional axis of pipeline state multiplies the count — two cull modes × two fill modes × two depth settings is eight — and that combinatorial growth is the honest reason "just make it a parameter" is not on the table. Real engines either enumerate a small fixed set like this or build them lazily and cache.

gpu_draw_item is one object ready to draw: a borrowed mesh pointer, two matrices, a material, a texture, a style. Note what is a pointer and what is a value — the mesh is borrowed because it lives on the device and somebody else owns it; the matrices are copied because they are small and change every frame. The day that pointer becomes a handle is the day the renderer stops being possible to hand a dangling pointer, and that day is Module 5.

gpu_scene_renderer owns the pipelines, the depth target and the white texel, and its render() is thirty lines: push the per-frame blocks once, then for each item bind what changed, push the per-draw blocks, draw. The only cleverness is that it skips a redundant bind and counts the ones it issues, next to the number a perfectly sorted list would have needed.

And the fourth thing it is not: render() does not sort. §3.2's callout gives the reason.

The wireframe pipeline, and what Module 2 cost

SDL_GPU_FILLMODE_LINE. One enum value, and it delivers what Lessons 2.1 and 2.12 spent two lessons building — Bresenham, DDA, the whole wireframe milestone. It is worth sitting with that for a second, because the reaction it invites ("so that was pointless") is exactly backwards. You now know what the hardware is doing when you set that enum, which is why you will not be mystified when its line-rasterization rules differ subtly from your own, or when it turns out to be slower than filled triangles on tiled hardware. The enum is cheap because somebody wrote the lesson.

Which brings us to the HUD, and to an honest failure

The software demo draws its HUD with SDL_RenderDebugText, which needs an SDL_Renderer, and Lesson 4.2 established that a window is claimed by an SDL_GPU device or driven by an SDL_Renderer and never both. Text on the GPU needs a font atlas and a shader — stb_truetype, Module 6 — and inventing three lessons' worth of that here to print eleven numbers would be the tail wagging the dog.

Exercise 4.8.5 is the way back.

The mesh cache, which is deliberately terrible

build_scene hands back an engine::mesh — spans into somebody's arrays — and the GPU needs those uploaded once and reused. So run_gpu_scene has a cache, and it keys a mesh by the address of its first vertex, holds eight, and never evicts.

Every one of those is wrong for an engine and right for a lesson. The pressure for a real asset system has now been named in Lessons 3.2 ("the awkwardness is the point"), 3.5, 3.9, 4.5 and here — five times, and this is the first where it actually hurts, because a std::vector rebuilt in place keeps its address and changes its contents, and the cache has to carry two extra fields to notice. That is precisely the bug a handle exists to make impossible. Module 5 builds one; letting the pain accumulate first is what makes it land as an answer rather than as a framework.

4.6Measuring a port

scratch/verify_48.cpp is where the numbers in §3 come from, and two of its design decisions are the difference between a measurement and an anecdote.

One import, two consumers. The cache holds the mesh_data after normal generation, and both renderers draw from it — the software rasterizer takes cpu.view(), the GPU takes the interleaving made from the same arrays. If the two paths imported separately, every disagreeing pixel would be ambiguous: is that the rasterizer, or did they start from different triangles? Sharing the import is what makes §3.6's comparison mean anything. The same argument applies to the checkerboard, built once by make_checker and given to both.

Controls, everywhere. Every surprising number in §3.6 has a control sitting next to it. The textured floor differs wildly; the untextured floor — same quad, same camera, sampler off — differs by at most one code, which is what makes "the disagreement is the sampler" a finding rather than a guess. The boxes show zero pixels changing under the naive normal matrix; the squashed icosahedron in the same scene shows 2,160.

Three bugs in the harness itself, all instructive

The software reference had no near clipping, so it skipped any triangle crossing the near plane — and the floor quad's near edge sits behind the camera. Result: the GPU covered 35,532 pixels the CPU did not, and the "port" looked catastrophically broken. The fix was to call engine::clip_polygon_near, which is Lesson 3.3's code, doing Lesson 3.3's job. A reference implementation that has been simplified is not a reference.

The sRGB sweep ran without a depth attachment while its pipelines declared one — undefined behaviour that happened to produce an entirely plausible table. Caught only because the table disagreed with a second measurement.

The first sweep probed the wrong values. Fourteen points across the whole range, none of them near the boundary where the disagreement lived, so the hypothesis was declared refuted when it was merely unmeasured. Resolution matters: a sweep is only evidence where it has samples.

And one thing the harness cannot do, which is worth stating because it is the fourth lesson in a row to hit it. build_scene, scene_object, collect_triangles and draw_triangles all live in main.cpp's anonymous namespace, so the harness cannot link to them and has to transcribe the scene by hand. That transcription is a real risk — it can drift from the demo and nothing would notice. Module 5's engine/demo split is exactly the fix, and this is the strongest argument for it the course has produced so far.

7Common Pitfalls & Debugging

Everything is black, or one object is

Symptom: the geometry is clearly there — you can see silhouettes against the sky, or the wireframe view shows it — but every fragment is the ambient colour.

Cause: zero normals. The mesh carried none and you uploaded it without generating any, so the vertex buffer holds (0,0,0) in the normal field, normalize produces a NaN or a zero, and dot(n, l) is not a cosine.

Fix: run the geometry through with_normals at import. The general form of this bug is worth recognising: a vertex attribute that is silently zero produces a plausible-looking picture, because zero is a legal float. Check the upload log's vertex counts — a flat-shaded cube that still reports 8 vertices never went through the generator.

The ground plane disappears from some angles

Symptom: the floor is there when you orbit one way and gone when you orbit the other; the horizon line is still visible.

Cause: it is being back-face culled. A ground plane is a sheet, not a solid, so both of its sides are visible and SDL_GPU_CULLMODE_BACK removes whichever one you happen to be looking at. This is Lesson 3.4's finding — culling is only valid on a closed surface — now enforced by hardware.

Fix: give it surface_style::two_sided. In the demo, scene_object::closed chooses; press U to force back everywhere and watch the floor go, which is the fastest way to confirm the diagnosis.

One object is unlit, or lit as if it were facing the wrong way

Symptom: a single surface is uniformly dark while everything around it shades correctly, and orbiting the light does nothing to it.

Cause: its generated normal points away from the light, because with_normals took the triangle winding at face value and the winding is backwards. This is exactly what happens to Lesson 3.2's ground plane: its generated normal is (0, -1, 0), pointing down, so the cosine term is zero everywhere and all it receives is ambient. Nothing in Module 3 noticed, because the floor scene there was never lit — it drew a procedural checker, unshaded.

Fix: reverse the triangle winding, or negate the generated normal — and pick the first, because winding is what culling reads too and a mesh whose normals and winding disagree will be wrong twice. The general point: a convention that nothing consumed was never actually being checked.

The whole scene is lit from a plausible but wrong direction

Symptom: nothing looks broken. Shading is smooth, highlights are round, and the lighting simply does not match where you put the lamp — most visibly on non-uniformly scaled objects.

Cause: the normal matrix arrived transposed. §3.4 is why this engine ships three explicit columns instead of a float3x3.

Fix: push the columns explicitly and rebuild the product in the shader. To confirm the diagnosis first, use matrix_probe.frag.hlsl: push a matrix whose transpose is unmistakably different and read both answers back. Never debug a suspected transpose with a symmetric matrix — a great many hours have been lost to test data that could not distinguish the two answers.

Nothing draws, and the validation layer says nothing

Symptom: the pass runs, the draw is issued, the pixels do not change.

Cause, in order of likelihood: (1) no texture bound at a sampler slot the fragment shader declares; (2) a pipeline created with has_depth_stencil_target = true used in a pass begun with a null depth info, or the reverse; (3) the winding is backwards and every triangle is culled.

Fix: for (1), bind the 1×1 white texture — the renderer does this for you and it is the third time this course has needed the trick. For (2), the pipeline and the pass must agree about attachments, not just formats. For (3), switch the pipeline to CULLMODE_NONE for one frame; if the geometry appears, it is winding, and Lesson 3.4 §2 is the fix.

The split view's two halves are subtly different sizes

Symptom: the left picture and the right picture do not line up; one is slightly stretched.

Cause: the projection's aspect ratio and the viewport's aspect ratio disagree. Half a window is not 16:9.

Fix: compute the aspect from the rectangle you are actually drawing into — fit.w / fit.h, after fit_centred — and put that in the matrix. This is precisely what Lesson 4.6's uniform block bought: a compile-time 16:9 could not have survived a split view at all.

Comparing two renderers and finding 100% of pixels differ

Symptom: a per-pixel comparison reports total disagreement, and the two pictures look identical.

Cause: almost certainly a one-code difference on a uniformly coloured surface. §3.6 has the full story.

Fix: report a histogram, never a percentage. "100% of pixels differ, all of them by one code, in one channel" and "2% of pixels differ, by up to 134 codes" are opposite findings, and only the second is a bug.

8Exercises

  1. 4.8.1 — Make the sort worth something. Three objects and two styles saves one pipeline bind, which is nothing. Build a scene of sixty objects alternating between solid and two_sided, and measure the bind count with O off and on. Then measure the frame time and report honestly whether you can detect a difference.

    Hint: you probably cannot, at sixty objects, and that is the useful result. A pipeline bind on modern hardware is cheap; the reason to sort is that the count scales with the scene while the sort does not, and that having a place to put a policy is worth more than the policy's current value. Report the crossover point if you can find one.

  2. 4.8.2 — Move normal generation offline. with_normals runs at startup, every run, for every mesh. Write the generated geometry back out with save_obj (Lesson 3.5) and load that instead. Measure the startup cost you removed.

    Hint: it will be a fraction of a millisecond, and the exercise is not really about the milliseconds — it is about noticing that you have just built the first two steps of an asset pipeline: a source format that is convenient to author and a cooked format that is convenient to load. Module 9 formalises the distinction.

  3. 4.8.3 — Gouraud, on the GPU. scene.frag.hlsl shades per pixel. Write a second pipeline that evaluates the lighting in the vertex stage and passes a colour down — which is Gouraud shading, and is what Lesson 3.6 did before 3.8 moved the equation into the fragment. Compare the two per pixel and count.

    Hint: the interesting comparison is on the icosahedron with smooth normals and a tight exponent (E to 128). Lesson 3.8's argument was that Gouraud cannot represent a highlight smaller than a triangle; here you can put a number on how much of the highlight it loses. Two pipelines sharing one vertex buffer is also the cleanest possible illustration of what a pipeline object is.

  4. 4.8.4 — Find the sub-pixel rule. §3.6 attributes the 42 and 60 coverage pixels to the GPU snapping triangle corners to a sub-pixel grid while our fill rounds to whole pixels. Verify it: change the software fill to keep fractional corner coordinates and re-run the comparison. Does the coverage disagreement shrink?

    Hint: engine::vertex stores int x, y, so this is not a one-line change and you should think about where the rounding should live before you start. If you get the count down substantially, you have measured your hardware's sub-pixel precision from the outside, which is a genuinely satisfying thing to have done.

  5. 4.8.5 — Give the HUD back. §4.5 gave up on-screen text. The cheapest route back is not a font: draw the HUD into the 320×180 software framebuffer and blit it over the GPU's picture as an overlay.

    Hint: a straight blit would erase what is underneath, so you need alpha blending — which means a pipeline with enable_blend and a full-screen quad sampling the framebuffer texture, rather than SDL_BlitGPUTexture. That is also the first post-processing pass this engine will have, and Module 6's stack is built on exactly this shape. pong.cpp already has a 3×5 digit font if you want numbers before Module 6 gives you letters.

  6. 4.8.6 — Break the port on purpose. Pick one convention from §1's list — the winding, the depth comparison, the NDC y flip, the matrix column order — and reverse it on one side only. Predict the picture before you build, then look.

    Hint: some of these produce nothing at all (winding), some produce an upside-down scene (the y flip), and one produces a picture that is subtly, plausibly wrong in a way you would ship (the matrix order, on a symmetric scene). Rank them by how long you think each would survive code review. That ranking is the real content of this exercise.

9Recap & Next

  • The port was an API change, not a maths change, and that was earned rather than lucky. Of fifteen pipeline stages, two are the same C++ function called from both sides, eight became the hardware's, one was translated line for line, two were folded into one matrix — and two could not cross.
  • A material and a cull mode cannot ride on a triangle. Fragments enter a GPU thousands at a time with no moment between them, so anything constant across a draw is fixed before the launch — as pipeline state or as a uniform push. Four objects is four draws, and that is the shape of the machine rather than a failure of ours.
  • Group data by rate of change, not by subject. Per frame, per draw, per instance, per vertex. Our three-object frame moves 560 bytes of uniform traffic and would move the same 560 if each object had a million triangles.
  • A vertex shader sees one vertex, so the face-normal fallback moved out of the renderer and into the importer. Flat shading forces unshared vertices — a cube's 8 positions become 36 — which turned Lesson 3.8's runtime toggle into a build-time decision. Area weighting comes free, because the cross product's length is twice the triangle's area.
  • The naive normal matrix is invisible on boxes by construction, because a box's normals are its own axes and a diagonal scale changes only their length. Zero pixels on the boxes; 2,160 on a squashed icosahedron. Choose test geometry that can fail.
  • The two renderers agree. 87% of shared pixels byte-identical; the shading equation itself within one float ULP across 12,288 comparisons. The disagreements have causes: sub-pixel coverage at silhouettes, texture undersampling under minification, and a one-code floor in the darks where two implementations of the sRGB curve part company. They agree in the configuration this harness built — which, as Lesson 6.1 discovers, is not the one the demo shipped. See the note in §3.
  • Never report a percentage without a magnitude. 100% of a flat floor's pixels differ, by one code, in one channel — and that finding is worth nothing at all.

Next: Lesson 4.9 — Debugging a Frame with RenderDoc. This lesson found its bugs by writing a harness, rendering offscreen and counting pixels, which works and is slow. There is a tool that shows you every draw call in a captured frame, every resource bound to it, every uniform's actual bytes, and the contents of every attachment at every stage — and the reason it comes after this lesson rather than before is that a frame debugger is only useful to somebody who already knows what they expect to see. You now do. Every object in the next lesson's capture is one you created and named.

10Further Reading

  • SDL_gpu.h, the documentation comments on SDL_PushGPUVertexUniformData and SDL_PushGPUFragmentUniformData. One sentence — "Subsequent draw calls in this command buffer will use this uniform data" — is the entire licence for the per-draw rate this lesson adds.
  • Real-Time Rendering, 4th ed., §18.4 "Pipeline Optimization" and §18.4.5 on batching. The canonical treatment of why draw-call count is a first-class performance concern and what the strategies are.
  • Eric Lengyel, "Transforming Normal Vectors" (Mathematics for 3D Game Programming, §4.5). The inverse transpose, derived rather than asserted — and worth reading now that §3.5 has shown you the exact class of geometry on which it does not matter.
  • Íñigo Quílez, "Normals for a mesh". A short, sharp treatment of area weighting versus angle weighting for smooth normals, including the cases where each one misbehaves.
  • IEC 61966-2-1 (the sRGB standard) and the Vulkan specification's section on sRGB conversions, which is explicit that implementations are held to a tolerance rather than to a bit-exact result. §3.6's one-code floor is that tolerance, measured.
  • Fabian Giesen, "A trip through the Graphics Pipeline 2011", parts 6 and 7, on triangle setup and rasterization. The best available account of sub-pixel precision and fill rules on real hardware, and the direct explanation of §3.6's coverage sliver.
  • Our own src/gfx/light.hpp and shaders/scene.frag.hlsl, read side by side. This is not padding: the exercise of confirming for yourself that the two are the same equation is worth more than any external reference in this list.
