EPA: Penetration Depth

8.5 ended on an IOU. collide(convex, convex) returned +0 when two shapes overlapped and its own doc comment called that a placeholder, because GJK cannot measure penetration: the origin is inside A ⊖ B and the depth is a distance to its boundary, which the search never looks at. This lesson pays the IOU, and starts by measuring how bad the placeholder really was — the axis 8.5 shipped beside it was not a poor normal but the zero vector, on 200,000 overlapping pairs out of 200,000, because gjk_result::direction is only written on the separated path. The definition that fixes it needs two lines and 8.5 already proved the equivalence they turn on: A and B + t overlap exactly when t ∈ A ⊖ B, so the translations that keep two shapes overlapping are the Minkowski difference, and the shortest one that separates them is the nearest point of its boundary. GJK measured the distance from the origin to the set; EPA measures the distance from the origin to the set's boundary, with the same support function and the same sandwich of bounds run in the other direction — a polytope growing inside the set, whose closest face only ever gets further away. Then the part every write-up skips. Every description of EPA opens “start from the tetrahedron GJK terminated with”, and on the commonest arrangement in a game — two crates standing on the same floor — 8.5 measured that there is none: a four-point simplex arrives 0 times in 50,000, and what arrives instead is a flat triangle. Building the starting polytope is on the critical path, and it is where three of this lesson's four bugs lived. One apex is not enough (the origin ends up on the base, so the lower bound starts and stays at zero — 30,000 times out of 30,000); six faces stitched by hand are not a polytope (52.85% of them are not convex); and the textbook visibility test tears the horizon on 4.17% of crates on a floor, worst case 61.7% low, while being almost invisible at free orientations and never firing at all on random hulls — the third lesson running in which the failure needs the structure a real scene is made of. Ships epa.hpp, a demo that draws the polytope growing beside a ghost of the shape it is about to push free, nine measured sections and 2,028 checks, and a 23% speed-up that came from deleting two default member initialisers.

1. The problem: +0 is not an answer

Here is the whole of 8.5's overlap path, as it shipped: 

    // Overlapping, or out of iterations, which a caller must treat the same way.
    // `depth` is +0 and it is a PLACEHOLDER — the header says so in as many
    // words, because a zero here is the one value that could be mistaken for a
    // measurement. The axis is the last search direction, which is a reasonable
    // guess at a contact normal and nothing more. 8.6 replaces both.
    out.axis = g.direction;
    out.depth = 0.0f;
    return out;

Lesson 8.9 will push two overlapping bodies apart with an impulse along a contact normal, scaled by how far they have interpenetrated. Neither number is there. A solver handed depth = 0 does nothing at all, and a solver handed a wrong normal does something worse than nothing: it pushes along a direction that does not separate the pair, so the next frame finds them still overlapping and pushes again, and the stack jitters or sinks.

But look more carefully at that comment, because the first measurement of this lesson is that it is wrong. “The last search direction” describes something reasonable. gjk_result::direction is assigned in exactly one place in gjk.cpp — at the bottom, on the separated path, after the distance has been computed: 

    r.direction = (d2 > 0.0f) ? -v * (1.0f / r.distance) : vec3{};

Both of the intersecting returns happen earlier and neither touches it, so on an overlap it is whatever gjk_result's default member initialiser left there: the zero vector. §A.2 of this lesson's harness counts it, because a claim like that should be counted rather than asserted: 

  A.2  200000 overlapping box pairs, random orientations

       8.5's axis (gjk_result::direction) was zero:
         200000 / 200000  = 100.0%

A comment that describes what the code was meant to do is worse than no comment. No reader of that function would have gone looking: it says the axis is a guess, guesses are allowed to be poor, and a poor normal and a zero normal look identical from the outside until something divides by it. The fix in this lesson is not to improve the comment — it is to delete the whole branch, which is the only kind of fix that cannot rot.

1.1 And centre-to-centre is not the fallback either

The obvious thing to reach for when you have no normal is the direction from one shape's centre to the other's. It is free, it is never zero for two shapes that are not concentric, and it feels like it ought to be close. §A.2 measures it against the true minimum-translation normal over the same 200,000 pairs: 

       centre-to-centre as a contact normal, vs the truth
         mean  40.15 deg   p50  37.75   p95  78.19   max  90.00
         push distance it implies, as a multiple of the MTV
         mean   1.71x       p50   1.46x  p95    2.4x  max    2538x

       CONTROL — EPA's normal vs the same exact reference
         mean 0.0089 deg   p95 0.0222   p99.9 0.0283
         depth error: mean 2.574e-07  max 3.107e-04 m

Forty degrees off, on average. And the second pair of rows is the one that matters for a solver: the translation you would actually have to make along that guessed direction to separate the pair is 1.71× the minimum on average and can be 2,538× — which happens when two long boxes overlap slightly along their length, so the centres are nearly in line with the worst escape direction rather than the best one. Push a resting box by 2,538 times the depth and it leaves the level.

The control at the bottom of that block is what this lesson delivers: the same comparison against the same exact reference, with the answer EPA computes. Nine thousandths of a degree, and a depth error of a quarter of a micron on average. Where the exact reference comes from is §11, and it is a pleasant surprise: it is 8.4's Separating Axis Theorem, which turns out to have been computing the true penetration depth for boxes all along.

2. Building intuition: the balloon in the dark room

You are standing in a dark room and you want to know the distance to the nearest wall. You cannot see the walls. You cannot feel them — the room might be enormous. The only thing you can do is shout a direction, and a voice tells you how far you could walk that way before leaving the room.

That voice is the support function, and it is all EPA has. The room is the Minkowski difference A ⊖ B; you are standing at the origin, which is inside it because the two shapes overlap; and the distance to the nearest wall is the penetration depth.

Shouting a direction gives you an upper bound and nothing else. If the voice says “you could walk four metres north”, then the nearest wall is at most four metres away — but it might be half a metre away to the east, and you have no way to know. Shout a thousand directions and you still only ever get upper bounds, each one possibly far too generous.

So do something else as well: inflate a balloon.

Start with a small shape you know exactly — you built it out of points the voice gave you, so every one of its corners is a real point of the room. Because the room is convex and all the corners are inside it, the whole balloon is inside it. And that gives you the thing shouting alone could never give you: the balloon's nearest wall is no further away than the room's. A lower bound, at last.

Two bounds that move toward each other is the entire method, and it is the same shape of argument 8.5 made. There, the upper bound was a real vector between two real surface points and it only fell; the lower bound was a supporting plane and it only rose. Here the roles swap. The lower bound is a polytope inside the set and it only rises as the polytope grows; the upper bound is a supporting plane and it only falls. Same sandwich, opposite handedness — which is why the two algorithms are usually described as one, and why this file is two hundred lines rather than a thousand.

Now the question is where to inflate. The answer is the obvious one and it is also the right one: push the balloon out at its own nearest wall. Find the balloon's closest face, shout in the direction that face points, and add the point the voice reports. That does two things at once — it tells you exactly how far the room's wall is in that one direction (an upper bound, and the best one you have so far), and it grows the balloon precisely where it was tightest (so the lower bound rises).

When the two agree, you are done, and you are done in a strong sense: you have a real point of the boundary and a proof that nothing is nearer. That is the whole of EPA. The rest of this lesson is what happens when you try to write it down.

3. What penetration depth actually is

“How far in do they go” is not a definition. Two crates meeting at a corner go a long way in along one diagonal and hardly any along another; ask three people to point at “the depth” and you will get three answers. The definition a solver can use is the one that says what you are going to do about it:

The penetration depth of A and B is the length of the shortest vector t such that A and B + t no longer overlap. The vector itself is the minimum translation vector, or MTV. It is a definition about motion, which is what makes it the right one for something that is about to move.

And that definition hands you the algorithm in two lines, because 8.5 §3 already proved the step it turns on. A and B + t overlap exactly when some point of A equals some point of B + t:

a = b + t for some a ∈ A, b ∈ B ⟺ t = a − b for some a ∈ A, b ∈ B ⟺ t ∈ A ⊖ B

Read that once more slowly, because it is the whole lesson. The set of translations that keep the two shapes overlapping is the Minkowski difference. Not related to it, not computed from it — it is the same set of vectors, seen as displacements instead of as differences of points. So the shortest translation that separates them is the shortest vector that is not in A ⊖ B, which is the vector from the origin to the nearest point of its boundary.

Compare that with what GJK computed. GJK asked for the distance from the origin to the set, which is zero the moment the origin is inside. EPA asks for the distance from the origin to the set's boundary, which is the interesting number in precisely the case GJK gives up on. Same set, same support function, two different questions about the same point.

3.1 A worked example, with real numbers

Take the simplest configuration there is. Box A is a unit cube centred on the origin, so it spans [−0.5, 0.5] on all three axes. Box B is the same cube, centred at (0.6, 0, 0). They overlap.

The Minkowski difference of two axis-aligned boxes is another axis-aligned box: its half extents are the two boxes' half extents added, and its centre is centreA − centreB. So A ⊖ B is a box with half extents (1, 1, 1) centred at (−0.6, 0, 0), spanning:

x ∈ [−1.6, 0.4], y ∈ [−1, 1], z ∈ [−1, 1]

The origin is inside it, which agrees with the boxes overlapping. Now, the six faces of that box are at distances 0.4, 1.6, 1, 1, 1 and 1 from the origin. The nearest is 0.4, on the face x = 0.4, whose outward normal is +x.

So the penetration depth is 0.4 m and the MTV is (0.4, 0, 0). Check it by hand: translate B by that vector and its centre moves to (1.0, 0, 0), so it spans [0.5, 1.5] in x while A spans [−0.5, 0.5] — touching at x = 0.5, exactly. Translate it by any less and they still overlap; translate it by the same distance in any other direction and, since 0.4 was the minimum over the faces, they still overlap too.

§A.1 of the harness runs that pair through the real code, with B nudged to (0.6, 0.15, 0) so the arrangement is not perfectly symmetric: 

  A.1  two unit cubes, centres 0.60 m apart in x, 0.15 in y

       GJK      intersecting   distance 0.000000
                direction (+0.0000 +0.0000 +0.0000)
       EPA      proven         depth    0.400000
                normal    (+1.0000 +0.0000 +0.0000)
       SAT MTV  depth 0.400000  axis (+1.0000 +0.0000 +0.0000)
       exact    depth 0.400000  (double, over 30 signed axes)
       bracket  lower 0.400000  upper 0.400000  slack 0.00e+00
       seed     simplex 3 pts, 2 found, 4 iterations

Note the last line already. The simplex GJK handed over had three points, not four, and the seed had to go and find two more before EPA could start. That is §5's whole subject and it is not a special case: it is what the commonest arrangement in a game produces.

3.2 The same thing, as a minimum over directions

There is a second way to write the definition, and it is the one that makes the answer checkable. For any unit direction n, let h(n) be the support function of the difference set:

h(n) = max over x ∈ A ⊖ B of dot(x, n)

h(n) is how far you can walk from the origin in direction n before leaving the set — the voice in the dark room, exactly. And for a convex set containing the origin, the distance to the boundary is the smallest of those:

depth = min over unit n of h(n)

Why that is true, in one picture. Every direction n gives a supporting plane at distance h(n), with the whole set behind it, so the boundary is nowhere further than h(n) in that direction and the depth is at most h(n). That is the “≤” half. For “≥”, take the boundary point p nearest the origin: the set has a supporting plane through p (convexity again), and its normal np has h(np) = |p|. So the minimum is attained, and it is attained at the answer.

Two things fall out of that form immediately, and both of them are load-bearing.

First, a claimed answer certifies half of itself in two support calls. If EPA returns a normal n and a depth d, then h(n) is computable directly — engine/include/engine/phys/epa.hpp exposes it as depth_along — and if d ≠ h(n) the answer is provably wrong. Achievability, proven from the output alone, with no reference implementation anywhere.

Second, minimality is the hard half and no cheap certificate gives it. Nothing you can compute in constant time proves that no other direction does better. §9 of the harness handles that with two instruments that fail in opposite ways: a falsifier that samples thousands of directions (being a minimum over a subset, it can only ever be too large, so it can convict EPA and can never acquit it), and an exact reference that works only for boxes.

3.3 And the exact reference is 8.4

Here is the pleasant surprise. h(n) is a piecewise-linear function of n — linear over each cone of the difference polytope's normal fan — so its minimum over the sphere of directions is attained at a cone boundary, which is to say on a face normal of A ⊖ B. And the faces of the difference of two boxes are: the faces of A, the faces of B, and one face per pair of edge directions. Their normals are ±ai, ±bj and ±(ai × bj) — which is 8.4's fifteen candidate axes, in both signs, and nothing else.

So the SAT's minimum-overlap axis is the true minimum translation, for boxes. 8.4 shipped that a whole lesson before EPA existed, and described it only as “the smallest translation that would separate them”. It was not an approximation and it was not a convenience — it was the exact answer, for the one shape whose face list is finite and short. Which makes 8.4 the reference this lesson is checked against, and makes §C's comparison a meeting between two algorithms with nothing in common but the set they are asking about.

§C runs that comparison on 2,000 overlapping pairs, with both instruments at once: 

  C.1  2000 pairs x 4096 sampled directions = 8192000 h() calls

       directions beating EPA by > 1e-4 m:  0
       worst margin:                        0.000e+00 m

       best sampled h / EPA depth (>= 1 if EPA is right)
         mean 1.01864   p05 1.00340   min 1.00032

       CONTROL — the exact minimum over the 30 face normals
         mean 1.000000   p05 1.000000   min 1.000000

Read the two blocks together, because the contrast is the point. Four thousand random directions per pair — eight million support evaluations — never once found a shorter escape than EPA did, but they never got closer than 0.03% either, and on average they overshoot by 1.9%. Thirty enumerated face normals land on the answer to six digits, every time. That is the difference between sampling a minimum and knowing where it lives, and it is exactly why the falsifier can only convict.

3.4 Or check it by doing it

There is a third instrument, and it is the most direct one available: the definition is a statement about translating a box, so translate the box. §B pushes the second shape by 1.01 × depth along the reported normal and requires GJK to report them separated; then by 0.99 × depth and requires GJK to report them still touching. 

  B.1  50000 overlapping pairs, moved by the reported MTV

       pushed by 1.01 x depth -> separated    50000 / 50000
         residual gap / expected: mean 1.0000  p05 1.0000
       pushed by 0.99 x depth -> still hit    50000 / 50000

       CONTROL — same distance, a different direction
       pushed 1.01 x depth elsewhere -> hit   47476 / 47484
       the translation that direction really needs,
       as a multiple of the MTV: min 1.0354  mean 2.93

All fifty thousand, both ways, and the residual gap after the push is exactly the 1% that was asked for. The control is the minimality claim made behaviourally: travel the same distance in a different direction and the pair must still overlap, because depth is the minimum. Eight of 47,484 came apart anyway, and the last line explains them — the shortest translation in those random directions was as little as 1.0354× the MTV, which is inside the 1% nudge plus the two algorithms' tolerances. The arithmetic version of the same check, the ratio never dropping below 1, holds on every single pair.

Both tolerances had to be tightened for that section to measure anything. At the default, 8.5 §F.6 measured GJK's contact margin at 2.11 × tolerance × size — 0.4 mm on metre-sized crates — which is wider than a 1% nudge on a 1 cm depth. The first version of §B reported 46 failures in 50,000 and every one of them was GJK declining to call a 10 µm gap a gap. A test whose resolution is coarser than the effect it is testing measures its own instrument.

4. The sandwich, run the other way

Now make the balloon argument precise. Let S = A ⊖ B be the difference set and P a convex polytope with P ⊆ S and the origin inside P. Then for every direction, P's support function is no larger than S's — that is just what “inside” means:

P ⊆ S ⟹ hP(n) ≤ hS(n) for every n ⟹ minn hP(n) ≤ minn hS(n)

And by §3.2 the right-hand side is the depth we want, while the left-hand side is the distance from the origin to P's own boundary. For a polytope that is the minimum over its faces of the plane offset, which is a number you can read straight off a list. So:

The closest face of an inner polytope is a lower bound on the penetration depth. Its plane distance, nothing else. No search, no refinement — the bound is the thing you already computed to decide which face to expand.

The upper bound is the other half and it is one support call. Expanding the closest face means asking for supportS(n) along its outward normal, and dot(w, n) is exactly hS(n) — an upper bound on the depth, by §3.2's “≤” half. Keep the smallest one seen and it only improves.

So each pass does both jobs with one support call:

  1. find the polytope's closest face; its distance d is the current lower bound;
  2. w = supportS(n) along that face's normal; dot(w, n) is an upper bound;
  3. if the two have met, stop — the depth is proven;
  4. otherwise add w to the polytope and repeat.

The figure runs that loop on the same two polygons figure 2 used, with the polytope drawn at each step. The number to watch is the lower bound, and the direction it moves in: it rises. GJK's equivalent quantity — |v|, the distance from the origin to the simplex — only ever fell, and 8.5 leaned on that monotonicity twice, once as a terminator and once as a corruption detector. The same argument works here with the inequality reversed: every expansion adds a vertex, so the new polytope contains the old one, so its distance to the boundary cannot decrease.

That gives epa.cpp the same two-line check, and for the same two reasons: 

        // MONOTONICITY IS A THEOREM HERE, EXACTLY AS |v| WAS IN GJK — and it is
        // checked for the same two reasons. Every expansion adds a vertex, so
        // the new polytope CONTAINS the old one, so its distance to the boundary
        // cannot decrease. A step that decreases it is not slow convergence; it
        // is a corrupted surface, which is what a torn horizon produces.
        if (f.d + tol * scale < lower)
        {
            status = epa_status::stalled;
            break;
        }

Hold on to the last sentence of that comment. It is a claim about a failure that has not been introduced yet, and it is §7's.

5. What GJK actually hands over

Every description of EPA you will read — every paper, every blog post, every implementation's comments — opens the same way: start from the tetrahedron GJK terminated with. It is such a natural sentence that it is easy to read past. GJK's last act on an overlapping pair is to find a tetrahedron enclosing the origin; EPA's first act is to expand a polytope enclosing the origin; the handoff writes itself.

8.5 §9 measured it, and it does not happen.

§D of this lesson's harness takes the measurement again, one lesson later, with the question put more precisely — not “is it a tetrahedron” but “what exactly arrives, by arrangement”: 

  D.1  terminal simplex handed to EPA, by arrangement

       fixture              pairs   1   2    3      4  tetra
       crates on a floor    50000   0  49 49951      0      0
         ...one tilted 1 deg  50000   0   1  197  49802  49802
       free orientations    50000   0  29  755  49216  49216
       ball in a crate      50000   0  63 1242  48695  48695
       capsule vs crate     50000   0  65 1032  48903  48903
       hull vs hull         50000   0   0  204  49796  49796

Read the first row. Fifty thousand pairs of crates standing on the same floor at arbitrary yaw — which is not an adversarial fixture but the default arrangement of almost everything in almost every level — and the four-point column is zero. Not rare. Never.

The cause is 8.5 §9's and it is exact rather than statistical. Two boxes standing on a floor share an up axis whatever their yaw, so their y half extents multiply the same world direction; support_local's >= 0 tie-break picks the same sign for both; and every vertex of the difference set the search visits comes out with w.y = (+h) − (+h) − 0, exactly zero. The search direction is then also in that plane, and the next vertex, and the next. The set is solid and three-dimensional; it is the search that never leaves its equator. Tilt one crate by a single degree and 49,802 tetrahedra appear.

5.1 It is a triangle, not a flat tetrahedron

And notice what does arrive: 49,951 of the 50,000 are a three-point simplex. This is a detail 8.5 could not have predicted and it changes the repair.

The literature's degenerate case — the one the folklore warns about — is a flat tetrahedron: four points that happen to be coplanar. That is not what GJK produces, because reduce_simplex discards any vertex the closest point does not use, and three points in a plane already span everything the fourth could reach. A fourth coplanar point is redundant by construction, so it is dropped before the function returns.

So the input is a triangle containing the origin, and that is much better news than a flat tetrahedron would have been. A triangle GJK reduced to is one whose closest point to the origin uses all three vertices — which is precisely what “the origin's projection lands inside it” means. §E checks it rather than believing it, and it holds 30,000 times out of 30,000. A flat tetrahedron would have needed a choice of which three of its four points to keep, with no guarantee that any triple covered the origin.

The last column of §D.1 is the same data read as a verdict. A textbook EPA — one that assumes a tetrahedron with volume — refuses: 

       CONTROL — a textbook EPA refuses everything but 'tetra'
       crates on a floor   100.00% refused
         ...one tilted 1 deg   0.40% refused
       free orientations     1.57% refused
       ball in a crate       2.61% refused
       capsule vs crate      2.19% refused
       hull vs hull          0.41% refused

Even at free orientations it is 1.57%, which on a scene with a thousand contacts a frame is fifteen contacts a frame with no answer at all. Building the starting polytope is on the critical path, not the error path, and that is why it gets its own section rather than a paragraph.

6. Building the starting polytope

So: a triangle, in a plane, with the origin on it. The polytope must be three-dimensional and must contain the origin. The obvious move is to leave the plane, and the obvious way to leave the plane is one support call along the triangle's normal.

That gives a tetrahedron. It is also useless, and the reason is worth drawing.

The origin is on the base. It was in the triangle's plane to begin with — that is what “GJK reduced to this triangle” means — and adding one apex above leaves it exactly there, on the tetrahedron's bottom face. So the closest face is the base, at distance zero, and the lower bound starts at zero and has nowhere to go: the expansion has no face to push out against, because the face it would choose is already touching the origin.

§E builds both seeds by hand from the public cso_support and measures their starting lower bounds over 30,000 flat simplices: 

  E.2  the starting lower bound each seed gives

       seed              mean      p95       max
       one apex     0.000e+00 0.000e+00 0.000e+00
       bipyramid    9.044e-02 2.234e-01 4.056e-01
       true depth   4.908e-01 9.252e-01 1.000e+00

       CONTROL — one apex puts the origin ON the base, so
       its closest face is at zero: 30000 / 30000 below 1e-6 m.

Thirty thousand out of thirty thousand, and the maximum is zero, not merely the mean. This is not a tail case; it is what one apex always does.

The repair is the symmetric one: take a support point on each side of the plane. Two support calls, four extra shape evaluations, and now the origin has a face above it and a face below it. The mean starting lower bound goes from nothing to 9 cm against a true depth of 49 cm — not the answer, but a real place to expand from.

6.1 And six faces stitched by hand are not a polytope

Here is where this lesson's third bug lived, and it is the kind that is invisible in a code review because the code looks obviously correct.

A triangle with an apex above and an apex below is a bipyramid: five vertices, six triangular faces, three fanning up from the triangle's edges and three fanning down. Writing those six faces out is four lines, and the winding falls out of the order the vertices are in. The first version of seed_polytope did exactly that.

It is wrong, because a bipyramid is convex only when each apex projects inside the triangle, and a support point taken along the plane's normal has no reason whatever to land over the middle of the triangle. When it does not, two of the six faces meet at a reflex edge, the “polytope” is a non-convex surface, and every argument in §4 stops applying — P ⊆ S still holds, but “the minimum over faces is the distance to the boundary” does not.

§E.3 counts it. Convexity is checkable — every vertex must be on the inner side of every face plane — so it is checked: 

  E.3  and the bipyramid must not be stitched by hand

       six faces from a triangle to two apexes is convex only
       when each apex projects INSIDE the triangle:

         NOT convex: 15855 / 30000  (52.85%)

Fifty-three per cent — and only 2.3% of queries produced a visibly wrong answer. That gap is why the bug survived the first full harness run. Half the seeds were not polytopes, and nineteen in twenty of those still came out right, because the very first expansion usually deleted the offending face and repaired the surface by accident. What made it visible was not a failing assertion but a status histogram: 1,172 of 50,000 crates-on-a-floor pairs returning stalled instead of proven, with a lower bound that had gone negative. Counting how your code finishes, not just what it returns, is worth the four bytes.

The repair is to stop hand-building it. There is already a routine in this file that adds a vertex to a convex polytope and produces a convex polytope — it is the expansion step itself, which is beneath-and-beyond and therefore computes the convex hull by construction. So the seed builds a tetrahedron on the first apex, which is always convex because a tetrahedron is, and then adds the second apex through expand: 

    gjk_vertex tet[4] = {pts[tri[0]], pts[tri[1]], pts[tri[2]], up};
    if (!build_tetrahedron(p, tet)) { failure = epa_status::capacity; return false; }

    // The face `down` is beyond is the base — the one whose normal points away
    // from `up`, which after the winding fix is face 0. Handing it to `expand`
    // as the certainly-visible seed is exactly what the main loop does with its
    // closest face.
    if (!expand(p, down, 0, true)) { failure = epa_status::degenerate; return false; }
    return true;

Two lines, and the seed and the loop now share one notion of what adding a vertex means. That is the whole fix, and it is smaller than the code it replaced.

6.2 The other four shapes of input

For completeness, because gjk_result::terminal can carry one to four points and all four occur in §D.1's table:

  • Four points with volume. GJK's good case, and the one the literature describes. Used as is, with the winding fixed by the sign of the triple product.
  • Four points without volume. Does not occur in practice, for §5.1's reason, but the code handles it: of the four triangles those points offer, at least two cover the origin, because either diagonal cuts the quadrilateral into two. The widest one that does is chosen.
  • Three points. The flat case above: the triangle plus two apexes.
  • Two points. The origin lies on a segment of the difference set, which happens on 49 of 50,000 floor pairs. Four probe directions perpendicular to the segment, the widest triangle wins, then the flat case. Four rather than one, because the set can be thin in any particular perpendicular direction and a triangle with no area gives a plane normal made of rounding error.
  • One point. The origin is a vertex of the difference set. Six axis probes, best of six rather than first of six — a first-match loop picks a direction that happens to be barely non-degenerate and hands the next step a segment made of noise.

And if the support function finds no extent along the plane normal at all, the set really is flat — two coplanar triangles, a point against a plane — and its penetration depth really is zero, because the minimum of h over directions includes the plane normal, where h is zero. That is reported as epa_status::degenerate with the plane direction in normal, rather than papered over. A caller pushing along it by zero is doing the right thing.

7. The horizon, and the set that must be connected

Adding a vertex to a convex polytope is a classical operation with a classical name — beneath and beyond, the incremental convex hull step — and it is two sentences long. Delete every face the new point is beyond. Stitch the resulting hole shut with triangles from its rim to the new point.

The deleted faces form a connected cap, like the part of a sphere you can see from outside it, and the rim of that cap is a single closed loop of edges: the horizon. The obvious way to find it is to store which face lies across each edge and walk the boundary. The cheaper way, and the one epa.cpp uses, needs no adjacency at all:

Every edge interior to the deleted cap belongs to two deleted faces and appears twice, once in each direction. Every edge on the rim appears once. So push each deleted face's three edges onto a list, and whenever an edge's reverse is already there, remove both. What survives is the horizon, already in winding order, and the new triangles inherit their orientation from it — which is why no normal ever has to be tested or flipped after the seed.

That identity is exact, and the argument that the visible set is connected is a theorem about convex polytopes and exterior points. Both of those sentences are true in exact arithmetic. Neither survived contact with two cubes meeting face to face.

7.1 The failure, traced

The fixture is as ordinary as it gets: two 1 m cubes, axis-aligned, overlapping along x by a tenth of a millimetre. EPA reported stalled with a depth of 5.774e−05 against a true 1e−04 — 42% low, and 1e−04 / √3 exactly, which is the signature of stopping on a face whose normal is (1, 1, 1)/√3.

Instrumenting the expansion showed why on the third pass:

    expand: TORN (9 edges, 3 visible of 10 faces)

Three faces classified visible, nine horizon edges, and nothing cancelled — which means the three share no edges at all. The visible set came back in three disconnected pieces, and stitching a fan across three separate loops produces a surface that is not a polytope, after which every answer is silently wrong.

Now read the numbers in the dump. One face, f03, is genuinely visible: dot(n, w) is 1.1548 against a plane offset of 0.0000577, four orders of magnitude beyond it. The other four all have dot(n, w) = 5.775690e−05, and their own plane offsets are 5.774461e−05 or 5.775690e−05. The new vertex lies exactly on those four planes — it is a corner of the difference box and they are octahedron faces through it — so dot(n, w) and d are the same number computed two different ways, and the last few bits decide which side of > each face falls on. Two landed above, two below, scattered around the polytope.

This is not a rounding problem in the usual sense; the arithmetic is fine. It is a classification problem: a comparison whose two sides are mathematically equal has no correct answer in floating point, and the code asked it anyway. The exact-arithmetic answer here is that the four coplanar faces are not visible — dot(n, w) = d fails > — so the visible set is the single face f03 and the horizon is its three edges.

7.2 The fix is to ask a question with an answer

So do not ask “which faces are beyond the point”. Ask “which faces are beyond the point and reachable from a face we are certain about”. The closest face is certainly visible, because the caller only reached the expansion by finding dot(n, w) well beyond its d. Flood fill from there, crossing only to faces that are also candidates and that share an edge: 

    int visible[k_epa_max_faces];
    int visible_count = 0;
    if (flood_fill)
    {
        bool taken[k_epa_max_faces];
        for (int i = 0; i < cand_count; ++i) { taken[i] = false; }

        int queue[k_epa_max_faces];
        int head = 0;
        int tail = 0;
        queue[tail++] = seed_slot;
        taken[seed_slot] = true;
        while (head < tail)
        {
            const int slot = queue[head++];
            visible[visible_count++] = cand[slot];
            for (int j = 0; j < cand_count; ++j)
            {
                if (taken[j]) { continue; }
                if (share_edge(p.faces[cand[slot]], p.faces[cand[j]]))
                {
                    taken[j] = true;
                    queue[tail++] = j;
                }
            }
        }
    }

In exact arithmetic this changes nothing, because the visible set was already connected. In float it projects a scattered classification back onto the answer exact arithmetic would have given. The candidate set is small — typically two to six faces — so the quadratic adjacency test costs nothing worth measuring.

On the fixture above the depth goes from 5.774e−05 to 1.0002e−04, proven, with the normal (+1, 0, 0).

7.3 How often, and on what

One fixture is an anecdote. epa_config::flood_fill_visible exists so that §F can run both formulations over populations and report the difference — a debug knob in a shipped config, justified by the fact that the lesson has to be able to demonstrate the failure:

Four per cent of crates on a floor, worst case 61.7% low. The same crates at free orientations: five in fifty thousand. Two convex hulls built from points on a sphere: never, not once.

This is the third lesson running with the same moral, and it has now been true often enough to be a rule. 8.4 §F found that the SAT's degeneracy needs a shared up axis, which uniform random orientations essentially never produce. 8.5 §C found that the textbook simplex solver fails on slivers, which uniform random triangles essentially never produce — but which GJK's own convergence produces constantly. And here: the tear needs the new support point to be exactly coplanar with existing faces, which requires the axis alignment, shared axes and face-on-face contacts that a real scene is made of and that a random fixture destroys. Measure the population you actually have.

7.4 And check the surface afterwards

The flood fill makes the tear rare. It does not make it impossible, so the loop check stays — and it is one line of counting, because on a single closed rim every vertex starts exactly one edge and ends exactly one edge.

Better, there is a check on the result that costs one comparison and is computable from the returned struct alone. A closed triangulated surface of genus zero satisfies Euler's formula V − E + F = 2, and every face here is a triangle, so 3F = 2E and the whole thing collapses to:

F = 2V − 4

epa_result::manifold() is that line. §F runs it at every exit over 200,000 queries across two populations — ordinary crates, and 1000:1 aspect-ratio plates whose faces have normals made of rounding error — and it holds every time, with the worst polytope reaching 32 vertices and 60 faces against capacities of 64 and 128.

Euler's formula also sizes the arrays, which is why they are not guesses. F = 2V − 4 means 64 vertices can never need more than 124 live faces. The number created over a whole query is a different quantity — each pass deletes k faces and stitches k + 2, so a long query creates far more than it ever holds — which is why dead faces are compacted out rather than flagged. Leaving them in would turn the face capacity from a bound on the polytope into a bound on the total work, and produce capacity failures on exactly the deep overlaps that matter most.

8. Implementation

Two new files. engine/include/engine/phys/epa.hpp is the interface; engine/src/phys/epa.cpp is the polytope, the seed and the loop. And one thing worth noticing before either: gjk.hpp needed no change at all. cso_support, simplex and gjk_result::terminal were already public and were already the interface EPA is written against, because 8.5 designed them for this moment and said so in its own doc comments. The handoff being free is what designing it a lesson early bought.

8.1 The answer type

epa_result carries the same shape of answer gjk_result did — a number, a direction, two witness points, and enough instrumentation to build a histogram from: 

struct epa_result
{
    epa_status status = epa_status::degenerate;

    /// Metres, never negative. **The minimum translation distance.**
    float depth = 0.0f;

    /// Unit, **pointing from `a` toward `b`** — `collide.hpp`'s convention,
    /// conventions.html §9e, unchanged since 8.4. Translating `b` by
    /// `+normal * depth` puts the two shapes exactly in contact.
    vec3 normal{};

    /// The contact point on `a`, in world space.
    vec3 point_a{};
    vec3 point_b{};

    float lower = 0.0f;
    float upper = 0.0f;
    [[nodiscard]] float slack() const { return upper - lower; }

    int iterations = 0;
    int support_calls = 0;
    int seed_vertices = 0;
    int vertices = 0;
    int surface_vertices = 0;
    int faces = 0;

    [[nodiscard]] bool manifold() const { return faces == 2 * surface_vertices - 4; }
};

lower and upper are the bracket at exit, not decoration: they are what lets a caller know how much of the answer is proven, and §11 uses them to show that the error never exceeds the slack. seed_vertices is what made §5's table possible. And surface_vertices is separate from vertices because an expansion can swallow a vertex whose whole fan was deleted — it stays in the array and leaves the hull — and Euler's formula counts the hull's.

The tolerance is relative to the SIZE OF THE SHAPES, not to the depth, and that is forced rather than chosen. 8.5's tolerance was relative to |v|, the answer itself, which works because a distance you care about is never zero. A penetration depth at a resting contact is a few microns or exactly zero, and a test relative to that would never terminate on the commonest contact in the engine. So the threshold is tolerance × scale, where scale is the largest difference-set vertex seen.

8.2 The polytope

A face is three vertex indices plus its cached plane: 

struct face
{
    int v[3];      ///< Indices into `polytope::vert`. Wound CCW from outside.
    vec3 n;        ///< Unit outward normal. Zero for a degenerate sliver.
    float d;       ///< Plane offset: `dot(n, vert[v[0]].w)`.
    bool alive;
};

The plane is stored rather than recomputed, and that is correctness rather than speed. The closest face is chosen by comparing d; the visibility test compares dot(n, w) against the same d. If those used independently recomputed planes they could disagree by a few ulps, and a face could be simultaneously “the closest” and “not visible from the point we are expanding toward” — which is a loop that cannot terminate and cannot be debugged, because each half of it is correct.

add_face keeps degenerate triangles rather than dropping them, with a zero normal and an infinite offset: 

    // Relative degeneracy test: `|cross|` has the units of an AREA, so compare
    // its square against `scale⁴` rather than against a constant in metres.
    if (n2 <= k_flat_rel2 * p.scale2 * p.scale2)
    {
        f.n = vec3{};
        f.d = 3.4028235e38f;   // FLT_MAX: never closest, never visible.
    }

A sliver whose normal is pure rounding error has a direction that means nothing, but it is still part of the surface, and dropping it would leave a hole for the horizon walk to fall through. Stored this way it is invisible to both the closest-face search (nothing is nearer than infinity) and the visibility test (dot(0, w) = 0 is never greater than infinity), so it is carried inertly until some later expansion removes it.

8.3 The loop

Twenty lines, and it reads like §4's four-step sketch: 

    for (int it = 0; it < cfg.max_iterations; ++it)
    {
        r.iterations = it + 1;

        const int index = closest_face(p);
        if (index < 0) { status = epa_status::stalled; break; }

        const face f = p.faces[index];

        if (f.d + tol * scale < lower) { status = epa_status::stalled; break; }

        lower = f.d;
        best_normal = f.n;
        best_tri[0] = p.vert[f.v[0]];
        best_tri[1] = p.vert[f.v[1]];
        best_tri[2] = p.vert[f.v[2]];

        const gjk_vertex w = cso_support(a, b, delta, f.n);
        r.support_calls += 2;

        const float h = dot(w.w, f.n);
        if (h < upper) { upper = h; }

        if (h - f.d <= tol * scale) { status = epa_status::proven; break; }

        /* …duplicate check, then… */
        if (!expand(p, w, index, cfg.flood_fill_visible)) { /* …stalled or capacity… */ }
    }

The best answer is snapshot into lower, best_normal and best_tri rather than being read back off the polytope at the end. 8.5's monotonicity block restored a 160-byte simplex when it fired; a polytope is seven kilobytes, so the answer is copied instead of the state. Same discipline, a hundredth of the copy.

The duplicate check deserves its own note, because it is 8.5's bug in a new costume. The threshold is k_duplicate_rel2 = 1e-12f, at float's own resolution, and not the user's tolerance: 

/// 8.5's `k_duplicate_rel2` exactly, and for its reason: this test exists to
/// catch a support function returning a point already present, which on a
/// POLYTOPE is how the expansion finishes exactly — the vertex set is finite, so
/// once the boundary face has been reached there is nothing new to find. Setting
/// it to the user's tolerance instead is the bug 8.5 measured at 2.4% slack on
/// curved shapes, where a support point moves continuously and a genuine small
/// step reads as a repeat.
constexpr float k_duplicate_rel2 = 1e-12f;

And note what that branch reports: proven, not stalled. On a polytope a repeated vertex means the boundary face has been reached and there is nothing beyond it, so the bound is tight even though the tolerance test did not fire. It is how the expansion finishes exactly rather than how it fails.

8.4 The question that was being asked too early

One more decision, and it is this lesson's fourth bug — the one that took two attempts.

§4's lower-bound argument needs the origin inside the polytope. GJK does not promise that: it reports intersecting when the origin is within its contact margin of the simplex, so a touching pair arrives here with the origin a few microns outside. The natural response is to check the seed's face offsets up front and report touching if any is negative.

That version reported a depth of zero for spheres seventy centimetres inside a crate, 19 times in 20,000. The cause is that GJK can terminate on a segment through the origin on a pair that overlaps deeply — the origin lying on a chord of the difference set says nothing about how far inside it is — and the seed then puts the origin on an edge of the polytope, where the incident faces' offsets are zero give or take a few ulps.

The second version scaled the threshold by cfg.tolerance. That made the failure come back when the tolerance was tightened, because the margin that put the origin outside was set by GJK's tolerance, not EPA's — a constant that gets worse as you ask for more accuracy is a constant in the wrong place.

There is no threshold that is right, because the question is being asked too early. So it is not asked. The expansion runs either way — beneath-and-beyond does not care where the origin is — and whether the polytope ever got the origin inside is read off lower at the end, where it is a measurement rather than a guess: if (lower <= 0.0f) { status = epa_status::touching; }. Error against the exact reference then tracked the tolerance properly across four decades, with zero results outside their own bracket.

8.5 Reading the answer off the winning face

The nearest boundary point is the origin projected onto the winning face's plane, n * d. Its barycentric weights on that triangle are also the weights of the contact points on the two original surfaces — the same combination applied to three different vertex sets, which is 8.5's witness trick used a second time and the reason gjk_vertex carries pa and pb into a file that never looks at a shape.

The weights are clamped to non-negative and renormalised. In exact arithmetic the projection lands inside the globally closest face — if it did not, some edge or neighbouring face would be nearer — so a negative weight is rounding at the rim, of order the tolerance, and clamping is the repair that keeps the contact point on the shapes rather than slightly off them.

8.6 And the façade 8.5 left open

collide(const convex&, const convex&) loses its placeholder: 

    // NOTE WHAT IS NOT PAID TWICE. EPA starts from `g.terminal`, so the search
    // GJK already did is reused rather than repeated — which is the entire
    // reason `gjk_result` is 180 bytes rather than 32, and the reason these two
    // algorithms are usually described as one.
    const epa_result e = epa_penetration(a, b, g.terminal);
    out.axis = (length_squared(e.normal) > 0.0f) ? e.normal : g.direction;
    out.depth = e.depth;
    out.axes_tested = g.iterations + e.iterations;
    return out;

separation does not change shape. A caller written against 8.4's box test can be handed the general one and will not notice, except that the number on the overlapping side has stopped being zero.

9. Watching it, and breaking it on purpose

“Expand the polytope toward its closest face” is a sentence. The thing it describes is a shape growing until it presses against a wall you cannot see, and that is worth watching.

demos/epa has two panels. The right one is 8.5's difference-set view, with the silhouette traced through the set's own support function and the origin as a white cross — except that now the cross is inside, because the shapes overlap. The gold mesh is the expanding polytope and the pink line runs from the origin to its closest face.

The left panel is new, and it earns its place by making the answer a position rather than a number. It draws a ghost of the second shape at the place the current answer would push it to. Step the search with [S] and watch the ghost climb out: while the lower bound is short the ghost is still buried in the first shape, which is what an unconverged lower bound is. A number that is 60% of the answer is an abstraction; a crate that is still 40% inside a wall is not.

And the pink line only ever gets longer. 8.5's demo drew the same vector and it only ever got shorter. Same panel, same colour, same set — and the direction of travel is reversed, because one algorithm is walking in from outside and the other is pushing out from inside. Putting the two demos side by side is the fastest way to see that EPA is not a separate algorithm but the same sandwich with the bread swapped.

Preset 3 is the one to sit with: two crates standing on the same floor, §5's arrangement. The panel reports seed vertices: 2 extra, which is the two support calls the flat triangle needed before there was a polytope at all. And [F] drops the flood fill from the visibility test — the textbook formulation — so §7's failure can be watched rather than read about: the status flips to stalled and the ghost stops short of coming free.

The demo builds its own polytope rather than only calling epa_penetration, for the same reason 8.5's demo drove its own loop: a single-stepping visualiser needs the iteration, not the answer. It uses only cso_support, which gjk.hpp exposes for exactly this, so 5.1's boundary is intact — and the panel prints the real epa_penetration's answer beside its own, so the picture and the engine can be seen to agree.

10. Polytopes terminate, curves do not

8.5 §9 asked which shapes make GJK finish and which only make it converge, and the answer was crisp: a polytope has finitely many vertices, so a search that improves the simplex can only improve it finitely often; a curved shape has infinitely many, so the error halves forever. Box, hull and sphere were flat against tolerance; only the capsule genuinely converged.

Ask the same question of EPA and one row changes places dramatically.

Box and hull are flat, for the same reason as before with the inequality reversed: the polytope's vertices are vertices of the difference set, that set is finite, and once the boundary face has been found there is nothing beyond it. The tolerance never stopped them — the duplicate check did.

And the sphere, which was GJK's easiest case at exactly one iteration, is EPA's worst by a factor of six, climbing from 28.6 expansions at a loose tolerance to the 60-expansion cap at a tight one.

Same shape, same support function, opposite behaviour — and the asymmetry is geometric rather than numerical. The nearest point of a ball to an exterior point lies on the line to its centre, so GJK's very first support call, taken along the centre-to-centre direction, lands on the answer. There is nothing to refine. But the nearest boundary point from inside is on a sphere of directions, and EPA's polytope approximates that boundary with flat triangles: every triangle is a chord, every chord under-reaches, and no finite number of them is ever exact. GJK walks to a curved surface; EPA has to cover it.

Which is the concrete argument for keeping 8.4's closed forms rather than replacing them with the general algorithm. collide(sphere, sphere) is a subtraction and a square root and it is exact; EPA needs sixty expansions to get within a tolerance of the same number. Generality is a property of the interface, not an instruction to use it everywhere.

10.1 Which makes the iteration cap load-bearing

A cap on a loop that terminates is defensive programming. A cap on a loop that only converges is what turns “converges” into “returns”, so its value is a real decision. §G.2 measures it on a ball deep inside a crate — the worst case there is — against 8.4's exact closed form: 

  G.2  the cap, on the shape that never finishes

       a ball deep inside a crate, tolerance 1e-4

       cap    hit the cap   mean depth error
        12    51.55%        8.866e-03 m
        20    11.12%        6.347e-04 m
        32     0.86%        1.609e-05 m
        48     0.01%        9.391e-06 m

Thirty-two is the shipped default, and the last row is why. Going to 48 removes 98% of the remaining cap hits and improves the answer by 1.6 microns — because at that point the error is no longer the iteration count, it is the flat-triangle approximation of a curved surface. Buying accuracy you cannot use with work you do pay for is the wrong trade.

11. How close, and where it gives out

§H asks the question that a tolerance is supposed to answer: if I set it to x, what do I get? The reference is §3.3's exact box MTV, computed in double over the thirty signed face normals of the difference polytope.

The maximum error tracks tolerance × size to within a factor of 1.4 across four decades, which is 8.5 §11's finding inherited intact: tolerance is a relative quantity, not a distance, and “set it to a millimetre” remains a category error. The two tightest rows flatten out at about 5e−08 because that is where float itself runs out on metre-sized geometry, not where the algorithm does.

11.1 The shallow end, which is where a physics engine lives

A resting contact has a penetration depth of microns. §H.2 walks two cubes face to face from a 10 cm overlap down to nothing:

       overlap      status      depth        error
       1e-01  proven       1.000e-01  +2.235e-08
       1e-02  proven       1.000e-02  -9.313e-09
       1e-03  proven       1.000e-03  -1.292e-08

Seven decades, with an absolute error that stays at a few times 1e−08 — which is not tolerance × size and is much better than it. The reason is §10's in reverse: two cubes face to face produce a difference set that is a box, the answer is on a genuine face of it, and the expansion reaches that face exactly. Curvature is what costs accuracy; flat contacts, which is most of them, are free.

The last row is the honest one. At zero overlap the status is touching rather than proven, the depth is zero, and the normal is still the direction along which the two are least overlapping — which is what a solver wants at a resting contact: nothing to push out now, and a direction to push along the moment they press together.

This table is the one that changed the design. Its first version had stalled and 5.774e−05 in the 1e−04 row, 42% low, which is §7's torn horizon. Its second had them back again at tight tolerances, which is §8.4's containment question asked too early. Two of this lesson's four bugs were found by one fixture that took nine lines to write, because it walked a parameter across decades instead of sampling it at random. A sweep finds what a sample misses.

11.2 And 8.5's precision cure, inherited

8.4 §11 measured a 1 mm gap between two boxes decaying to exactly zero at 100 km from the world origin. 8.5 §11 built the cure into convex: every support point is returned relative to its own shape's centre, so the single world-sized subtraction b.origin − a.origin happens once, outside the loop, where Sterbenz's lemma makes it exact. EPA inherits it by inheriting cso_support, and §H.3 is the check that it really did — two arms which are not two implementations, since the same epa.cpp is in both columns and only the convex view differs.

The relative form holds at 1.5e−08 m from two metres out to 1.8 million; the naive world-space form degrades to a full centimetre. The reference in both columns is the exact MTV of the boxes as float stored them, so the comparison measures arithmetic rather than geometry: what the positions themselves threw away is gone either way, and no formulation recovers information that was never held.

An earlier version of this fixture measured a difference of exactly zero at every distance, and the fixture was the problem. It used half extents of 0.5, which is a multiple of the float grid spacing at every scale below 221, so centre + half stayed exact even a million metres out and the naive arm never suffered. Half extents of 0.37 are a multiple of nothing. A null result from a fixture that cannot express the effect is not a null result.

12. The budget, and two struct definitions

EPA is fourteen times GJK, and that is the shape of the answer rather than a defect. GJK on these pairs runs three or four iterations and stops; EPA runs eight to sixteen expansions, each of which scans every face twice — once to find the closest and once for visibility — and rebuilds a chunk of the surface. The work is genuinely there.

The depth table on the right is the same fact seen from the side: a deeper overlap is a bigger difference set with the origin further from its boundary, so there is more polytope to build. Six expansions at a shallow overlap, sixteen at a deep one, and the cost tracks it.

Which is one more argument for a solver that stops objects getting deep in the first place. 8.10's warm-started sequential impulses will keep a stack settled with penetrations of microns, and the narrow phase is quietly one of the things that pays for it. A physics engine that lets things sink is not merely ugly; it is slower.

12.1 Nothing allocates, and that is measured rather than claimed

The polytope lives on the stack: 64 vertices and 128 faces, about six and a half kilobytes, with no heap anywhere. §I.2 replaces the global operator new and counts, the way 6.17 §9 did, and reports zero over a thousand queries. An allocation in a per-pair loop is a lock, a cache miss and a variance source, and none of those show up in a profile as themselves.

12.2 And then two struct definitions were worth 20.5%

The first working version of this file ran at 1344.04 ns per pair. The shipped one runs at 1068.14, and the two were measured back to back — the library rebuilt with the initialisers restored, timed, rebuilt without them, timed — because run-to-run spread on this machine is a few per cent and a “before” taken an hour earlier would be quoting drift as a finding. The entire difference is this, applied twice: 

struct face
{
    int v[3] = {0, 0, 0};   // ← these
    vec3 n{};               // ←
    float d = 0.0f;         // ←
    bool alive = false;     // ←
};

A default member initialiser on any member makes the whole type non-trivially-default-constructible, and that property propagates into arrays of it. So polytope p; — an ordinary local variable, declared once per query — wrote four kilobytes of zeroes into the face array that the seed overwrote on the next line. Worse, expand's horizon array is three kilobytes and is declared per pass, so a ten-expansion query paid it ten times.

Default member initialisers are a good default and this is where they stop being one. They are excellent on a type callers construct by hand, because they remove a whole class of uninitialised-read bug for free. They are wrong on a type that exists as a hundred slots of scratch space, every one of which is written before it is read. The tell is the array, not the struct.

The third array was left alone, and the reason is in the file: 

/// THE THIRD ARRAY IS LEFT ALONE ON PURPOSE. `gjk_vertex` carries member
/// initialisers of its own, so `vert` below is 2.3 KB that `polytope p;` zeroes —
/// the same waste `face` had. Giving this file a trivially-constructible mirror
/// type and converting at the boundary was tried and measured back to back:
/// **1054.4 ns against 1053.1**, inside the noise, because the compiler can see
/// that every slot below `vertex_count` is written before it is read and the
/// array is touched once per query rather than once per pass. A change that buys
/// nothing still costs a reader, so it was reverted.

Two of the three mattered and one did not, and the difference between them is not obvious from the source: it is once-per-pass against once-per-query, and whether the compiler could see far enough to elide it. Which is the whole argument for measuring the change rather than reasoning about it — and for measuring the change you did not make, so the file can say why.

13. What this does not do

Three honest limits, all of which are somebody else's lesson.

One contact point is not a contact manifold. EPA returns a single pair of witness points, and for a box resting flat on the floor that is one corner of a square. A solver given one point will rock the box on that corner; it needs four, and it needs them to be the same four next frame or the box will judder as they swap. That is 8.7, which clips the two contacting faces against each other to generate the manifold and keys it on feature ids so it persists.

The normal is discontinuous, and no amount of care fixes it. The depth is a continuous function of the configuration, but the direction that achieves it jumps the moment the minimising face changes — slide a box along a wall toward a corner and the MTV swings ninety degrees at the instant the corner takes over. That is a property of the minimum, not of the algorithm; every EPA has it, and it is another reason 8.7's persistent manifold matters more than it looks.

A curved shape is approximated, forever. §10 is not a tuning problem. If your scene is mostly spheres and capsules, keep the closed forms — and 8.8's broadphase will make that dispatch cheap by narrowing the candidate set before anyone asks what shape anything is.

16. Common pitfalls & debugging

Four of these are bugs this lesson actually shipped and then removed, which is why the symptoms are described so precisely.

The depth is always about 58% of the truth

Symptom. Two axis-aligned boxes overlapping by d report d / √3, with a normal like (0.577, −0.577, 0.577) and a status of stalled.
Cause. The horizon tore, so the expansion stopped early on an octahedron face — the polytope's vertices are face centres and edge midpoints of the difference box rather than its corners, and the octahedron they span has faces at exactly d / √3.
Fix. §7. Flood fill the visible set from the closest face instead of taking every candidate. If you want to see the failure first, set epa_config::flood_fill_visible to false.

The depth is zero on everything that rests on a floor

Symptom. proven, depth exactly 0, a plausible normal, and a stack of crates that never separates.
Cause. The seed used one apex. The origin is in the plane of GJK's terminal triangle, so a single support call out of that plane leaves it on the tetrahedron's base, and the closest face is at distance zero.
Fix. §6. Two apexes, one on each side.

The lower bound goes negative

Symptom. stalled, with a closest-face distance below zero on a pair that is definitely overlapping. Rare — a couple of per cent — and only on flat seeds.
Cause. The starting bipyramid was stitched by hand and is not convex, because an apex projected outside the triangle.
Fix. §6.1. Build the tetrahedron on the first apex and add the second through the same expansion the main loop uses.

A deeply buried sphere reports a depth of zero

Symptom. touching, depth 0, on a ball 70 cm inside a crate. 19 in 20,000, and the rate rises as you tighten the tolerance.
Cause. A containment check on the seed's face offsets, run before the expansion. GJK can terminate on a segment through the origin on a deep overlap, which puts the origin on an edge of the seed where the offsets are zero to within a few ulps.
Fix. §8.4. Do not ask. Let the expansion run and read lower at the end.

The normal is right but the contact points are not on the shapes

Symptom. point_a and point_b float slightly off the surfaces, and point_a − point_b is not quite normal × depth.
Cause. The barycentric weights of the projected origin came out slightly negative at the rim of the winning face and were used as they stood.
Fix. Clamp to non-negative and renormalise. In exact arithmetic the projection is inside the globally closest face, so a negative weight is rounding of order the tolerance.

Everything works and it is inexplicably slow

Symptom. A narrow phase whose profile shows time in a function with no obvious hot loop.
Cause. A default member initialiser on a struct you hold an array of, so every declaration of the containing type memsets kilobytes.
Fix. §12.2, and measure it back to back: 1344.04 ns to 1068.14 ns here, from deleting four initialisers.

It hits the iteration cap on spheres

Symptom. iteration_limit, on about 1% of deep sphere overlaps.
Cause. Not a bug. A ball has no faces, so EPA converges rather than terminates and a cap will always be reached sometimes.
Fix. Use 8.4's closed forms for spheres, which is why they are still there. Raising the cap does not help: §G.2 measures 48 against 32 as 1.6 microns of accuracy.

17. Exercises

  1. Do the arithmetic by hand. Box A is the unit cube at the origin. Box B is the unit cube centred at (0.7, 0.4, 0). Write down the Minkowski difference (it is another axis-aligned box — say which), list the distances from the origin to its six faces, and give the penetration depth and the MTV. Then check it against collide(obb, obb).

    Hint. The difference of two axis-aligned boxes has half extents hA + hB and centre cA − cB. The answer is 0.3 along −x — note the sign, and say what it means for which box you would move.

  2. Break it on purpose, then measure the break. Set epa_config::flood_fill_visible to false and run §F.3 with a fourth population of your own: two capsules. Predict, before you run it, whether the tear rate will look more like the crates-on-a-floor row or the hulls row, and say why.

    Hint. The tear needs the new support point to be exactly coplanar with existing faces. What does a capsule's support function return for a direction perpendicular to its axis, and is it ever the same point twice?

  3. Add a second termination test and find out it is free. The upper bound minn h(n) over the directions tried is already tracked. Add an early-out when upper stops improving for three consecutive passes, and measure what it saves on §I.1's mix. Report the answer even if it is “nothing”.

    Hint. Think about what the expansion does to a face whose support point is already on it. §10's duplicate check may be getting there first.

  4. Make the closest-face search sub-linear, or show it does not matter. closest_face scans every live face on every pass. Replace it with a binary heap keyed on d, and measure. You will need to handle the fact that expansion deletes arbitrary faces.

    Hint. §F measured the worst polytope at 60 faces and the mean far below it. A heap has a constant factor and a scan of 30 contiguous 32-byte structs is one cache line every other element. Predict the sign of the result before you measure it.

  5. Engine challenge: the shallow-contact shortcut. A resting contact has a depth of microns, and EPA still builds a whole polytope for it. When GJK exits through its contact-margin test rather than by enclosing the origin, the answer is nearly the last search direction. Implement that shortcut, measure how much of §I.1's cost it removes on a fixture of stacked crates, and — the real question — measure how much accuracy it costs by comparing against the full path. Then decide, with the numbers in front of you, whether an engine should ship it.

    Hint. §H.2's table is the accuracy half, already written. The honest answer may well be that the shortcut is a bad trade, because 8.10's solver is about to keep every contact in exactly this regime, and a systematically wrong normal on the commonest contact in the scene is worse than a slow correct one.

18. Recap & next

The engine can now answer the question a solver asks. Given any two convex shapes it has — box, sphere, capsule, arbitrary point set, in any combination — it returns how deeply they overlap, the shortest direction that separates them, and a contact point on each surface. The +0 8.5 shipped with an apology is gone, and so is the branch that produced it.

What made it possible was that 8.5 designed the handoff a lesson early: gjk.hpp needed no change at all, because cso_support, simplex and gjk_result::terminal were already public and already had doc comments explaining that they existed for this. The two algorithms share a set, a support function and a sandwich of bounds; only the direction of travel differs.

And four of this lesson's findings are worth carrying forward rather than filing:

  • The seed is the algorithm's hard part, not its preamble. Three of the four bugs lived there, and the literature does not mention it, because the literature assumes a tetrahedron that on the commonest arrangement in a game never arrives.
  • A comparison whose two sides are mathematically equal has no correct answer in floating point. Do not ask it. Ask a question whose answer is stable — “reachable from a face I am certain about” — and the scatter disappears.
  • The failures need the structure real scenes are made of. Three lessons running, now: axis alignment, shared up axes, faces resting on faces. A uniform random fixture destroys exactly the property that triggers the bug, and reports green.
  • Count how your code finishes, not only what it returns. The non-convex seed was invisible in the answers and obvious in a status histogram, because nineteen in twenty broken seeds repaired themselves by accident.

Next is Lesson 8.7, Contact Manifolds and Persistence, and §13 named the reason it has to come immediately. One contact point is not enough to rest a box on a floor: give the solver a single corner and the box rocks on it. A resting box needs four points, and it needs them to be the same four next frame, or they swap and the box judders. 8.7 clips the two contacting faces against each other to generate the full manifold, keys each point on a feature id so it survives into the next frame, and thereby makes possible the warm starting that 8.10's solver needs to settle a stack instead of sinking it.

  • Gino van den Bergen, Collision Detection in Interactive 3D Environments (2003), chapter 4. The fullest treatment of GJK and EPA together, including the support-function algebra that makes both of them shape-agnostic.

  • Preparata and Shamos, Computational Geometry: An Introduction (1985), §3.4. Where beneath-and-beyond comes from, and the proof that the visible set of a convex polytope from an exterior point is connected — the theorem §7 relies on and then has to defend against floating point.
