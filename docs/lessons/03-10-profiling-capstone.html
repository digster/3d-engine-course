1The Problem

The renderer works. Nine lessons have added depth testing, perspective-correct interpolation, near-plane clipping, back-face culling, an OBJ loader, lighting, specular highlights, per-pixel shading and textures, and every one of them ended with a picture that was right. Here is a question none of them answered: is it fast?

Not "does it feel fast". The demo runs at several hundred frames per second on a small framebuffer, which tells you almost nothing, because the framebuffer is 320×180 — about a fortieth of a real one. Make it a real one and the same scene, the same geometry, the same code, runs at 43 frames per second. Something in there scales with the pixel count by a factor of fifteen and something else does not move at all, and if you cannot say which is which you cannot make a single defensible decision about what to change.

So try answering three questions from what you already know. They are not trick questions; they are the questions you would actually have to answer to optimise this renderer.

Three predictions — write them down before you read on
  1. The scene contains a floor made of 2 triangles and a torus made of 2,304. Which costs more to rasterize, and by roughly what factor?
  2. Inside the fragment loop, rank these four by cost: the perspective divide (one division per pixel, Lesson 3.2), the depth test, a bilinear texture fetch (four texels, Lesson 3.9), and converting the final colour to sRGB (Lesson 1.6).
  3. If you made the rasterizer's inner loop 30% faster, how much faster would the frame get?

The first draft of this lesson answered the first one with "the torus, by about a thousand." The measured answer is that the two-triangle floor costs eight times more than the two-thousand-triangle torus — 1,390 microseconds against 172. The second prediction almost everybody gets wrong in the same direction: the texture fetch is expensive, but the most expensive single item is the sRGB conversion, which costs more than coverage, interpolation, the perspective divide and the depth test put together. The third has an exact answer that you can compute before you touch anything, and it is the difference between a week well spent and a week wasted.

None of that is knowable by reading the code. All of it is a ten-minute measurement. That gap — between how obvious performance feels and how reliably intuition gets it wrong — is what this lesson is about, and it is why the method matters more than any of the numbers it produces.

Every number on this page is from this machine

An Apple M4 Pro, clang, -O2, and scratch/verify_310.cpp, whose full output is in scratch/verify_310.log. Your numbers will differ, and some of them will differ in kind rather than degree — §3.3 flags one finding that is genuinely a property of this CPU's cache and not of the code. That is not a weakness of the lesson; it is the lesson. The method transfers. The constants do not, and quoting somebody else's constants is the specific mistake this whole discipline exists to prevent.

2Building Intuition

2.1Three instruments, three questions

"Profiling" is one word covering at least three different activities, and reaching for the wrong one is how people spend a fortnight optimising three percent of a frame. Before writing any code it is worth separating them, because each answers a question the others cannot.

InstrumentAnswersGranularityWhere it lives
The frame budget Which phase of the frame costs what? Coarse — tens of microseconds and up In the engine, always on
The differential benchmark What does this one line cost? Fine — sub-nanosecond, by subtraction Offline, in a harness, with a control
The counter How much work is there? Exact — it is not a timing at all Anywhere; it is free

The counter is the one people undervalue, and this engine already has several: Lesson 3.3 counts clipped triangles, 3.4 counts culled ones, 3.9 counts differing pixels. A count is not a time, but it is what explains a time — and it has the enormous advantage of being exactly reproducible, which no timing ever is. When a phase gets slower, the first question is not "why is this code slow" but "is it doing more work", and only a counter can answer that.

The budget and the differential benchmark are different tools because they operate at different scales, and the reason is not taste. It is the clock.

2.2The clock is neither free nor fine

A timer is a piece of hardware that counts. To use one you read it, do the thing, read it again, and subtract. Two properties of that hardware decide everything about what you are allowed to measure, and they are independent:

  • How much a read costs. It is a function call, and possibly a trip into the kernel.
  • How often it ticks. The counter increments at some fixed rate, and between two increments it reports the same number.

On this machine, SDL_GetPerformanceFrequency() reports 24,000,000 Hz — so the counter ticks once every 41.667 ns — while a single SDL_GetPerformanceCounter() call costs 5.42 ns. Read those two numbers again, because their order is the surprise: reading the clock is nearly eight times faster than the clock ticks. You can ask it what time it is far more often than it has anything new to say.

Figure 1 draws that to scale, and the consequence falls straight out of the picture. One sRGB encode takes about 9.3 ns. Four and a half of them fit inside a single tick. So if you bracket exactly one encode with two clock reads, the difference between them is zero ticks most of the time and one tick the rest of the time — which is to say your timer reports either 0.00 ns or 41.67 ns for a thing that takes 9.3. Not imprecise: categorically unable to represent the answer.

The table underneath is the way out, and it is the shape every microbenchmark in this course has. Time a batch and divide by the count. At N = 1 the whole measurement spans zero to sixteen ticks and the "answer" ranges over the entire scale. At N = 1,000 the batch spans about 230 ticks, the quantisation error is under half a percent, and twelve repeated trials agree to within 0.12 ns. Same code, same machine, same second — the only thing that changed is that the question was asked in a form the clock can answer.

The rule this gives you, in one line

Let r be the clock's tick and c the cost of a timer. Never instrument anything shorter than 100 × max(r, c), which is the duration at which quantisation and overhead are both under one percent. On this machine that is 4.17 µs, and note which of the two terms decided it: the tick, 41.667 ns, not the 13.46 ns timer. The cheaper operation is the one that limits you, which is exactly backwards from what you would guess, and it is why profiler exposes both numbers rather than either.

2.3Measuring changes what is measured

There is a tempting idea that survives right up until you try it: if coarse zones are not detailed enough, put a timer inside the pixel loop and find out exactly where the fragment's time goes. It does not work, and it is worth seeing the number rather than being told.

Take a loop doing one fragment's worth of final work — an sRGB encode and a store — 200,000 times. Bare, it runs at 9.71 ns per iteration. Wrap each iteration in a scope_timer and it runs at 18.08 ns: an 1.86× slowdown, from an instrument whose whole job was to observe without disturbing.

And the damage is worse than the slowdown, because the reported number is also a fiction. The timer reads the clock, runs the body, reads the clock, and adds the difference — but the second read happens inside the interval it is trying to close, so it cannot be part of what it measures. The instrument systematically under-reports by roughly its own cost while making the program run nearly twice as slow. You end up with a set of per-line timings that sum to less than a frame that is itself no longer the frame you wanted to profile.

This is not an argument against fine-grained measurement

It is an argument against getting it by instrumentation. Fine-grained answers come from subtraction instead: build two versions that differ by exactly one thing, time both coarsely, and take the difference (§2.5 and §4.6). The measurement stays cheap because it stays outside the loop, and the resolution comes from the experiment's design rather than from the clock's. A sampling profiler — perf, Instruments, VTune — is the other honest route: it interrupts periodically and records where the program was, which perturbs it barely at all. Module 8 uses both properly.

2.4Median for a frame, minimum for a kernel

Timings are noisy, so you need a summary statistic — and the right one is different for the two instruments, which is the sort of detail that sounds fussy until it silently ruins a week.

Start by noticing that timing noise is one-sided. The machine can always be slower than your code deserves: a context switch, a migration to another core, a page fault, a frequency drop, another process waking up. It can never be faster. So the distribution of measurements is a hard floor with a long tail to the right, which is not remotely symmetric, and the mean — which assumes symmetry — is the one summary that is wrong for both cases.

A kernel benchmark: take the minimum.
There is one true cost for "how long does linear_to_srgb take", and everything above it is interference. The smallest observation across several batches is the closest you got to running uncontended, so it is the best estimate. That is what best_ns() does in the harness.
A frame budget: take the median.
Here there is no single true cost. A frame genuinely varies — the camera moves, more triangles survive the cull, a different number of pixels get covered — and the question is "what does a typical frame cost", not "what is the best frame I ever had". A minimum would report the emptiest view of the scene, which is a number you cannot budget against. And the mean is worse than useless here: one 40 ms hitch moves the mean of 120 frames by a third of a millisecond and moves the median by nothing at all.

The rule underneath both: pick the statistic that matches the question, and say which one you used. A performance number quoted without saying whether it is a mean, a median or a minimum is not a measurement, it is a rumour.

3The Theory

3.1A frame budget is a division

At 60 Hz a frame is 16.667 ms. That is not a target to aim near, it is a quantity to be divided up: everything the engine does in a frame has to fit inside it, and the moment the sum exceeds it, the frame rate halves — not degrades, halves, because with vsync on you either make the deadline or wait for the next one.

So the useful object is not a frame time but a frame budget: a list of disjoint phases whose costs sum to the whole. Ours has six, chosen so that each is a phase a reader can name and each lasts long enough to measure honestly (§2.2):

ZoneWhat it coversScales with
buildPlacing objects; building transformsObject count
collectModel → world → view → clip → NDC → screen, clipping, per-vertex lightingTriangles
sortThe painter's-algorithm depth sort, when it runs at allTriangles · log(triangles)
fillRasterization: coverage, depth, interpolation, texturing, shadingCovered pixels
overlayDebug draw and HUDHow much you are drawing
presentThe row-by-row copy into the streaming textureFramebuffer pixels

Two design constraints on that list are load-bearing, and both are about arithmetic rather than taste. The zones must be disjoint — if two are ever running at once, the same nanoseconds get counted twice and the total exceeds the frame, so profiler::zones_overlapped() exists to catch it. And they must cover the frame, or the sum means nothing. Nothing enforces coverage, which is why §3.2 exists.

Figure 2 is that budget, measured, on the capstone scene at two resolutions. Read the fill bar first — 96.1% of the frame at 320×180, and 99.2% at 720p — and then read the thing the figure exists to show, which is the row above it. collect measures 53.08 µs at the small size and 55.04 µs at the large one. Sixteen times as many pixels moved it by 3.7%, which is noise. Over the same change fill went from 1.55 ms to 23.24 ms.

That is one measurement carrying two facts. The obvious one is that the fill dominates. The useful one is why: the two phases are not on the same axis at all, and §3.3 is what that means.

3.2The row everybody forgets

Add up the six zones and subtract from the frame. Whatever is left is time your program spent doing something you are not measuring: the event drain, the fixed-timestep loop, an allocation, a cache miss on the way into a zone, a bit of glue between phases.

Display it. This is the single most common way a frame budget lies, and it lies by omission: six honest bars adding to 60% of the frame look like a complete picture, right up to the moment you make the biggest of them twice as fast and the frame improves by nine percent. The 40% you never charted was always the ceiling on your work, and you could have known that on day one.

Ours is 3.17 µs of 1,610.88 — 0.2%, which is a good remainder and means the six zones really do cover the frame. But it is 0.2% because it was checked, and a remainder is the kind of number that only stays small while somebody is looking at it.

A sum of medians is not the median of a sum

other_ns() subtracts a sum of per-zone medians from the median frame, and those two are not the same statistic — they differ whenever two zones peak on different frames. The disagreement is small and it is honest; the alternative would be to derive one of the numbers from the others so the table adds up perfectly, which is how a budget stops being a measurement. If the arithmetic ever has to look tidy, measure the total separately and let the discrepancy show.

3.3Pixels per triangle is the axis

Here is the intuition that Figure 2 was quietly demolishing. Ask someone how expensive a mesh is and they will tell you how many triangles it has. It is the number every modelling tool displays, every asset pipeline reports, and every artist is asked to reduce. And for the rasterizer it is close to irrelevant.

The reason is structural, and once seen it cannot be unseen. Work in a renderer happens at two frequencies:

\[ T_{\text{frame}} \;\approx\; n_{\text{tri}} \cdot c_{\text{vertex}} \;+\; n_{\text{px}} \cdot c_{\text{pixel}} \]

frame time ≈ (how many triangles × what a triangle costs) + (how many covered pixels × what a pixel costs)

A triangle covering 200 pixels pays its vertex cost once and its pixel cost 200 times. A triangle covering 0.3 of a pixel pays its vertex cost once and its pixel cost about a third of a time. The ratio between those two costs is set entirely by one number, which has a name worth using out loud: pixels per triangle.

Figure 3 sweeps both axes independently, and the two panels are deliberately the same experiment run in perpendicular directions. On the left, the same torus at increasing resolution: fill is a straight line of slope 1 — double the pixels, double the cost — and collect is flat, 61.75 µs to 63.75 µs across a 36× change in pixel count. On the right, the same torus at the same size on screen, tessellated from 36 to 36,864 triangles: now collect climbs by a factor of 840 and the two lines cross.

Everything about performance advice depends on which side of that crossing you are standing. "Optimise the fill" is correct on the left of it and wrong on the right, and nothing about the renderer changed to move from one to the other — only how finely the same object was cut up. This is also the honest version of a folklore rule you will meet constantly: small triangles are expensive. They are, and now you can say why in one sentence. Below about one pixel per triangle you are paying full price for a vertex to produce a fragment you can barely see, and on real GPUs it is worse than this chart shows, because they rasterize in 2×2 quads and a sub-pixel triangle wastes three of every four fragment lanes.

The number to quote, and the number not to

Nanoseconds per covered pixel is a property of your fill loop. Nanoseconds per triangle is a property of your vertex stage. Both transfer between resolutions, scenes and machines well enough to be worth writing down. Total milliseconds per frame transfers to nothing at all — it is a fact about one scene at one resolution on one computer — and it is the only one of the three most people ever record.

Our two numbers, from Figure 2: 45.47 ns per covered pixel and 23.0 ns per triangle. Multiply the first by 921,600 and you can predict the 720p frame before rendering it, which is a check worth doing — a prediction that comes out right is how you know the model in your head matches the machine.

3.4Amdahl's law as arithmetic

Now the third prediction from §1, which has an exact answer. Suppose a phase takes a fraction p of your frame, and you make that phase s times faster. The frame was 1 unit; afterwards it is (1 − p) + p/s. So the whole-frame speedup is

\[ \text{speedup} = \frac{1}{(1 - p) + p/s} \]

one, divided by (the part you did not touch, plus the part you did, shrunk by s)

That is Amdahl's law, and it is worth deriving rather than quoting because the derivation is the whole content: the part you did not speed up does not go away. Let s go to infinity and the expression does not go to infinity, it goes to 1/(1 − p). Delete a phase entirely and that is all you get.

Figure 6 is that formula plotted, with this lesson's own optimisation marked on it. Every curve flattens against its own ceiling: a phase that is half your frame can never buy you more than 2×, no matter what you do to it. Our fill is 96% of the frame, so its ceiling is 25× — generous, and it is what makes the fill worth working on at all. If it had been 50% the honest conclusion would have been to go and look somewhere else first.

Use it in two directions, and the second is the one people skip:

  • Before — as a filter. Measure p, compute the ceiling, and decide whether the work is worth doing at all. This is the calculation that saves fortnights.
  • After — as a check on yourself. You measured p, you measured s, and you measured the whole-frame speedup. Those three numbers are not independent. If the frame improved by more than the formula allows, you did not discover something wonderful; you mismeasured something. verify_310 §H makes exactly this check, and it holds: a ceiling of 1.284× against 1.287× measured, which is agreement to within the noise.

3.5The differential method, and its control

§2.3 ruled out instrumenting the inner loop, but the question it was trying to answer is still a real one: what does the depth test cost? what does the texture fetch cost? The answer is to get resolution from the experiment instead of from the clock.

Build a ladder. Take one fixed piece of geometry — same triangles, same pixels, same everything — and render it repeatedly with fill styles that differ from each other by exactly one thing. Coverage alone. Then coverage plus the perspective divide. Then plus the depth test. Then plus the sRGB encode. Each rung is a coarse measurement, comfortably above the 4.17 µs floor, and the difference between two adjacent rungs is the cost of the one thing that changed.

Two disciplines make the difference mean something, and both are easier to state than to remember at two in the morning:

  1. Change exactly one thing per rung. If a rung adds a texture fetch and turns on lighting, its delta is the sum of two costs and you have learnt neither.
  2. Have a control. When you cannot isolate a feature by adding it, isolate it by removing it from an otherwise identical loop. This is the one people skip, and skipping it is how the first draft of §4.10 of this lesson reported that a texture fetch costs 6.4× more in situ than in a microbenchmark — a number produced by adding an encode and two stores alongside the fetch and then attributing all three to the fetch. With the control in place the honest figure is 2.27×, which is still a striking result and has the advantage of being true.
A benchmark without a control is a story

Lesson 3.9 §5.1 shipped a table that read "textured is 5.04× a procedural rule" and then a second table, because the first one was measuring the sRGB encode that only one of the two variants performed. This lesson repeated the same class of error in a different place and caught it the same way. The pattern is worth naming, because you will make it again: when a measured ratio is much larger than you can explain, the usual cause is that the two sides differ in more than one way. Write down what else changed, and if you cannot, build the control that holds it still.

Drag the second slider to the far right on the default setting: the phase is 96% of the frame and you have made it 64× faster, which no real optimisation ever is, and the frame is 18.2× faster rather than 64×. Now drag the first slider down to 50% and watch the whole curve collapse — the same heroic 64× on half a frame buys 1.97×. The lesson is in which slider moves the answer, and it is not the one you spend your week on.

4Implementation

4.1The profiler, and what it refuses to do

The new file is src/core/profile.hpp, and it goes in core/ rather than gfx/ for the reason all of Module 5's structure will eventually turn on: measuring time is not a graphics concern. The physics step will want a zone, the asset loader will want a zone, and none of them should have to include a rasterizer to get one.

Start with the zones. They are an enum class with a count sentinel last, which is the standard C++ idiom for "this enum also indexes an array":

C++ — inline constexpr at namespace scope

inline on a variable is a C++17 feature and it means exactly what it means on a function: this definition may appear in every translation unit that includes the header, and they are all the same object. Without it, every .cpp that includes this header gets its own k_zone_count — harmless for a constant, and a real one-definition-rule problem the moment somebody takes its address. Lesson 2.12 met the same rule from the other side with inline constexpr mesh arrays; this is the scalar case.

Now the instrument itself. scope_timer is the entire public interface for measuring something, and it is eleven lines:

Two decisions in there are worth defending. It is a class rather than a matched pair of calls for the reason RAII exists at all (Lesson 0.5): a phase with an early return in the middle of it would skip the closing call and silently lose the measurement, whereas a destructor runs on every path out of a scope including the ones you did not think of. And all four copy and move operations are deleted, because two live copies would each report the interval on destruction and the zone would count it twice. Deleting them makes that unwritable rather than merely discouraged — the same move vertex made in Lesson 2.4, where bundling attributes with positions made a whole class of bug impossible to type.

Then the part most profilers leave out: the profiler measures itself at construction.

That is §2.2's rule made executable. The engine logs both numbers at startup and the arithmetic that follows from them, so the first line of every run tells you what you are allowed to measure on this machine:

Notice that the calibration loop's body is an unnamed-looking object with a name — const scope_timer t{...} — and the name is doing real work. Write scope_timer{*this, zone::build}; without one and you get a temporary that is destroyed at the end of the statement rather than the end of the scope. Here those happen to be the same point, so it would work; in a real zone it would be a timer that closes immediately and reports nothing. It is one of the most common C++ RAII mistakes and it fails completely silently, so the habit is: always name your guard.

One arithmetic detail in to_ns is worth pausing on, because it is the single most common way this class of code is written wrong:

Multiply first, then divide. The other order — ticks / freq_ * 1e9 — performs an integer division that truncates to whole seconds before scaling, so every interval shorter than a second reports as exactly zero. It compiles, it runs, and it produces a profiler in which every zone is free.

4.2The median, in one call

§2.4 settled that a frame budget wants a median. The ring buffer holds k_history = 120 frames — two seconds at 60 Hz, long enough to be stable and short enough to respond when you press a key and the cost genuinely changes — and the median comes out of it like this:

std::nth_element is a partial sort: it puts the k-th element where it would be in a sorted range and leaves everything else merely partitioned around it — smaller on the left, larger on the right, in no particular order. That is O(n) rather than the O(n log n) a full sort costs, and the ordering it does not produce is information we would have thrown away anyway. The copy is not laziness either. The ring is the record; sorting it in place would destroy the chronological order that makes it a history at all, and a profiler that corrupts its own data when you read it is a profiler you will debug at the worst possible moment.

4.3Cutting the frame into zones

In main.cpp the frame is now bracketed, and where the brackets go is a decision rather than a formality:

The frame starts before the event drain, because draining events is work the frame does and a budget that starts after it has a hole in it by construction. It ends before SDL_RenderPresent, because with vsync on that call blocks until the display is ready, and a budget whose largest bar is "waiting for the monitor" tells you nothing about your renderer. The HUD prints the wall-clock frame time beside the engine total so the gap between the two is visible rather than hidden — and that gap is worth understanding, because it is why a frame-rate counter cannot tell you that you made anything faster. With vsync on, it reads 60 either way.

The sort is the interesting one. It used to live inside draw_triangles, where it would have been counted as part of fill and quietly inflated the fragment stage's number. That is worth generalising, because it will keep happening: instrumentation shapes the code it measures. The harness's renderer projects all triangles into a vector and only then rasterizes them, which is not how you would write a naive renderer — the obvious version projects and fills one triangle at a time — and the difference exists entirely so that collect and fill can be measured apart. Pretending otherwise is how people end up with a profiler whose zones interleave and whose numbers cannot be trusted.

A zone is a category of work, not a point in time.

A zone that reads zero

build reports 0.00 µs, and it is not broken. Placing four objects takes well under the 4.17 µs floor from §2.2, so the counter genuinely cannot resolve it. That is worth keeping rather than deleting, because it is the rule from §2.2 appearing in the engine's own output: a zone that reads zero is either work you are not doing or work you cannot measure, and the two look identical in a table. If you ever need build's real cost, it is a differential benchmark's job (§3.5), not a zone's.

4.4The budget on screen

3 toggles the budget panel. It draws a stacked bar rather than one bar per zone, and that is the whole design: separate bars answer "how big is fill?", while a stacked bar answers "what is a frame made of?", which is the question a budget exists for. It also makes the unmeasured remainder impossible to overlook, because it is the gap at the end rather than a row you can skip reading.

The remainder is drawn to the end of the bar so the bar is always exactly full, in a desaturated grey that deliberately does not look like a phase — because it is not one. It is the part of the frame nobody is measuring, and giving it a cheerful colour of its own would suggest otherwise.

The panel covers part of the render, which is not a compromise for want of screen space. It is what a profiler HUD is: it obscures the thing it is measuring, which is why it lives on a key and why its own cost is charged to overlay rather than being quietly free.

4.5The prediction, and getting it wrong

Now the measurement §1 promised. Same scene, same camera; each object drawn alone, timing only the fill.

The remaining 1.42× is itself informative rather than noise. The torus's triangles are tiny — about 1.2 pixels each — so each one pays the per-triangle setup in fill_triangle (the area, the reorientation, three colour decodes, the pre-divides) against barely any pixels to spread it over. It is the same effect as §3.3's crossing, seen from inside a single draw call.

Why get a prediction wrong on purpose?

Because the alternative is to measure without predicting, and then every result looks reasonable in hindsight. A written-down prediction is the only way to notice that your model of the machine is wrong — and noticing that is worth more than the measurement, because the model is what you will use on the next question, when there is no time to measure. Do this every time. Write the number down where you cannot revise it.

Lesson 3.2 introduced it with an honest warning — "one divide per pixel, and it is the honest cost of this lesson" — and the measurement says the warning was unnecessary. 4.7Four candidates for one function

So: make linear_to_srgb cheaper. The obvious move is a lookup table, and it does not exist — Lesson 1.6 already explained why. Decoding has 256 possible inputs so it fits in a kilobyte; encoding takes a continuous float, and there is nothing to index.

"There is nothing to index" is true about the input. Here are four candidates, of which the second and third both come from noticing that:

  1. Exact — std::pow, what we have.
  2. A fitted polynomial in nested square roots. Below the toe (x ≤ 0.0031308) the exact function is 12.92 · x, one multiply, so keep it exactly. Above it, fit a√x + b⁴√x + c⁸√x + dx — three chained square roots, each a single hardware instruction, and four multiply-adds.
  3. A table indexed by the output. There are only 256 possible answers, so there are only 255 places where the answer changes, and those tabulate exactly. The lookup becomes "how many thresholds is this value above", which is a binary search.
  4. A uniform table indexed by the input, 4,096 entries — the thing Lesson 1.6 said could not work, built anyway so that "could not work" has a number attached.
The coefficients are fitted here, not quoted from anywhere

scratch/fit_srgb.py derives them: least squares in that basis, reweighted toward minimax over sixty passes, constrained to be exact at white so that 1.0 encodes to 255 — the one value a reader will check against a screenshot. Excluding the toe from the fit is what makes it good: every basis function has an infinite slope at zero and the truth has a slope of 12.92, so a fit forced to cover the toe spends all its freedom there and is worse everywhere. Measured: including the toe costs 3 codes of worst-case error; excluding it costs 0.0115.

The exact threshold table is slower than pow. 6.919 ns against 3.497 — a "speedup" of 0.51×. 7Common Pitfalls & Debugging

Every zone reports 0.00, or absurdly round numbers

Symptom. Timings come out as 0, or as suspiciously exact multiples of some constant.

Cause. The interval is below the clock's resolution (§2.2). The multiples are the tick itself showing through — if everything is a multiple of 41.67 ns, you have found your timer's granularity the hard way.

Fix. Print 1e9 / SDL_GetPerformanceFrequency() and compare. Then either merge zones until each is above 100× that, or move the question to a differential benchmark (§3.5). Do not reach for a finer clock; there usually is not one, and if there were, §2.3 is waiting.

The zones sum to more than the frame

Symptom. other_ns() is pinned at zero and zones_overlapped() is true.

Cause. Two scope_timers were alive at once — usually a zone wrapping a function that itself opens a zone.

Fix. Zones are a partition, not a call tree. Either split the outer one so it excludes the inner, or delete the inner. A nesting profiler is a real and useful thing, but it needs a stack and a different display, and pretending a flat one supports nesting produces a budget that double-counts.

A "huge speedup" that vanishes in the real program

Symptom. The microbenchmark says 40×. The frame does not move.

Cause. Usually one of three. The compiler deleted the work because nothing consumed the result. The function is faster in a tight loop than in situ (§4.10 — 2.27× here, with a control to prove it). Or Amdahl: the phase was 2% of the frame and you never checked (§3.4).

Fix. Accumulate into something volatile; measure inside the real loop with a differential ladder; and compute the ceiling before starting the work.

Numbers that change every run by 2×

Symptom. The same build gives wildly different answers minute to minute.

Cause. Thermal or power state, most likely. A laptop on battery, a hot chip that has clocked down, a background build, or the OS moving your thread between a performance core and an efficiency one — the last is very easy to hit on Apple silicon and big.LITTLE ARM.

Fix. Take the minimum over several batches (§2.4), which rejects one-sided noise by construction; plug in; close things; and never compare two numbers taken hours apart. Best of all, measure both variants in the same run, back to back — which is exactly what §4.7's candidate loop does.

The optimisation is correct and the picture is wrong

Symptom. An approximate encode ships and gradients grow faint bands, or white is 254 instead of 255.

Cause. The fit was judged on its worst rounded error. That column saturates: anything under half a code reports "1" (§4.7).

Fix. Measure the error before rounding, and constrain the fit to be exact at 0 and 1. Then run the round trip — decode all 256 stored values, re-encode them, and require all 256 back — because that is the test Lesson 3.9's bit-identical 1:1 blit rests on, and an encode that breaks it breaks a previous lesson.

Optimising the thing you were already thinking about

Symptom. A week on the perspective divide. No change.

Cause. Attention is not a measurement. The divide had a whole lesson written about it, so it feels expensive; it costs −0.018 ns/px, which is nothing (§4.6). The encode is a one-line call nobody has thought about since Lesson 2.4, and it is the largest item in the loop.

Fix. Predict, then measure, then act — in that order, and write the prediction down where you cannot revise it afterwards.

A frame-rate counter that refuses to move

Symptom. You halve the fill's cost and the fps display reads 60 before and 60 after.

Cause. Vsync. The frame ends when the display is ready, not when you are, so with headroom to spare the wall clock is pinned to the refresh interval.

Fix. Measure engine time, not wall time — which is why end_frame() is called before SDL_RenderPresent (§4.3) and why the HUD prints both. V disables vsync if you want to watch the wall clock move too.

8Exercises

8.1  Find your own floor

Print your machine's SDL_GetPerformanceFrequency(), compute the tick in nanoseconds, and time a single to_encoded call one thousand times, recording the distribution of answers rather than their average. How many distinct values appear? Predict that number before you run it.

Hint

If the tick is r and the work is w < r, the only possible answers are 0 and r — and the proportion that come back as r is w/r. That gives you a way to measure w out of a timer that cannot resolve it, which is a genuinely useful trick.

Solution sketch

Two distinct values on this machine, in a ratio near 9.3/41.667 ≈ 0.22. If you see many distinct values your counter is finer than ours (some x86 machines report 1 GHz); if you see one, the work is far below the tick and you need a bigger batch. Note what you have just built: a statistical measurement out of a quantised instrument, which is the same idea as dithering.

8.2  Instrument the inner loop, and watch it lie

Put a scope_timer around the fragment body inside fill_triangle. Record what the zone reports and what the whole fill now costs. Then compute what fraction of the reported fragment time is the timer itself.

Hint

You need three numbers: the fill's cost before, the fill's cost after, and what the zone claims. They will not be consistent, and the inconsistency is the answer.

Solution sketch

The fill roughly doubles (§2.3 measures 1.86× on an equivalent loop) while the zone reports less than the fill took, because the closing clock read cannot be inside the interval it closes. So the instrument makes the program slower and under-reports. Now revert it, and note that you cannot recover the answer you wanted from any of the three numbers — the experiment was unsound in principle, not merely noisy.

8.3  Predict a resolution, then check

Using only the two unit costs from §3.3 — 45.47 ns per covered pixel and 23.0 ns per triangle — predict the fill and collect times for the capstone scene at 2560×1440. Then run it and compare. State your prediction's error as a percentage before looking.

Hint

Covered pixels scale with total pixels for a fixed camera, so scale 475,218 by the ratio of resolutions. Triangle count does not change at all — which is the whole point of having two separate unit costs.

Solution sketch

You should land within about 10%. Where the model breaks down is instructive: at very high resolutions the framebuffer stops fitting in cache and the per-pixel cost creeps up, so a constant ns/px is itself an approximation with a domain. Finding where it fails is worth more than the prediction succeeding.

8.4  Make the ladder finer

§4.6's ladder jumps from "coverage + depth" straight to "+ sRGB encode", which bundles the linear-space blend with the encode. Split it: add a rung that interpolates in linear light but writes with blend_space::encoded. How much of the 6.09 ns is the pow, and how much is the three table lookups that decode the corners?

Hint

The corner decode happens once per triangle, not once per pixel — so predict its per-pixel cost before measuring, and be ready for the delta to be indistinguishable from zero. A rung whose delta is zero is a real result.

8.5  Fit your own approximation (engine challenge)

Open scratch/fit_srgb.py and change the basis. Drop the x term to three coefficients; or use √x, ⁴√x only; or add ¹⁶√x. For each, report cost in nanoseconds and error in un-rounded codes, and plot the trade-off. Where is the knee?

Hint

Each extra term is one more sqrt in the chain — a latency cost, since they are dependent — plus one multiply-add. Removing the x term costs nothing at all in time, because that multiply-add runs in parallel with the square roots.

Solution sketch

Two square roots get you to roughly 0.1 codes, which is still under half and still ships at 8 bits; four gets you under 0.005 for no measurable extra time on this machine. The knee is sharp and it is not where the timing suggests, because the chain is latency-bound rather than throughput-bound. Then ask the question this lesson would ask: at what output bit depth does the two-term version stop being good enough? That is the criterion, not the code count.

8.6  Budget something that is not the renderer (engine challenge)

Add a zone::simulate and wrap the fixed-timestep loop from Lesson 1.4 in it. Then run Pong. What fraction of that frame is simulation, and what happens to the shape of the budget when the frame rate drops far enough that the accumulator runs several steps?

Hint

A zone that opens and closes several times in one frame accumulates, which is what add is for. Watch the relationship between the number of steps and the zone's total; if it is not proportional you have found something.

Solution sketch

It is a rounding error at 60 Hz, and it grows discontinuously when the step count goes from 1 to 2 — which is exactly the spiral-of-death mechanism Lesson 1.4 derived, now visible as a bar rather than an argument. Hold Y to throttle the frame and watch it happen.

        reason and is KEPT, as the rule appearing in the engine's own output.
  observer-effect: INSTRUMENTING THE INNER LOOP DESTROYS WHAT IT MEASURES. Measured:
        9.71 -> 18.08 ns/iteration, 1.86x, from a timer whose job was not to disturb.
        AND THE REPORTED NUMBER IS A FICTION — the closing clock read happens inside
        the interval it closes, so the instrument UNDER-reports by ~its own cost while
        making the program ~2x slower. Both errors at once, in opposite directions.
        THE FIX IS NOT A FINER CLOCK, it is SUBTRACTION: two variants differing by one
        thing, both timed coarsely (see differential, below). Resolution comes from the
        EXPERIMENT's design, not the clock's.
  statistics: PICK THE ONE THAT MATCHES THE QUESTION, AND SAY WHICH.
        TIMING NOISE IS ONE-SIDED — the machine can be slower than your code and never
        faster — so the distribution is a hard floor with a right tail and the MEAN is
        wrong for both cases below.
          KERNEL BENCHMARK  the MINIMUM over batches. There IS one true cost and
                            everything above it is interference. best_ns().
          FRAME BUDGET      the MEDIAN over a bounded history. There is NO single true
                            cost — a frame genuinely varies — and one 40 ms hitch moves
                            a mean of 120 by 0.3 ms and the median by nothing.
        k_history = 120 (2 s at 60 Hz): stable, and still responds when a keypress
        changes the cost. std::nth_element, not sort — O(n), and the ordering it does
        not produce is information we would discard. COPY first: sorting the ring in
        place destroys the history that makes it one.
  frame-budget: SIX DISJOINT ZONES THAT COVER THE FRAME. build / collect / sort / fill
        / overlay / present, in the order they happen, so the enum reads as a timeline.
        DISJOINT IS AN ARITHMETIC REQUIREMENT, not tidiness: two live timers count the
        same nanoseconds twice. zones_overlapped() checks it.
        THE UNMEASURED REMAINDER IS THE MOST IMPORTANT ROW. Six honest bars summing to
        60% of a frame look complete until you double the biggest and gain 9%. Ours is
        0.2% — because it is displayed. Drawn to the END of the stacked bar, in a grey
        that deliberately does not look like a phase.
        A SUM OF MEDIANS IS NOT THE MEDIAN OF A SUM. They differ when zones peak on
        different frames. The disagreement is left visible; deriving one number from
        the others to make the table add up is how a budget stops being a measurement.
        THE FRAME STARTS BEFORE THE EVENT DRAIN (draining is work) and ENDS BEFORE
        SDL_RenderPresent (which blocks on vsync — a bar reading "waiting for the
        monitor" says nothing about the renderer). The HUD prints engine time AND wall
        time so the gap is visible: WITH VSYNC ON, AN FPS COUNTER CANNOT TELL YOU THAT
        YOU MADE ANYTHING FASTER.
        NOT EVERY PHASE IS A LEXICAL SCOPE. The HUD spans hundreds of lines to the
        bottom of the loop, so it uses an explicit now_ticks()/add() pair — which is
        why profiler::add is public. A zone is a CATEGORY of work, not a point in time,
        and `overlay` accumulates from two places in the frame.
        INSTRUMENTATION SHAPES THE CODE IT MEASURES, and say so rather than pretending.
        The sort left draw_triangles for sort_back_to_front so it could be timed apart
        from the fill; the harness stages all triangles into a vector before filling so
        collect and fill cannot interleave. Neither is how you would write it otherwise.
  px-per-triangle: THE AXIS. Work happens at two frequencies — per-VERTEX (triangles)
        and per-PIXEL (covered pixels) — and their ratio is set by one number.
        MEASURED, same geometry, 16x the pixels: collect 53.08 -> 55.04 us (3.7%, i.e.
        noise); fill 1.55 -> 23.24 ms (15x, i.e. the pixel count). Two slopes.
        MEASURED, same screen size, 36 -> 36864 triangles: collect 1.15 -> 967.62 us
        (840x) and fill only 338 -> 1070. THE TWO LINES CROSS near 1 px/triangle, and
        "optimise the fill" is correct on one side of that crossing and wrong on the
        other — with NOTHING about the renderer changed to move between them.
        THE PREDICTION EVERYONE GETS WRONG: the 2-triangle floor costs 1390.14 us and
        the 2304-triangle torus 171.78 — the floor is 8x MORE expensive with 1152x
        FEWER triangles. ns/tri: 695069 vs 74.6. ns/px: 45.01 vs 64.14, a factor of
        1.42, and that residue is real (tiny triangles pay fill_triangle's per-triangle
        setup against ~1.2 px).
        QUOTE ns/PX AND ns/TRIANGLE, NEVER ms/frame. Ours: 45.47 ns/covered px and
        23.0 ns/triangle. Total milliseconds is a fact about one scene at one
        resolution on one machine, and it is the only one most people record.
  amdahl: speedup = 1 / ((1 - p) + p/s). DERIVE it, because the derivation IS the
        content: the part you did not speed up does not go away, so s -> infinity gives
        1/(1 - p) and no more. Our fill is p = 0.96, ceiling 25x.
        USE IT TWICE. BEFORE, as a filter — compute the ceiling and decide whether the
        work is worth starting. AFTER, as a check ON YOURSELF: p, s and the whole-frame
        speedup are NOT independent, so a frame that improved by more than the formula
        allows means you mismeasured. VERIFIED: ceiling 1.284x, measured 1.287x.
  differential: RESOLUTION FROM THE EXPERIMENT, NOT THE CLOCK. One fixed geometry,
        rendered with fill styles differing by exactly ONE thing; each rung is coarse
        (well above 4.17 us) and the DELTA is the cost of what changed.
        TWO DISCIPLINES: change one thing per rung, and HAVE A CONTROL. Without the
        control this lesson first reported a texture fetch at 6.40x its microbenchmark
        cost — a number produced by adding an encode and two stores alongside the fetch
        and attributing all three to it. With the control: 2.27x, which is true.
        WHEN A MEASURED RATIO IS BIGGER THAN YOU CAN EXPLAIN, the two sides differ in
        more than one way. (3.9 §5.1 made the same error; this is its second sighting.)
  fill-anatomy: ns PER COVERED PIXEL, 2 triangles over 272,223 px at 960x540:
          coverage + colour interp        2.682
          + perspective divide            2.664   (-0.018 — FREE, below the noise)
          + depth test & write            2.936   (+0.272)
          + sRGB encode (std::pow)        9.024   (+6.088)  <-- the largest single item
          textured, nearest              18.186   (+15.487)
          textured, bilinear             25.076   (+6.889)
          lit per pixel                  33.048   (+7.973)
          lit + textured                 46.456   (+13.408)
          lit + textured, fast encode    35.212
        THE ENCODE ALONE IS 2.1x COVERAGE + DIVIDE + DEPTH COMBINED (6.088 vs 2.936).
        3.2 warned that its per-pixel divide was "the honest cost of this lesson"; the
        measurement says the warning was unnecessary — it hides entirely behind other
        latency. COST DOES NOT DISTRIBUTE ITSELF ACCORDING TO HOW MUCH YOU THOUGHT
        ABOUT SOMETHING: to_encoded has been an unremarked one-liner since 2.4.
  srgb-encode: FOUR CANDIDATES, TIMED AND CHECKED (ns/call, speedup, un-rounded error
        in 8-bit codes, % of codes differing, round-trip failures of 256):
          exact std::pow              3.497  1.00x       —      0.00%   0/256
          FITTED SQRT CHAIN           1.856  1.88x  0.0115      0.60%   0/256  <- ships
          threshold table + bsearch    6.919  0.51x   exact      0.00%   0/256
          uniform input table, 4096   0.994  3.52x  0.4022      3.66%   0/256
        THE EXACT TABLE IS SLOWER THAN pow. 8 dependent L1 loads in a binary search is
        a longer latency chain than a modern powf. A beautiful idea, timed and rejected.
        JUDGE THE ERROR BEFORE ROUNDING. The "worst code" column SATURATES — everything
        under half a code reports 1 — so it cannot tell 0.0115 from 0.4022, which is the
        whole question. At a 16-bit target those become 3.0 and 103.4 codes.
        THE CACHE ARGUMENT AGAINST THE TABLE IS FALSE ON THIS MACHINE, measured: with a
        framebuffer-sized stream running alongside, all four candidates read 0.98-1.04x.
        The hypothesis is KEPT WITH ITS RESULT rather than dropped, because shipping the
        right decision with the reasoning that failed is how folklore is manufactured.
        THE REAL REASON is that the polynomial's error is a property of the CURVE and
        the table's is a property of its CELL WIDTH — so only one of them is still
        usable when Module 6 stops being 8-bit.
        THE FIT: toe kept EXACT (12.92*x below 0.0031308 — one multiply, and every basis
        function has infinite slope at 0 where the truth has 12.92, so fitting the toe
        costs 3 codes instead of 0.0115). Above it, a*sqrt(x) + b*x^1/4 + c*x^1/8 + d*x,
        three CHAINED sqrts (a latency chain of ~3, not a throughput win of 3).
        Coefficients FITTED in scratch/fit_srgb.py — least squares reweighted toward
        minimax, constrained to sum to 1 so white encodes to 255 exactly. Exact at 0
        and 1; worst |err| 0.0000451; error OSCILLATES about zero (a wobble, not a bias).
        THE NaN GUARD AND CLAMP ARE REPEATED DELIBERATELY, not duplicated by accident:
        they are not part of the approximation, they are what "encode a float into a
        byte" means. sqrt of a negative is a NaN too (3.3's undefined cast).
        encode_mode {exact, fast} IS THE FIRST KNOB IN THIS ENGINE THAT IS NEITHER RIGHT
        NOR WRONG. blend_space::encoded / interpolation::affine / draw_line_naive are
        kept so a MISTAKE can be summoned; this one is a defensible speed/accuracy point.
        fill_style::encode DEFAULTS TO exact — a decision about the COURSE, not about
        renderers: every measured claim in 3.1-3.9 was made against it, and a default
        that moved 0.60% of those pixels by one code would falsify nine lessons. The
        DEMO selects fast. (Same bargain as inv_w = 1 in 3.2, lights = nullptr in 3.8,
        an unbound albedo in 3.9.)
        RESULT: fill 1.30x, FRAME 1.29x (and 1.29x / 1.28x at 720p), at a cost of 2657
        of 473199 shaded px differing by exactly 1 code — 0.56%.
  cache: MEASURE THE CLIFF, DO NOT ASSERT IT. 2^21 random samples, sample count and
        access pattern held fixed, only the image size varying: 32x32 through 1024x1024
        all read 2.25-2.38 ns/fetch, and only 2048x2048 (16 MiB) jumps to 3.88 (1.72x).
        THE CLIFF IS NOT WHERE THE FOLKLORE PUTS IT. This machine's L2 makes a few MiB
        free, so "the texture must fit in L1" is advice for a different computer.
        RANDOM, NOT SEQUENTIAL: a sequential walk is prefetched perfectly and would
        measure the prefetcher.
        A FUNCTION'S COST IS NOT A PROPERTY OF THE FUNCTION. The same 64x64 nearest
        fetch costs 2.26 ns in a tight loop and 5.13 ns in situ (2.27x), isolated with a
        control that keeps the encode and both stores and removes only the fetch. In a
        tight loop consecutive fetches overlap; in situ each sits in a dependency chain
        (uv from the perspective divide, result into the encode) with nothing to overlap
        with. This is why the ladder measures inside the REAL loop and why a
        microbenchmark can only ever be a hypothesis about one.
  benchmark-hygiene: accumulate into something VOLATILE or the compiler deletes the
        work and you publish a 3000x speedup. Measure at -O2 and never profile a debug
        build — 1.5 measured put_pixel vs a row pointer at 5.1x (-O0) and 14.8x (-O2),
        so a debug profile is a ranking of a DIFFERENT PROGRAM. Measure both variants in
        the SAME RUN, back to back, so thermal and scheduling state cannot drift between
        them.
  demo-keys: [3] the frame budget panel (over the render, and charged to `overlay`
        because a profiler HUD is not free); [4] encode fast/exact, with the differing
        pixel count printed — one code is invisible, so the counter is the only evidence
        the toggle reached the rasterizer at all.
