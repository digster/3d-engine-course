Shadow Mapping: Bias, Acne, and PCF

Every light this engine has ever had is unoccluded. lambert() asks whether a surface faces the light and calls the answer illumination — a question about one surface's orientation and nothing else — so a floor in the open and the same floor under a table are shaded identically, and every object in every picture so far floats. The fix is a z-buffer rendered from the light, which the engine has owned since Lesson 3.1. What is genuinely hard is the comparison: the number stored in that buffer and the number tested against it describe the same point on the same surface and are computed differently. They disagree, always, and that disagreement has a name — shadow acne — and, it turns out, a magnitude you can derive on paper before you write a line.

1The Problem: Nothing Blocks Anything

Open any picture this course has produced since Lesson 3.6 and look at where the objects meet the ground. There is nothing there. The cube does not sit on the floor, it hovers above it; the torus in Lesson 6.7's demo could be one metre up or one centimetre and you cannot tell. That cue — the dark patch where a thing touches another thing — is the single strongest signal the human visual system uses for spatial arrangement, and we have never drawn one.

The reason is one line, and it has been the same line since Lesson 3.6:

\[ \text{lambert}(n, l) \;=\; \max(0,\; n \cdot l) \]

Read it as a question and it asks: does this surface face the light? That is a fact about one surface's orientation. It is not the question we want, which is: can this surface see the light? — a fact about the surface and every other object in the scene. Nothing in the shading equation has ever consulted the rest of the scene. It cannot. shade() receives an albedo, a normal, an eye direction and a light, and from those five arguments there is no possible way to discover that a wall is in the way.

So the shading equation has to be given a sixth thing: a number saying how much of the light actually arrives. Where that number goes in the equation is not arbitrary and is worth settling now, because getting it wrong is a bug that looks like a material bug. Lesson 6.2 split the shading equation into three factors with three separate physical meanings:

\[ L_o \;=\; \underbrace{f_r}_{\text{what the surface does}} \cdot \underbrace{E_{\perp}\cos\theta}_{\text{what the light delivers}} \;+\; \underbrace{\text{albedo}\cdot L_a}_{\text{the ambient term}} \]

A shadow is a statement about whether the light arrives. It says nothing whatsoever about what the surface would do with the light if it did. So it multiplies the middle factor, beside the cosine — which is exactly where Lesson 6.2 put the cosine, and for the same reason.

And it must not multiply the ambient term

Ambient models light that has bounced off everything else in the room. An object standing between a surface and the sun does not stop the room from existing — indeed, the ambient term is precisely what a shadowed surface is left with. That is why lighting::ambient being a cool blue is what makes shadows read as blue rather than as holes.

Multiply the ambient by the shadow too and shadows render pure black, which is what a shadow looks like in vacuum and nowhere else. It is a one-character mistake and it is instantly recognisable once you have seen it.

2The Idea: A Z-Buffer, Pointed Somewhere Else

Here is the whole technique in one sentence, and the pleasure of it is that the engine already owns every part: render the scene from the light's point of view and keep only the depth.

Think about what that buffer contains. For every ray the light emits — one per texel — it holds the distance to the nearest surface along that ray. Which is to say: it is a record of exactly what the light can see. Anything further along a ray than the stored value is behind something, and behind something is what "in shadow" means.

And "the distance to the nearest surface along every ray from a viewpoint" is not a new algorithm. It is a z-buffer. Lesson 3.1 built one, Lesson 4.7 built the GPU's, and both have been computing this quantity every frame for three modules while we used it only to decide which fragment wins.

So a frame gains a second pass:

  1. The depth pass. Rasterise the scene from the light. No colour, no shading, no textures — a surface's colour cannot change where the surface is. What comes out is a depth buffer, which we now call a shadow map.
  2. The scene pass. For every fragment, project its world position into the light's space, look up the stored depth for that direction, and compare. Further than stored means occluded.

On the CPU that first pass is collect_triangles followed by draw_triangles — Module 2's vertex stage and Module 3's rasterizer, unmodified, with a different camera. Not one line of the rasterizer changes. That is the cheapest claim in this lesson and also its biggest: a shadow map is not a new renderer, it is the renderer you already have, aimed somewhere else.

Two things this lesson deliberately does not do

One light. lighting has one key and this has one map. Two lights are two maps and two lookups: a linear cost and no new ideas.

One cascade. A single map stretched over a whole outdoor scene gives the near field far too few texels — §11 shows what that looks like. Splitting the camera's frustum into ranges and giving each its own map is cascaded shadow mapping, it is Lesson 6.9, and it is an extension of this file rather than a replacement for it.

3A Light With No Position

"Render from the light" needs a camera, and a camera needs an eye. Our light does not have one. directional_light carries a direction and an irradiance and nothing else, because that is what a directional light is: the sun, modelled as parallel rays arriving from infinitely far away. There is no point they converge on, so there is no place to put an eye and no field of view to ask for.

3.1 — What replaces the pyramid

A perspective frustum is a pyramid because rays converge on an eye. If the rays are parallel, the shape swept out is not a pyramid. It is a box.

And a box maps onto the clip cube by scale-and-offset alone — no divide, no w trick, nothing. Lesson 2.10 spent a whole lesson deriving perspective because the projection is genuinely non-linear and a matrix is linear; the entire difficulty there was arranging for the divide to happen afterwards. Here there is no divide to arrange.

3.2 — Deriving it, one axis at a time

We want the box's left edge l to land at NDC −1 and its right edge r at +1. Any map that takes one interval to another affinely is the same four steps: subtract the start, divide by the width, scale to the new width, add the new start.

\[ x_{ndc} \;=\; 2\,\frac{x - l}{r - l} - 1 \;=\; \left(\frac{2}{r-l}\right) x \;-\; \frac{r+l}{r-l} \]

In prose: multiply x by 2/(r-l) and add -(r+l)/(r-l). A multiply and an add — which fits in a matrix row with room to spare.

y is the same sentence with b and t. z is the one that carries this course's conventions: view space looks down −z (Conventions §5), the near plane sits at z = −near and must land at device depth 0, and the far plane at z = −far must land at 1 — SDL_GPU's depth range, not OpenGL's (Conventions §4).

\[ z_{ndc} \;=\; \frac{-z - \text{near}}{\text{far} - \text{near}} \;=\; \left(\frac{-1}{\text{far}-\text{near}}\right) z \;-\; \frac{\text{near}}{\text{far}-\text{near}} \]

Assembled, with each brace group a column in our column-major storage (Lesson 2.6):

\[ P_{ortho} = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{2}{r-l} & 0 & 0 & -\frac{r+l}{r-l} \\[2pt] 0 & \frac{2}{t-b} & 0 & -\frac{t+b}{t-b} \\[2pt] 0 & 0 & \frac{-1}{f-n} & \frac{-n}{f-n} \\[2pt] 0 & 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \]

Look at the bottom row. Perspective's bottom row is (0, 0, -1, 0), and that −1 is what copies −z into w and makes the divide happen. Here the bottom row is (0, 0, 0, 1), so w comes out exactly 1, and the perspective divide that follows is the identity.

3.3 — A worked example

Take a ten-unit-wide box: l = −5, r = 5, b = −5, t = 5, near = 1, far = 21. Then 2/(r-l) = 0.2, (r+l)/(r-l) = 0, the depth scale is −1/20 and the depth offset is −1/20.

Push the view-space point (2.5, 0, −6) through by hand:

Push the same point through perspective(50°, 1, 1, 21) and the depth comes out 0.792: three quarters of the depth range consumed by the first quarter of the distance. That crowding is Lesson 2.10's, and Lesson 4.9 measured it on real hardware.

A prediction this lesson's plan got wrong, corrected

The plan for this lesson said the projection's non-linear depth distribution is why shadow error grows with distance. That is true of perspective and it is exactly not true here. An orthographic projection spreads device depth evenly over the box, so one depth code is worth the same number of metres at the near plane and at the far one — verify_68 §A checks that the two are equal to six decimal places, where under perspective the ratio is over 100×.

The consequence is that the quantisation half of §4's bias is a constant across the whole map, which makes a directional shadow map the easy case. The non-linear story returns in full the day this light becomes a spot light and its projection becomes a perspective one. Naming that now is cheaper than discovering it in Lesson 6.9.

3.4 — Fitting the box, and a negative near plane

A box has to be fitted to something, and the something is the scene's world-space bounds. The procedure is four steps and each one has a decision in it:

  1. Pick the light's axes. direction is the direction of travel (Lesson 3.6's convention, stated in capitals in light.hpp because it is the commonest sign error in graphics), so it is the light camera's forward axis, with no negation. The up hint only has to be non-parallel to it.
  2. Put the eye at the scene's centre. This sounds wrong and is exactly right: a directional light has no position, so any point on the axis gives the same rays.
  3. Transform the scene's AABB into light view space and read the extents straight off it.
  4. Square the box in x and y. Every formula in §4 says "one texel" as though a texel had a single size; squaring is what earns that sentence. It costs resolution on the narrow axis and buys one honest number, world_per_texel.

Step 2 has a consequence worth stopping on. If the eye is in the middle of the scene, half the scene is behind it — at positive view-space z — so the near plane comes out negative. perspective could not accept that for a moment: a negative near plane means dividing by a quantity that changes sign, and everything explodes. orthographic does not care, because it has no divide. verify_68 §B measures the scene reaching z = +2.608 in light view space and every one of the eight corners still landing inside the clip cube.

Two numbers fall out of the fit and the whole of §4 is built from them:

\[ \text{world\_per\_texel} = \frac{\text{box side}}{\text{resolution}}, \qquad \text{depth\_range} = \text{far} - \text{near} \]

The first is the map's resolving power in metres. The second is how many metres one unit of device depth is worth — and because the projection is orthographic, it is a single number that is valid everywhere in the box.

4The Comparison, and Why It Fails

Everything so far is machinery. This section is the lesson.

Build the map, render the scene, and what you get is not shadows. It is this:

4.1 — Two numbers, computed differently

Here is the situation, stated precisely, because the precision is what makes the fix derivable rather than a guess.

A point P on a lit surface projects into the light's space at some continuous position (u, v) with depth d. We compare d against the depth the map stored for that direction. But the map does not store a depth "for that direction" — it stores one depth per texel, sampled at one point, and P is somewhere else inside that texel.

Both numbers describe the same plane. Neither is wrong. They differ because they were evaluated at two different points on it — and on a surface tilted relative to the light, two different points have two different depths.

Now the crucial observation, and it is the one that turns a mystery into arithmetic: half of every texel's footprint lies downhill of the point the map sampled. Downhill means further from the light, which means the comparison says "something is in front of me", which means shadow. So with no bias at all, half of every lit surface should shadow itself.

That is a prediction with a number in it, and verify_68 §C measures it: on an unoccluded plane, sampled at 9,216 points, 50.1% come back shadowed. Not "some". Half. Shadow acne is not an artefact of a subtle bug; it is what the algorithm does when you implement it correctly and stop.

4.2 — How big is the disagreement?

Bound it. The fragment and the stored sample are at most some lateral distance apart in the light's uv plane. Call that distance's size in texels the reach. For a single lookup the fragment sits somewhere inside its own texel, so the worst case is half a texel in each axis — half a texel diagonal:

\[ \text{reach}_1 = \tfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2} = 0.7071 \ \text{texels} \]

Walking that far across a surface changes its depth. By how much? If the surface's normal makes an angle θ with the direction to the light, then moving one unit perpendicular to the light changes the depth along the light by exactly tan(θ) — that is what a slope is. And cos(θ) is n · l, which the shading equation already computed:

\[ \tan\theta \;=\; \frac{\sqrt{1 - (n\cdot l)^2}}{n \cdot l} \]

So the worst depth error, in world units, is the lateral travel times the slope; and dividing by depth_range converts it into the device-depth units the comparison is actually performed in:

\[ \text{bias} \;=\; \text{reach} \cdot \text{world\_per\_texel} \cdot \frac{\tan\theta}{\text{depth\_range}} \]

In prose: how far the furthest tap can be (in texels), times how big a texel is (in metres), times how steeply the surface is leaning, divided by how many metres a unit of depth is worth. Four quantities, all of which the engine already knows.

The bound is tight, and that is the check that matters

A bound you cannot exceed is easy to write; a bound that is reached is a derivation. verify_68 §C walks a tilted plane and records the worst actual disagreement between a fragment's depth and its texel's stored depth:

measured 1.931 × 10⁻³, derived bound 2.101 × 10⁻³ — the measurement reaches 92% of the bound. The remaining 8% is simply that no sample landed at the exact worst corner. Had the measurement come out at 20% of the bound, the bound would be describing something other than what actually happens, and the bias built on it would be a superstition that works.

4.3 — The worked example, with real numbers

A 1024-texel map fitted to a 20-metre box gives world_per_texel = 20/1024 = 0.01953 m. A surface at 60° to the light has tan θ = 1.7321. With a 24-metre depth range and a single tap:

\[ 0.7071 \times 0.01953 \times \frac{1.7321}{24} \;=\; 9.966\times 10^{-4} \]

About a thousandth of the depth range — which in world units is 0.024 m, or roughly one and a quarter texels' worth of height. Now vary the two inputs and watch what the formula says:

4.4 — The other half: the format's own error

There is a second, smaller disagreement that has nothing to do with geometry. The stored depth was rounded to the nearest code the depth buffer can represent, so it can already be wrong by half a code before any surface is tilted:

It matters on a 16-bit map with a very large depth range, and it matters on a spot light where — because the projection is perspective — that half code is worth a hundred times more world depth at the far plane than at the near one.

A real subtlety: where your rasterizer puts its samples

This engine's fill_triangle evaluates every attribute — depth included — at integer pixel coordinates, not at pixel centres. So the depth stored "for texel i" is the depth of the surface at exactly x = i, and the nearest stored sample to a fragment at x is round(x), putting the offset in [−0.5, +0.5).

Write floor there instead — which is what a GPU lookup does, because hardware samples at pixel centres and SampleCmp selects the texel containing the uv — and every offset is biased half a texel in one direction. The reach doubles, the derived bias is half what is needed, and acne returns on one side of every slope. The two renderers spell it differently and must mean the same thing; this cost a debugging pass and it is written into the code where the decision lives.

5Three Cures, and What Each One Costs

5.1 — A constant, and why it must peter-pan

Subtract a fixed number from the fragment's depth before comparing. It is what everyone writes first, it does remove the acne, and it is worth understanding exactly how it fails rather than just being told it does.

The error being cancelled is proportional to tan θ. The correction is not. So the constant must be sized for the steepest surface in the scene, and every flatter surface then receives a bias far larger than it needed. verify_68 §D puts a number on it: a bias sized for a 50° surface is 3.1× too small at 75°. Size it for 75° instead and everything shallower is over-corrected.

What over-correction looks like has a name — peter-panning, after the boy whose shadow came unstuck — and it too is exactly quantifiable. Subtracting b from a fragment's device depth is the same as moving that fragment b × depth_range world units toward the light along its own ray. Any occluder nearer to it than that stops occluding. So:

\[ \text{shadow lost wherever the caster is within } b \cdot \text{depth\_range} \text{ of the receiver} \]

Which is worst precisely where a caster touches its receiver, because that is where the gap is smallest — so the contact shadow, the one cue we added shadows to get, is the first thing a constant bias destroys. verify_68 §D measures it directly: a receiver under a caster 0.541 world units away along the light ray is fully shadowed at a bias worth half that gap and fully lit at a bias worth 1.5× it.

5.2 — Slope-scaled: the derived answer

§4.2's formula, applied per fragment. Each surface gets exactly the bias its own geometry requires and no more — zero when it faces the light square-on, growing with the tilt, clamped where the map genuinely cannot resolve the surface at all.

Measured, on the same plane that showed 50.1% acne with no bias: 0.0%. And because the bias is zero on surfaces facing the light, the contact shadows a constant bias destroys are exactly the ones this preserves.

A scale factor of 1.0 is the derivation

shadow_settings::slope_scale defaults to 1.0, and a number other than 1 is an admission that something upstream is not what the derivation assumed. That is worth knowing rather than absorbing silently into a magic constant — if you find yourself needing 3.0, the question to ask is which of the four quantities in the formula is wrong, and §6.3 gives one very common answer.

5.3 — Normal-offset, and why 6.7 is why it works

The third answer is structurally different: instead of moving the fragment's depth, move the sample point, along the surface normal, before projecting it into the light's space.

\[ p' = p + n_{geom}\,\cdot\,\text{scale}\cdot\text{world\_per\_texel}\cdot\sin\theta \]

Nothing at all when the surface faces the light; one texel's worth when it is edge-on. And edge-on is exactly where the depth-side bias diverges, which is the trade: depth error grows without bound as tan θ does, while the lateral offset is bounded by construction because it never exceeds a texel.

It converts the error; it does not remove it

Be honest about the mechanism. Moving along the normal buys clearance partly by lifting the point off the surface and partly by landing the lookup in a neighbouring texel — one whose stored sample is further uphill. That second half is a heuristic, not a bound, and what it costs is a lookup that has moved sideways: near a silhouette the sample can slide off the caster entirely and light a pixel that should be dark. The magnitude below is the standard one (Holbert, Saying Goodbye to Shadow Acne, GDC 2011); the derivation that would make it a bound does not exist, and pretending otherwise would be the kind of hand-waving this course is against.

And here is where Lesson 6.7 collects. Which normal? The engine now has two that disagree: the geometric normal the triangles have, and the shading normal a normal map invented. It must be the geometric one, and the reason is a sentence: shadow acne is a disagreement about where the triangles are, and a normal map does not move a triangle. Offset along a mapped normal and the correction wobbles per texel of an image that has nothing to do with the geometry causing the problem.

The same argument applies one line earlier, to the cosine itself. The n · l that scales the light uses the shading normal, because it asks how much light the surface receives. The n · l that scales the bias uses the geometric normal, because it asks how steeply the surface leans against the texel grid. They are two different cosines that have been the same number for five lessons, and this is the first line in the engine that has to tell them apart.

5.4 — And a fourth, which is free

Render only the back faces of casters into the map. Then every front face being shaded is compared against a surface that is genuinely behind it, and the disagreement has the whole thickness of the object to absorb it. It costs nothing — it is one field on the pipeline's rasterizer state — and on closed geometry it is excellent.

On open geometry it is a disaster, and the failure is total rather than subtle: a ground plane has no back face, so it contributes nothing to the map, so the map is empty and nothing is ever shadowed. verify_68 §D measures exactly that — 0.00% acne on a plane, because the plane's only face was culled. The default is therefore cull_mode::none and the choice belongs to whoever knows what is in the scene.

6PCF: Compare, Then Filter

A shadow map's edge is a hard step: one texel is occluded and its neighbour is not. Blown up to screen scale that is a staircase, and the cure is obvious — filter it. What is not obvious is that the obvious way to filter it is wrong, in a way that produces a plausible picture.

6.1 — The order is not a preference

Take two adjacent texels storing depths 0.3 and 0.9, and a receiver at 0.5. Filter the depths first, as a linear sampler naturally would:

\[ \frac{0.3 + 0.9}{2} = 0.6, \qquad 0.6 > 0.5 \;\Rightarrow\; \textbf{fully lit} \]

The receiver is reported completely unshadowed — with half of its taps occluded. That is not a blurrier answer. It is a wrong one, and it stays wrong however many taps you add.

Compare first, then filter:

\[ 0.3 < 0.5 \Rightarrow 0, \qquad 0.9 > 0.5 \Rightarrow 1, \qquad \frac{0 + 1}{2} = \textbf{0.5} \]

Half shadowed, which is the answer. The rule underneath is one line and it generalises far beyond shadows: the values you average must be the values you want the average of. We want an average visibility, so visibility is what has to be averaged — and a depth is not a visibility, it is an input to computing one.

That is the entire reason the graphics API has a type for this. An SDL_GPUSampler with enable_compare = true — HLSL's SamplerComparisonState — performs the depth test on each of its four bilinear texels before blending them, so a single fetch is already 2×2 percentage-closer filtering in fixed-function hardware. The comparison operator lives in the sampler object, not in the shader, which means it is free.

6.2 — And the bias has to widen with the kernel

Widen the kernel beyond hardware's 2×2 and the taps get further away. This is the finding this lesson made by rendering, and it is worth the space because the symptom points at the wrong culprit.

§4.2's reach was half a texel diagonal, because the only sample involved was the fragment's own texel. A 3×3 kernel reads a texel one step out in each direction, so its furthest tap is 1.5 texels away on each axis:

\[ \text{reach}_r = (r + 0.5)\sqrt{2} \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad \text{reach}_0 = 0.707, \quad \text{reach}_1 = 2.121 \]

Three times as far. So a bias sized for a single tap covers a third of what a 3×3 kernel needs, and two thirds of the acne walks straight back in — at the exact moment you turn PCF on. Which makes it look like a filtering bug. It is not; it is the same bias formula with the wrong reach.

Measured, twice

On the controlled plane in verify_68 §E: 1 tap with the derived bias gives 0.00% acne, 3×3 with the reach-corrected bias gives 0.00%, and 3×3 with the bias deliberately sized for one tap gives 79.6%.

And in the actual renderer, before the fix was made: shapes.glb at PCF radius 0 had 78 stray pixels, at radius 1 it had 10,348, and at radius 2 it had 20,265. After the fix, 174 and 224 — and what is left is the legitimate soft edge, which should differ from a hard one.

6.3 — What it costs, and what it does not buy

A 3×3 kernel is nine comparison fetches per shadowed fragment, and each hardware fetch is already filtering four texels — so the footprint is 4×4 texels of effective coverage for nine fetches. That is the cheapest soft shadow there is and it is what most games ship.

What it does not buy is a physically meaningful penumbra. A real shadow's softness depends on the light's angular size and on the distance from the caster to the receiver: sharp at contact, blurring with distance. PCF's blur is a constant number of shadow texels, so it is the same everywhere — which reads as "slightly out of focus" rather than as "soft light". Percentage-closer soft shadows estimate a blocker distance first and scale the kernel by it; that is a real technique and it is out of scope here.

7Implementation

7.1 — A box, at its fourth call site

Before anything else: the shadow fit needs the scene's world-space bounds, and that pattern has now been written out by hand three times — gltf_view accumulates a bounds_min_/bounds_max_ pair to frame its camera, mesh_report measures an extent, and Lesson 6.6's importer walks every position for the same reason. Three call sites is this course's threshold for promoting a pattern into a type (Lesson 3.3's projector, 3.8's fill_style), and this is the fourth.

The one design decision worth defending is the default: 

vec3 min{ +1e30f, +1e30f, +1e30f };
vec3 max{ -1e30f, -1e30f, -1e30f };

A default-constructed box is empty by being inside out, which is not a trick — it is the identity element for expand. Growing an empty box by a point gives exactly that point, with no "is this the first one?" branch at any call site. A first-point special case is the commonest bug in bounds code, and this representation removes the opportunity rather than documenting it.

7.2 — orthographic, in mat4.hpp

§3.2's matrix, written as four columns, next to perspective so the one structural difference — the bottom row — is visible without scrolling. It takes a signed near plane, and the doc comment says why in one sentence: an orthographic box has no divide, so nothing forbids the near plane being behind the eye, and a light fitted to a scene routinely wants that.

7.3 — The fit

fit_directional is §3.4's four steps and about thirty lines. Two of them are worth reading closely.

The first is the padding. Without it the nearest vertex sits exactly on the near plane, where near_distance returns 0 and the clipper's >= 0 test is decided by the last bit of a float — a caster that flickers in and out of its own shadow map for no visible reason. One per cent of the range, or a millimetre, whichever is larger.

The second is the sign flip. View space looks down −z, so the nearest surface has the largest z. Reading the near and far planes off the transformed box therefore means negating and swapping: 

float near_z = -lv.max.z;   // the NEAREST surface has the LARGEST view-space z
float far_z  = -lv.min.z;

7.4 — The depth pass, which is Module 3

shadow_map::render computes the bounds, fits the camera, clears the buffer, and then does this: 

collect_triangles(tris_, scratch_, objects, meshes, cv, pr, unused, opts, &cstats);
draw_triangles(*unused_colour_, depth_.get(), tris_, false, style);

That is the entire pass. cv is a camera_view holding the light's view matrix; pr is a projector holding the orthographic matrix and a viewport the size of the map. Nothing else changed. Every claim in Modules 2 and 3 about clipping, winding, the top-left rule and perspective-correct interpolation carries over intact, because it is literally the same code.

Two settings on the way through. opts.shading = shade_eval::palette is the "off" position of the shading cycle — a depth pass has no use for a colour and computing one would be per-vertex work thrown away. And fill_style::depth_only is new: the fragment function is never called and no colour is stored.

The colour target that nothing writes to

shadow_map owns a full-resolution framebuffer that is never touched. It has to: fill_triangle clips its bounding box against the framebuffer's dimensions, and there is nowhere else for a pass to learn how big it is. At 1024² that is four megabytes allocated and never written.

On the GPU there is genuinely no colour attachment — num_color_targets = 0, and the render pass carries its own dimensions. That gap is what a depth-only pass is, and this is the most concrete form it takes in this course: the CPU can skip the work, and only the GPU can skip the attachment. On tiled hardware the difference is the entire tile write.

7.5 — The lookup

shadow_map::visibility(world_pos, geometric_normal, n_dot_l) returns 1 for fully lit and 0 for fully shadowed. Six steps, and each has already been argued for:

  1. Short-circuit if there is no map, or if n_dot_l <= 0 — a surface facing away is already dark by Lesson 3.6's clamp, and consulting the map there could only introduce a wrong answer at a silhouette.
  2. Under normal_offset, move the sample point (§5.3).
  3. Project with clip_from_world, then vp.to_screen — the same viewport that wrote the map, y-flip and all. Two spellings of one mapping is how a shadow ends up half a texel from its caster.
  4. Range-check. Outside the light's box means "occluded by nothing", which is lit.
  5. Compute the bias from the policy, plus the format's half-code.
  6. Take the taps, comparing each one then averaging (§6.1).

Step 3 carries the round-versus-floor decision from §4.4, written into the code beside the reason, because it is a two-character difference that halves the reach.

7.6 — Where the term enters the equation

shade() gains a final parameter, float visibility = 1.0f, and it multiplies E: 

const float er = lights.key.colour.r * lights.key.irradiance * n_dot_l * visibility;

Beside the cosine, for the reason §1 gave: both are statements about how much light reaches this point, and neither is a statement about the surface. The default of 1 means every call site written before this lesson behaves identically — which is why the reference render is byte-identical for a seventeenth lesson.

In raster.cpp the fragment computes the visibility itself, from the geometric normal and a cosine taken against it, and hands the result to shade. §5.3 is the argument; the code says it again where the choice is made, because a reader who has not read §5.3 will otherwise assume the two normals are the same one.

8The GPU Port: A Pass With No Colour

The port is interesting for exactly three reasons, all of them things the software version cannot express.

8.1 — A second render pass

Everything the GPU renderer has drawn since Lesson 4.8 has happened inside one SDL_BeginGPURenderPass. The shadow map is a pass that runs before that one, on the same command buffer, into a different attachment, with a different pipeline.

And it needs no barrier of ours at all, which is worth noticing rather than accepting: a render pass is the unit SDL_GPU synchronises on, so everything the depth pass wrote is visible to everything issued after SDL_EndGPURenderPass. That is one of the real arguments for a pass-based API over a bare command list.

8.2 — num_color_targets = 0

pipeline_desc's constructor fills in one colour target taken from the device's swapchain, which is right for anything drawing into a window and wrong here. Zeroing the count is what makes this a depth-only pipeline, and the pass then begins with a null colour array: 

raw.target_info.num_color_targets = 0;
// …and then, with a null colour array and a count of zero:
SDL_GPURenderPass* pass = SDL_BeginGPURenderPass(cb, nullptr, 0, &dsi);

Lesson 4.4's colour_target_format comment predicted this exact call — "it is wrong for a shadow map, a post-processing chain, or a headless test… Module 6 lives on this method."

The vertex shader is four lines and one attribute. The mesh is the identical gpu_vertex_pnu, 48 bytes with position, normal, uv and tangent interleaved; this pipeline declares the position at offset 0 and the hardware never fetches the rest. Lesson 6.7 established that a vertex layout is per-pipeline state when describe grew its with_tangent parameter; this is the same fact at its limit, and it is why a depth pass is cheap on bandwidth as well as on arithmetic.

Why there is a fragment shader that does nothing

shadow.frag.hlsl is void main() {}. SDL_GPUGraphicsPipelineCreateInfo has a fragment_shader field and SDL's header does not document NULL as a legal value for it, so we do not pass one. ⚠ VERIFY: several backends do accept a null fragment shader for a depth-only pipeline, and if SDL ever states that it forwards NULL this file becomes deletable — check SDL3/SDL_gpu.h. Until then, an empty shader is the portable spelling: five instructions the driver optimises to none, against a crash on the one backend that disagrees.

It is also the honest home for the thing a depth pass sometimes does need. An alpha-tested caster — a leaf, a chain-link fence — has to sample its albedo here and discard, which is why "depth-only" and "no fragment shader" are not quite the same statement.

8.3 — A depth target that is also a texture

create_depth gains a sampled flag, which adds SDL_GPU_TEXTUREUSAGE_SAMPLER to the usage. It is a parameter rather than always-on because the comment it replaces was right: asking for a usage you do not need can force the driver into a slower layout, and the scene's own depth buffer is read by nothing.

There is a second cost, and it is the one that bites on phones. The scene's depth attachment has used SDL_GPU_STOREOP_DONT_CARE since Lesson 4.7 — nothing reads it after the pass, so on tiled hardware it never leaves tile memory. A shadow map is read by a later pass, so its store op must be STORE and the whole buffer is written out to main memory. The comment in gpu_scene.cpp named this moment two modules ago.

And whether a depth format can be sampled at all is a question, not an assumption. SDL guarantees D16_UNORM as an attachment and guarantees nothing about sampling any depth format, so a device can legally support a depth buffer and refuse a shadow map. supported_shadow_format asks for both usages in one query, because that is the texture we are actually going to create — a device may support a format as an attachment and as a texture and still refuse the combination.

8.4 — The comparison sampler, and a third identity element

gpu_sampler::create_comparison sets the two fields §6 exists to justify — enable_compare = true and compare_op = LESS_OR_EQUAL — and this discharges the ⚠ VERIFY Lesson 6.7 left open: both names are confirmed against SDL3/SDL_gpu.h. Every other field is dictated by the job, which is why it is a named function rather than four more defaulted parameters: LINEAR, because filtering the comparison results is the entire point; CLAMP_TO_EDGE, because a lookup outside the map must not wrap round to the far side of the scene.

In the fragment shader the pair is a different type from the two above it: 

Texture2D<float>       shadow_map     : register(t2, space2);
SamplerComparisonState shadow_sampler : register(s2, space2);

And the scene renderer gains a third fallback binding, which makes the pattern explicit at last: hand the shader the identity element of the feature it is missing. White is the identity for a multiply (4.7), lavender for a basis change (6.7), and 1.0 is the identity for a depth comparison — a map saying the nearest surface along every ray is the far plane says nothing occludes anything.

The one that cannot be uploaded

The first two fallbacks are 1×1 images pushed through a copy pass. SDL_UploadToGPUTexture cannot target a depth texture at all, so the only way to put a value into this one is to have a render pass clear it: begin, clear to 1.0, end, draw nothing. Three lines, completely ordinary, and the shape every "clear a render target" helper in every engine eventually takes.

8.5 — The lookup, and the varyings it does not cost

The textbook arrangement computes the light-space position in the vertex shader and interpolates it — four more floats per fragment, on every draw in the scene, shadowed or not, because a varying list is part of the shader signature and cannot vary per material.

We do it in the fragment shader instead, from input.world — the varying Lesson 3.7 added for the specular term. That is only legitimate because of §3's finding: the light's projection is orthographic, so w is exactly 1, the map from world to light clip is affine, and evaluating an affine function at an interpolated point gives the same answer as interpolating its values at the corners. One matrix multiply per fragment, zero new varyings, and Lesson 6.7's warning about the cost of a fourth varying does not have to be paid a fifth time.

The uniform block grows from 64 bytes to 176 — a float4x4 and eleven floats. Lesson 6.7 made a point of a flag that cost zero bytes, so the contrast is worth naming: this is a per-frame push, one copy for the whole frame, where material_uniforms is charged once per draw. The two blocks are billed at completely different rates and the design pressure on them differs in exactly that proportion.

One function fills all of it. gpu_shadow_map::fill_uniforms takes the same light_camera and shadow_settings the CPU renderer reads, because eleven floats transcribed twice is how a shadow ends up behaving differently on the two paths for a reason nobody can find.

The measurement that was measuring its own grid

§C's first version reported 3.3% acne where the truth is 50%, and a worst error at 22% of a bound that is in fact tight. Nothing was wrong with the renderer. The harness walked a plain 96×96 grid across the plane, whose spacing came out at 4.8 shadow texels — so every sample landed at nearly the same position inside its texel, and the measurement covered a sliver of the sub-texel space instead of all of it.

The fix is a Weyl sequence: step the sub-texel offset by the golden ratio's fractional part, the number hardest to approximate by a rational and therefore the one that never falls back into step with the grid. A regular grid sampled against a regular grid measures the beat frequency, not the thing. That is not a testing footnote — it is the same aliasing the shadow map itself suffers from, it is why a shadow's edge crawls when the light moves, and it is the reason cascades snap their box to texel boundaries.

11.1 — The software renderer

gltf_view gained a ground plane — a shadow needs a receiver, and acne is a pattern across a lit surface, so a scene with nothing large and lit in it cannot show one. 

# the artefact, first (Lesson 3.5's rule: show the failure)
./build/demos/gltf_view --model torus.obj --shadow 1024 --bias none --pcf 0

# the derived fix
./build/demos/gltf_view --model torus.obj --shadow 1024 --bias slope-scaled --pcf 0

# and a soft edge
./build/demos/gltf_view --model torus.obj --shadow 1024 --pcf 2

# what a small map looks like
./build/demos/gltf_view --model torus.obj --shadow 128

What to look for, in order. With --bias none: a correct shadow under the torus, and everything else covered in the moiré of Figure 4 — 36,786 pixels of a 480×270 frame. With --bias slope-scaled: the ground goes completely clean, and a little speckle remains on the torus itself at its grazing edges, which is §4.3's clamp being honest rather than a bug. With --pcf 2: the shadow's edge softens by about two texels, and the ground stays clean, because the bias widened with the kernel. With --shadow 128: the same shadow with visibly stair-stepped edges — one texel now covers eight times as much world, and every formula in §4 scaled with it.

The run also prints the two numbers the whole lesson is built from: 

  shadow map    : 1024x1024, bias SLOPE-SCALED (derived), PCF 1x1, caster cull none
  shadow pass   : 2306 caster triangles into 1048576 texels, 8.50 ms
  fit           : 0.0115 world units per texel, depth range 9.653

11.2 — The GPU renderer

sandbox renders the depth pass every frame and binds the map at fragment slot 2. Three keys:

  • [1] — shadows on/off. Off is shadow_strength = 0, which short-circuits the lookup, so the picture is bit-for-bit Lesson 6.7's.
  • [2] — cycle the bias policy: none → constant → slope-scaled → normal-offset. Two of those four exist to show failures, which is Lesson 3.5's rule.
  • [3] — cycle the PCF radius, 0 through 3. Watch the edge soften, and watch the ground stay clean — the reach correction is what buys that.

On startup it logs whether this machine can have shadows at all: 

  shadow map      : 1024x1024 D32/D24/D16, sampled + comparison sampler

…or unavailable on this device, which is a capability report rather than an error: the demo runs, the renderer binds its 1×1 far map, and everything is lit.

12Common Pitfalls & Debugging

Shadow-mapping failures: symptom, cause, fix
SymptomCauseFix
Dense moiré stripes across every lit surface No bias, or one far too small. §4. Slope-scaled bias. If it persists, check the reach against the PCF radius (§6.2).
Shadows detached from the objects casting them, worst at contact Bias too large — a constant one sized for the steepest surface. §5.1. Slope-scaled. The quantity being over-corrected is bias × depth_range world units.
Clean at one PCF setting, speckled at the next The bias was sized for one tap. §6.2 — the 3×3 kernel reaches 3× further. pcf_reach_texels(radius) in the bias, not a constant.
Everything is lit; the map appears empty Front-face culling on open geometry (§5.4), or the caster list is empty, or the light's box does not contain the casters. Log shadow_stats::triangles. Zero says the pass, not the lookup.
The whole screen goes black, or nothing draws A declared sampler slot with nothing bound. The shader declares t2 and the draw bound only t0 and t1. Bind the fallback. And bind all three in ONE SDL_BindGPUFragmentSamplers: a partial bind replaces the range it names rather than merging.
A validation error the moment the shadow texture is bound An ordinary sampler on a comparison lookup, or a comparison sampler on a colour texture. create_comparison for the map; SamplerComparisonState in the shader.
The shadow is offset half a texel from its caster The lookup's NDC → texel mapping is not the one the pass used to write. Share the viewport. And check round-versus-floor against where your rasterizer samples (§4.4).
Shadow smeared to the horizon beyond the light's box CLAMP_TO_EDGE is reporting the edge texel's occluder for everything outside. Range-check the NDC in the shader and return "lit". Clamping is the sampler's fallback, not the semantics you want.
Casters flicker in and out of their own shadow at the box's edge A vertex sitting exactly on the near plane, where the clipper's test is decided by the last bit of a float. Pad the fit (§7.3).
Shadows are blocky even at 1024 The box is fitted to something huge — usually a ground plane included as a caster. Fit to the casters. Log world_per_texel; if it is centimetres when the scene is metres, the box is the problem.
Shadows are pure black The visibility term multiplied the ambient term too. §1. It multiplies E only.
Your acne measurement disagrees with your eyes The sampling grid is commensurate with the texel grid. §9. Jitter with a Weyl sequence.

13Exercises

  1. Find the resolution where the bias stops mattering. The derived bias is proportional to world_per_texel. Run gltf_view at 128, 256, 512, 1024 and 2048 with --bias none, and record the fraction of ground pixels that speckle. Predict the trend before you measure it.

    Hint. The acne fraction should barely move — half of every texel is downhill regardless of how big a texel is. What changes is the spatial frequency, and therefore how it aliases against the screen. Predicting "less acne" and measuring "the same acne, finer" is the useful failure here.

  2. Make peter-panning photogenic. §5.1 measures it; the demo barely shows it, because the torus meets the ground along a thin circle at an oblique light angle. Change the light direction to something nearer vertical and put a box on the ground, then sweep --constant-bias from 0 to 0.1 and find where the contact shadow disappears. Check that the value matches gap / depth_range.

  3. A spot light. Replace orthographic with perspective in fit_directional and give the light a position. Almost everything survives; two things do not. Find them.

    Hint. depth_range stops being one number — §3.3 — so the conversion from a world-space error to a device-space bias becomes position-dependent. And w stops being 1, so §8.5's argument for computing the light-space position in the fragment shader needs re-examining. (It survives, but for a different reason: a homogeneous coordinate is still affine in the world position. Work out why.)

  4. Measure what the fallback costs. gpu_scene_renderer now binds three sampler slots on every texture change instead of two. Use Lesson 4.9's frame log to count the binds in a frame, then work out whether hoisting the shadow binding out of the per-item loop is possible at all.

    Hint. Read the comment in gpu_scene.cpp about what a partial SDL_BindGPUFragmentSamplers does to slots outside its range. This is a "the obvious optimisation is a bug" exercise.

  5. Engine challenge: variance shadow maps. Store depth and depth squared in a two-channel colour target, and use Chebyshev's inequality to get a probabilistic visibility that can be blurred, mipmapped and filtered like an ordinary texture — no comparison sampler, no bias, no acne. Then find the artefact it trades those away for.

    Hint. It is called light bleeding, and it shows up wherever two occluders at very different depths share a filter footprint. Notice that this is the same 0.3/0.9/0.5 situation from §6.1, arriving from the other side.

14Recap & Next

The engine can now answer a question it has never been able to ask: can this point see the light? Concretely, it gained:

  • an orthographic projection, derived from an interval remap, whose w stays exactly 1 — which makes device depth affine, precision uniform, and a fragment's light-space position recoverable for free;
  • an AABB, at its fourth call site, and a fit that squares a box around a scene for a light that has no position;
  • a shadow map on both renderers — the CPU's being collect_triangles and draw_triangles with a different camera, the GPU's being a second render pass with no colour attachment at all;
  • a bias that is derived rather than tuned: reach × texel × tan θ ÷ range, measured to within 8% of the bound it claims;
  • four bias policies, two of which exist to show their own failure modes;
  • PCF through a comparison sampler, and the reason the comparison has to come first;
  • and the first place in the engine where the geometric normal and the shading normal must be told apart — Lesson 6.7's gift, collected.

Three things are worth carrying forward. The first is that an artefact with a name is usually an artefact with a formula: acne looked like a mystery and turned out to be half a texel of lateral travel times a tangent. The second is the reach finding — a correct bias that stops being correct the moment a kernel widens, and arrives looking like somebody else's bug. The third is §9's: a measurement can alias against the thing it is measuring, and a number that disagrees with your eyes deserves suspicion in both directions.

Next: Lesson 6.9 — Cascaded Shadow Maps. One map over a whole outdoor scene gives the near field far too few texels, and §11's --shadow 128 is a preview of what that looks like everywhere. The fix is to split the camera's frustum by distance and give each slice its own map — which brings back everything this lesson could fit to a static scene and leave alone: a box that moves with the camera, and therefore texel snapping; a per-cascade world_per_texel, and therefore a per-cascade bias; and the seam between two cascades, which is a new artefact with a new name.
