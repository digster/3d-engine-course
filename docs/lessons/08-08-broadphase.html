Module 8 — Physics · Lesson 8.8

Broadphase: A Uniform Grid

Four lessons of narrow phase have built something precise, and this one is about the fact that you cannot afford to run it. 8.7 measured the whole chain — GJK, EPA, the manifold — at 395 ns for one pair; §1 re-measures it here at 432 ns on 8.7's own fixture. A scene of n bodies has n(n−1)/2 pairs, so that budget is gone at n = 278. Not at ten thousand bodies, not at “some large number” — at two hundred and seventy-eight crates. A broadphase is the stage that asks the cheap question of every pair and hands the narrow phase only the survivors, and it gets to be cheap because it is allowed to be wrong in exactly one direction: it may report pairs that do not touch, and it may not omit one that does. Everything in this lesson falls out of that asymmetry. We build a hashed uniform grid, rebuilt from scratch every frame, and the two things that are actually hard turn out not to be the hashing. The first is duplicates: two proxies sharing four cells meet four times, and the usual fixes — a std::set of pair keys, a sort-and-unique — cost 45% and 5% of what the entire broadphase costs. There is an exact rule that costs three max calls and no memory, and §5 predicts how many duplicates it removes in closed form and gets 3271 against a counted 3271. The second is the teapot in the stadium: one ground plate at a 1 m cell wants 80,802 cells to itself, nearly three times what a scene of two thousand crates wants, and it is alone in almost every one of them. Along the way three claims that everyone repeats get measured rather than repeated. The famous floor-versus-cast bug loses zero pairs here, because a range-walk grid needs only that its cell map be monotone — the mistake that really loses pairs is the innocent-looking one next to it, and it loses 66.7% of them. The rule of thumb for cell size is replaced by a two-term cost model whose constants come out the same on scenes of different density, and whose minimum moves as the sixth root of the crowd. And the textbook spatial hash really is 25.7% worse than chance on a lattice of crates — and stays, because §7 also measures what that is worth. Ships broadphase.hpp, a demo that checks the contract live against n(n−1)/2 every frame, ten measured sections and 51 checks, and an honest crossover: below about a hundred bodies this whole stage is a net loss.

1. The problem: the quadratic is already here

Four lessons of Module 8 have gone into one question, asked of one pair of shapes. 8.4 gave the Separating Axis Theorem and an exact answer for boxes. 8.5 replaced the enumeration with a search, so that any convex shape could be asked. 8.6 turned “they overlap” into “by this much, in this direction”. 8.7 turned that into a set of contact points a solver can actually push on. At the end of 8.7 §9 the whole chain — GJK, then EPA, then the manifold — cost 395 ns for one pair of crates.

That is a good number. It is also, in a sense the rest of this module will keep discovering, the wrong number to be proud of, because nothing in a game asks about one pair.

Here is the arithmetic, and it takes one line. A scene of n bodies contains n(n−1)/2 distinct pairs — every body against every other, each counted once. At sixty frames a second a frame is 16.67 milliseconds. So the largest scene you can afford, if you hand every pair to the narrow phase, is the n that solves

\[ \frac{n(n-1)}{2}\,\cdot\,t_{\text{pair}} \;\le\; 16.67\ \text{ms} \]

— that is: half of n times n minus one, multiplied by the cost of one pair test, must fit in one frame.

§1 of this lesson's harness re-measures t_pair rather than quoting 8.7's, because a quoted timing is a timing from somebody else's machine. On 8.7's own fixture — crates yawed about the up axis, overlapping by 5 cm, which is what a settling stack looks like — it comes out at 432.4 ns. (The same measurement on freely tumbling crates driven deep into each other gives 795.9 ns; the lesson uses the smaller number throughout, because it makes every claim below harder to support.) Put 432.4 ns into the inequality and solve the quadratic: 

  16.67 ms budget: all-narrow at n = 278, all-AABB at n = 5293

Two hundred and seventy-eight bodies. That is a small pile of crates. It is fewer bodies than a single collapsing wall in a game you have played. The quadratic is not a problem that shows up when your game gets big; it is a problem that shows up before your game gets interesting, and it shows up as a hard ceiling rather than as a slope, because the cost grows as the square: double the scene and the pair count quadruples.

Look at the red curve in Figure 1 and notice what it is not. It is not steep near the origin and then fine. It is a straight line on log-log paper with slope 2, which means it has no flat part anywhere — every doubling costs four times as much, at every scale, for ever. There is no scene size at which you are “past the worst of it”.

2. The observation that makes a fix possible

Think about what those 499,500 pair tests at n = 1000 are actually doing. Almost every one of them is two crates at opposite ends of the room, and for each of those the narrow phase runs GJK — building a simplex, iterating toward the origin, converging, and reporting a distance of eleven metres. It does the full, careful, correct thing to establish something a child could see at a glance.

And here is the asymmetry that the whole of this lesson lives inside: establishing that two objects are far apart is a much easier question than establishing exactly how they touch. The narrow phase cannot exploit that, because it does not know in advance which question it is being asked. A separate, earlier stage can.

The idea

Put a cheap, conservative test in front of the expensive exact one. Run the cheap test on every pair; run the expensive test only on the pairs the cheap test could not rule out. The cheap test is the broadphase; the expensive one is the narrow phase Module 8 has spent four lessons building.

The cheapest useful test we already own. 6.16 built axis-aligned bounding boxes for frustum culling and 8.4 moved aabb into engine/math/ precisely so that physics could use it without reaching into gfx/. Two AABBs are apart exactly when their intervals are disjoint on one of the three world axes — the Separating Axis Theorem with twelve of its fifteen axes deleted by symmetry, because an AABB's face normals are the world axes. Six comparisons. Measured: 

  narrow phase, resting crates (8.7's)     432.4 ns
  narrow phase, tumbling and deep          795.9 ns
  one AABB overlap test                    1.190 ns
  ratio                                      363x

Three hundred and sixty-three times cheaper. Substituting one for the other moves the wall from 278 bodies to 5,293 — and, crucially, does not remove it, because n(n−1)/2 AABB tests is still a quadratic. You can see both walls in Figure 1 as the two circles on the amber line. Eighteen times more room, and the same shape of catastrophe waiting at the end of it.

So the broadphase cannot merely be a cheaper test applied to every pair. It has to avoid looking at most pairs at all. That is what the grid is for, and it is why the rest of this lesson is about space rather than about arithmetic.

3. Building intuition: two stages, and an asymmetry

Before any data structure, the contract. It is two sentences and it is the most important thing on this page.

The broadphase contract

A broadphase may report pairs that do not touch. A broadphase may not omit a pair that does.

Read the two errors and price them.

A false positive — a pair reported that turns out not to be touching — costs exactly one narrow-phase call. That is 432 nanoseconds, and the answer that comes back is correct: the narrow phase says “these are apart”, and nothing downstream is misled. The broadphase is allowed as many of these as it likes; they show up in a profile as a number that could be better and never as a bug.

A false negative — a pair that really touches and is never reported — has no stage downstream that could notice. The narrow phase is never called. No manifold is generated. The solver has nothing to solve. The two objects pass through each other, and the symptom reaches the player as “sometimes things fall through the floor”, with no line of code to attach a breakpoint to, at a frequency that depends on velocity and position and which therefore cannot be reproduced.

That asymmetry is not a footnote. It is the design rule, and every approximation in this lesson is made in the conservative direction on purpose: cell ranges round outward, a proxy the grid cannot place is kept rather than dropped, and a candidate is rejected only when a cheap exact test says so. When you are writing a broadphase and you catch yourself thinking “this case is so unlikely”, you are about to write a false negative.

3.1 What the two stages cost each other

The arrangement only pays if the broadphase is enormously cheaper per pair than the narrow phase, because the broadphase runs on everything and the narrow phase runs on a fraction. Figure 2 is the measured version of that sentence, on a scene of two thousand bodies.

Two thousand bodies is 1,999,000 pairs. The grid reports 983 of them — a factor of 2,034 — and costs 0.091 ms doing it, which is 17.7% of the two stages together. The narrow phase then spends 0.426 ms on the 983 survivors. Had we skipped the broadphase, the same narrow phase on all 1,999,000 pairs would have taken an implied 844 ms: fifty frames' worth of budget, for one frame.

Note the shape of the trade. The broadphase does not make the narrow phase faster — it is the identical code doing the identical work per pair. It makes there be two thousand times less of it. Almost every optimisation in a real engine has this shape, and it is worth naming, because the instinct when a profile says “the narrow phase is 82% of collision detection” is to go and optimise the narrow phase. The 82% is fine. It is the 18% that bought it.

4. Checking a thing that is allowed to be wrong

8.5 and 8.6 had a hard time verifying themselves. GJK's answer is a distance, and the only second opinion available was another distance algorithm with its own bugs; EPA's answer is a penetration depth, and 8.6 §8 had to prove that the SAT's minimum-overlap axis is the true minimum translation before it had an oracle at all.

This lesson has it easy, and it is worth pausing on why, because the luxury is a consequence of the contract rather than of the algorithm. A broadphase answers a question that something else can answer exactly, in bounded time, for any input: which pairs of boxes overlap? Test all of them.

Nine lines, and it does three separate jobs that the rest of this lesson leans on constantly.

It is the oracle. Every claim below about the grid being correct is checked by running both on the same scene and comparing the pair lists as sets. It is the baseline, because “the grid is faster” is not a claim until there is a number it is faster than. And — this one surprises people — it is the right answer below some scene size, which §10 measures and which turns out not to be small.

Notice j = i + 1. That is the whole of why this is n(n−1)/2 and not n²: a pair is unordered, so starting the inner loop past the outer index visits each one exactly once. And notice that it still sorts the two indices before pushing. The proxies arrive in whatever order the caller had them; the pairs leave in canonical order, always, which is a promise §11 will explain the cost of breaking.

4.1 The check itself

§3 of the harness runs twelve independent placements of 900 tumbling proxies — 5,230 genuinely overlapping pairs across the twelve — and asks two questions of each frame: how many pairs of the oracle's are missing from the grid's (the contract), and are the two sets in fact equal (stronger than the contract, and true here because the grid runs the AABB test itself). 

  12 frames, 900 proxies: 5230 true pairs
  missed by the grid                         0
  frames on which the sets were equal       12 / 12

Zero is the only acceptable number in the middle row. Note that this is a much stronger test than “the demo looks right”: a broadphase that dropped one pair in ten thousand would look perfect in a demo and would still put an object through a floor every few minutes.

5. Cells, and two ways to get the cell map wrong

Now the structure. Cut space into a lattice of equal boxes — cells — and give each one a list of the proxies that touch it. Two proxies that share no cell cannot possibly overlap, so the only pairs worth testing are pairs found together in some cell. That is the entire idea, and it is the reason a grid can beat a quadratic: it never enumerates pairs, it enumerates cells, and most cells hold nothing.

Which cell is a point in? Divide by the cell size and take the floor:

\[ c(x) \;=\; \left\lfloor \frac{x}{s} \right\rfloor \]

— the cell index on one axis is x divided by the cell size s, rounded down.

Work one through by hand, with s = 2 m. A point at x = 5.4 gives 5.4 / 2 = 2.7, floor 2 — cell 2, which spans [4, 6). A point at x = −0.4 gives −0.2, floor −1 — cell −1, which spans [−2, 0). A point at x = −2.0 gives exactly −1.0, floor −1 — the cell boundary belongs to the cell above it, which is what makes the map a partition rather than an overlap.

5.1 A proxy is not a point

And here is the first thing a first draft gets wrong. A proxy is a box, so it does not occupy a cell; it occupies a range of cells — every integer from the cell of its minimum corner to the cell of its maximum corner, on all three axes.

Panel A of Figure 3 is one proxy spanning cells 1 through 3 on x and 1 through 2 on y: six insertions for one object. The natural first draft does something much simpler — objects are small and cells are big, so put each proxy in the one cell containing its centre — and it is a false-negative machine. Two crates a centimetre apart, either side of a cell boundary, are in different cells and are never tested. Measured on 3,000 proxies: 

  control 2: one cell per proxy, by its centre
    true overlapping pairs                  2836
    lost                                    1892  (66.7%)

Two thirds of the pairs there are. Not an edge case, not a rare configuration — two thirds. The reason it is such an attractive mistake is that it is nearly right: the third it finds are the ones where both objects happen to sit well inside the same cell, which is most of the ones you will notice while you are debugging it.

5.2 The floor, and the warning everybody repeats

Every tutorial on grids says the same thing: use std::floor, never a cast to int, because a C++ cast truncates toward zero. Under truncation −0.4 and +0.4 both give cell 0, so cells −1 and 0 merge into one double-width cell straddling the origin, and every cell to the left of it shifts by one. Panel B of Figure 3 draws exactly that.

The warning is real. The conclusion usually drawn from it — that truncation loses pairs — is false for this structure, and the harness was written expecting to demonstrate the opposite. It measured zero. 

  control 1: cell index by cast, not floor
    3000 proxies centred on the origin, cell 2.33 m
    pairs lost by truncation                   0
    pairs lost by floor                        0

The reason is one line of order theory, and it is worth having. A range-walk grid finds a pair when the two proxies' cell ranges intersect. A cell map is a function from the reals to the integers, and both floor and truncation are monotone — non-decreasing. If two intervals overlap, so do their images under a non-decreasing map:

\[ \max(a_0, b_0) \le \min(a_1, b_1) \;\Longrightarrow\; c(\max(a_0, b_0)) \le c(\min(a_1, b_1)) \]

— if the later of the two starts is no later than the earlier of the two ends, then applying a non-decreasing map to both sides preserves the inequality, and the mapped ranges still meet.

Monotonicity is all the range walk needs. It does not need the cells to be the same size, or contiguous, or centred anywhere in particular. Truncation is monotone, so it is correct, and so would be a map that made every third cell twice as wide.

What truncation costs is occupancy. Eight cells become one, and a cell's pair loop is quadratic in what is inside it, so where the correct map runs eight small loops the truncating one runs a single large one. Measured on a dense scene centred on the origin: 

    6000 dense proxies, the {-1,0}^3 neighbourhood:
      fullest cell, truncating                83 proxies
      fullest cell, flooring                  28 proxies
      pair tests there, truncating          7844
      pair tests there, flooring            1957
      excess                                 4.0x

Four times the comparisons — in one cell, of a scene with thousands. It is a footnote, not a bug. Use floor anyway: it is the same instruction count, and a cell map that behaves identically everywhere is one less thing that is only true away from the origin. But know which of the two mistakes on this page is the one that will cost you a weekend, because it is not the one with the famous name.

Two corrections, in one section

§4 of the harness was written to show that truncation loses pairs and that a naive grid is obviously broken. It disproved the first claim and relocated the second. This is the third time in Module 8 that a control has answered a question the section did not mean to ask — 8.6 §1 found a knob measuring exactly zero, 8.7 §9 found a control convicting the code of the test's mistake — and the pattern is the same each time: a measurement written to confirm something is only worth writing if it is allowed to refuse.

6. Duplicates, and the cell that owns a pair

Insert every proxy into every cell of its range, walk each cell, test every pair inside it. Now count how many times a given pair gets reported.

Two proxies whose cell ranges overlap are both present in every cell of the overlap. If their ranges share a 2×2 block, they meet four times and the pair is reported four times. Figure 4 draws that case; the harness measures a worst case of eight shared cells on ordinary crates, and it gets worse fast for anything larger.

Duplicates are not merely untidy. A duplicated pair means a duplicated narrow-phase call at 432 ns, a duplicated manifold, and — once 8.10's solver arrives — the same contact solved twice in one iteration, which is a stiffer contact than the one you asked for.

6.1 The two fixes everybody writes

The obvious answer is to remember which pairs you have already emitted. 8.7 already gave us the key: pair_key(a, b) packs two 32-bit indices into one 64-bit integer, order-independently. So keep a std::set<std::uint64_t> and skip anything already in it. Or, if you know sets are slow, collect all the emissions into a vector, sort it, and run std::unique.

Both work. Here is what they cost, on the same 2,000-proxy scene, measured against the entire broadphase they are supposed to be a small part of: 

  control: deduplicating 2131 keys instead
    std::set                               0.036 ms
    sort + unique                          0.004 ms
    the WHOLE broadphase, rule included    0.080 ms
    the set, as a share of the broadphase     45.2%

The std::set costs 45% of the whole stage — a red-black tree allocating a node per key and chasing pointers through it, to solve a problem that is not actually about memory. The sort is far better at 5%, and it is still 5% spent on a question that has a closed-form answer.

6.2 The owner cell

Here is the observation. The overlap of two axis-aligned cell ranges is itself an axis-aligned range of cells — a box of cells — and a box has exactly one minimum corner. So:

The owner-cell rule

Report a pair only from the cell at the minimum corner of the overlap of its two cell ranges, which is max(lo_a, lo_b) on each axis.

The proof that this reports each pair exactly once is one sentence in each direction. At least once: the owner cell lies inside both ranges by construction, and every proxy is inserted into every cell of its own range, so both proxies are present there and the pair is examined. At most once: there is only one minimum corner, so the test this cell == owner cell passes in exactly one of the shared cells.

It is exact, not heuristic. It costs three max calls and three integer comparisons, and no memory at all.

And here is the instrument that makes this section a measurement rather than an argument. The number of duplicate meetings is computable in closed form: for each pair whose ranges meet, the number of shared cells is the product over the three axes of the overlap's width, and the duplicates are that minus one. Sum it over the scene and compare it against the counter the grid keeps while doing something completely different: 

  2000 proxies, cell 2.322 m
  pairs whose cell ranges meet              5204
  most cells one pair shares                   8
  duplicate meetings, predicted             3271
  duplicate meetings, counted               3271
  pairs reported                             963
  duplicates in the reported list              0

3271 and 3271. Two completely independent routes to the same integer, agreeing exactly rather than approximately — which is the kind of check worth reaching for whenever it is available, because it cannot be passed by accident.

7. The cell size, and a cost model instead of a rule of thumb

One number decides how well the grid works, and every source states the same rule of thumb about it: make the cell about the size of an average object. That is good advice and it is not an explanation, so let us build one, and then check it.

Two costs pull in opposite directions.

Too small, and each object spans many cells. A box of extent e at cell size s spans about e/s + 1 cells on each axis, so the number of insertions is

\[ E(s) \;\approx\; n\left(\frac{e}{s} + 1\right)^{3} \]

— the entry count is the body count times the cube of (extent over cell size, plus one). Halve the cell and each object claims roughly eight times as many.

Too large, and each cell holds many objects. A cell of side s in a scene of density ρ holds about ρs³ of them, its pair loop is quadratic in that, and there are about V/s³ occupied cells, so

\[ T(s) \;\approx\; \frac{V}{s^{3}} \cdot \frac{(\rho s^{3})^{2}}{2} \;=\; \frac{n\rho\,s^{3}}{2} \]

— the pair-test count is the number of cells times the pairs inside each, which simplifies to the body count times the density times the cube of the cell size.

Both wings are cubic. That is the fact worth carrying away, and it has an immediate consequence. Setting the derivative of a·E(s) + b·T(s) to zero, with the small-s approximation E ≈ n e³/s³:

\[ s^{*} \;=\; \left(\frac{2a\,e^{3}}{b\,\rho}\right)^{1/6} \]

— the best cell size goes as the sixth root of everything in it, including the scene's density.

A sixth root is an extraordinarily flat function. Quadruple the density of a scene and the optimum moves by 4^(1/6) = 1.26 — twenty-six per cent, for a fourfold change in the thing it depends on. That is why the vague rule of thumb survives: the optimum barely moves, so any formula in the right neighbourhood is close enough.

7.1 Fitting the model

The derivation gives the shape. To get the number we need the two constants, and here the grid helps: broadphase_stats already reports entries and bucket_tests, and both are deterministic — they do not vary by a single count between runs. So instead of trusting a timer, sweep the cell size, read the two counts, and solve

\[ \text{cost} \;=\; a\,E \;+\; b\,T \]

from two rows chosen because each is dominated by one term: at 0.5 m the sweep inserts 2.6 entries for every pair test it runs, and at 15 m it runs 78 pair tests for every entry. Two equations, two unknowns, no fitting and no judgement.

The result is better than it had any right to be. On the sparse scene the fit gives 8.09 ns per entry and 5.45 ns per pair test; on a scene four times denser, 8.11 and 5.44. The constants are the same because they are properties of this machine — a cache miss and a comparison — rather than of the scene, which is exactly what a cost model should find and exactly what a timing measurement of the whole build cannot tell you.

And the model predicts where the minimum is, from counts that carry no noise: 

  model minimum    3.30 m  = 2.13 x mean side       (sparse)
  model minimum    2.05 m  = 1.32 x mean side       (dense)

Two point one three and one point three two, for a fourfold change in density. The sixth-root prediction said 1.26×; the measurement says 1.61×, and the gap is the +1 in (e/s + 1)³, which is not negligible when s is only twice e. The scaling law has the right shape and the wrong constant, which is the usual fate of an asymptotic argument evaluated near the middle.

7.2 Why the timer was the wrong instrument

Look at the spread column in the harness's own sweep, which reports (max − min) / median over forty-one runs: 

   cell     entries    bucket    best  sprd
      m                 tests      ms
   1.50       30656     22129   0.393  0.22
   2.00       20752     19105   0.242  0.76
   2.50       16109     22747   0.186  1.06
   3.00       13314     24223   0.192  1.18
   4.00       10324     31306   0.212  1.15

A spread above 1.0 means the slowest of forty-one runs took more than twice as long as the fastest. At a few hundred microseconds per build, the operating system's interruptions are larger than the thing being measured — so the timing cannot distinguish 2.5 m from 4 m, and the “fastest measured” row moves between them from run to run. Every timing in this lesson is therefore a minimum over many runs rather than a median, which is the standard robust estimator for “how fast can this code go”; and where a number could be had without a clock at all, this lesson uses the count instead.

3.10's rule, four modules later

Measure, do not guess — and its corollary, which this section is: when the measurement is noisier than the difference you are trying to see, find a quantity that does not need a clock. entries and bucket_tests were added to broadphase_stats because a grid is the system in this engine where intuition is least reliable. They paid for themselves here.

7.3 And the control that makes the sweep mean anything

A cell size is a performance decision. So the thing to check, at every point of the sweep, is that the answer does not change: 

  pair sets identical at every size          13 / 13

Thirteen cell sizes across three decades, entry counts differing by a factor of three thousand, and the pair list identical at every one of them. A grid that reported different pairs at different tunings would be reporting at least one of them wrongly, and the bug would present as “the physics changes when I change a constant that is supposed to be about speed”.

Which leaves auto_cell_size, whose job is to be in the right neighbourhood without being told anything about the scene. It returns one and a half times the mean box's longest side — between the two measured optima, since the “too large” wing grows with crowding and the “too small” wing does not — and it lands at 1.14× and 0.97× the best measured time on the two scenes. The longest side rather than the mean side, because a cell has to hold a box in its worst direction; the mean rather than the maximum, because the maximum is one ground plane away from being useless, which is §9's subject.

8. Hashing rather than allocating

A grid needs somewhere to put the entries. The obvious answer is a three-dimensional array indexed by cell coordinate, and it has two problems that are both fatal and neither of them is speed.

It allocates for cells that are empty. A one-kilometre world at a one-metre cell is 10⁹ cells. At four bytes an index that is four gigabytes of memory for a scene of forty crates, almost all of it storing the fact that nothing is there.

It has an edge. An array is finite, so a proxy that walks off it has to be clamped, wrapped, or rejected — and two of those three are silent false-negative machines. Clamping puts everything beyond the boundary into the boundary cell, which is correct and catastrophically slow; wrapping makes two objects a kilometre apart share a cell, which is correct and merely wasteful; rejecting loses pairs, which is the one thing we may not do. And all three require the engine to decide, in advance, how big the world is.

Hashing the cell coordinate removes both at once. The table is sized against the number of occupied cells rather than the number of possible ones, so memory follows the scene; and a cell coordinate is an unbounded int32, so there is no edge and no world bounds to declare. A scene that happens to be a kilometre across costs exactly what a scene a metre across costs.

The price is collisions: two unrelated cells can land in the same bucket, and their proxies then get compared. That costs a comparison and produces no wrong answers, because every entry carries its cell coordinate and a pair whose two entries disagree about the cell is rejected. Note the direction: a collision adds work, never removes an answer, which is the only direction the contract permits.

8.1 Measuring a hash against chance

The standard spatial hash comes from Teschner and colleagues' 2003 paper: multiply each coordinate by a large prime and exclusive-or the results.

The cast through uint32_t before multiplying is not decoration: signed overflow is undefined behaviour in C++, and a negative cell coordinate is entirely ordinary here — it is one metre to the left of the origin.

Is it a good hash? “Good” needs a reference, and there is one that is neither of the candidates: balls in bins. Throwing k distinct cells into m buckets at random puts more than one cell in a bucket

\[ m\left[1 - \left(1-\tfrac{1}{m}\right)^{k} - k\tfrac{1}{m}\left(1-\tfrac{1}{m}\right)^{k-1}\right] \]

times, in expectation — the number of buckets, less those expected to be empty, less those expected to hold exactly one.

That is what a good hash should match. Measured on two fixtures — a lattice of crates on a floor, which is what a physics scene looks like the moment it settles, and the same number of cells scattered at random: 

   fixture      cells  buckets   xor   mix   ideal
  lattice       1445     4096   254   209     202
    xor is +25.7% off ideal, mix is +3.4%
  scattered     7301    16384  1175  1240    1215

On random cells the textbook hash sits on the prediction, which is the control: the difference belongs to the input, not to the measurement. On a lattice it is 25.7% above chance, while a stronger mixing function is at 3.4%. Physics scenes are lattices — things come to rest in rows, on floors, at regular spacings — so this is the input that matters.

8.2 And why it stays anyway

Having found the textbook answer wanting, the honest next step is to ask what the deficit is actually worth. Two more measurements.

First, in the loop that matters. Shared buckets are not the cost; comparisons are, and the whole query volume is Σ C(k, 2) over the bucket occupancies — computable exactly, with no timer: 

  inner-loop volume on the lattice, by hash:
    xor       99852 pair tests
    mix       90514 pair tests

A 25.7% excess in shared buckets is a 9.4% excess in comparisons. Second, the hash itself: three multiplies and two exclusive-ors against a rotate-and-finalise mix. 

  one hash: xor 0.115 ns, mix 0.231 ns (+100%)
  the same xor behind a call   0.692 ns (6.0x)

The better-spreading hash costs twice as much per call, and it is called once per entry — tens of thousands of times a frame — to save 9.4% of a loop that is a fraction of a stage that is 18% of collision detection. The textbook hash stays, and now it stays for a reason rather than by inheritance. Swapping it and measuring end to end is Exercise 5.

That third line is a separate lesson, and it changed the code. The first version of this comparison timed the engine's hash at 0.66 ns and the mix at 0.23 and concluded the mix was three times faster — which is absurd, since it does strictly more work. The difference was that hash_cell lived in broadphase.cpp, so the harness paid a function call for it while the mix was inlined in its own translation unit. Six times the cost, for three multiplies. cell_of and hash_cell now live in the header — inside build it never mattered, because that is the same translation unit, but these two are the engine's introspection hooks into the grid, so everybody else is exactly who calls them.

A comparison between two things has to be a comparison between two things

An A/B measurement where one arm crosses a translation-unit boundary and the other does not is not measuring what it says it is measuring. 4.8 §3 paid for the same lesson in a different currency — a harness that built its own render target and therefore tested a configuration the program never ran. Ask what else differs between the arms before believing the difference you were looking for.

8.3 The load factor

One more number the table decides for you: how many buckets per entry. build sizes the table at twice the entry count, rounded up to a power of two so the reduction is & (n − 1) rather than a division. Why two? 

  load factor (entries per bucket) on the lattice:
    ratio   buckets   shared   ideal
    1:1        2048      346     323
    1:2        4096      254     202
    1:4        8192      176     113
    1:8       16384      156      60

The second doubling buys 31% fewer shared buckets for twice the memory; the third buys 11% for twice again. Two is where the return per byte stops being worth a colder table.

And look at the ideal column, which is the same balls-in-bins prediction. The gap between the xor hash and chance widens as the table grows — 7%, 26%, 56%, 160%. A bigger table cannot fix a hash that is not spreading its low bits, and buying one is how people end up concluding that hashing is expensive.

9. The margin

gjk.hpp promised, three lessons ago, that this one would want a margin. Here it is, and it has two uses that are worth keeping apart.

Speculation. A pair a micron short of touching produces no manifold at all — 8.7's manifold_status::none. A solver that wants to stop a collision before it happens, rather than after, has to be told about pairs that have not happened yet, and the broadphase is where that decision is cheapest to make. 8.9 owns the question of how much speculation is right; this lesson provides the knob.

Hysteresis. A proxy fattened by more than one frame's motion need not be reinserted on a frame in which its body barely moved, which is the door to an incremental broadphase. This grid rebuilds from scratch every frame and does not use the margin that way — §11 says why.

What does it cost? The naive guess is “the ratio of the volumes”, and the right answer is a little larger and comes from the geometry rather than from the boxes.

Two boxes A and B overlap exactly when the difference of their centres lies inside a box of extent e_a + e_b — their Minkowski sum, which is the same object 8.5 and 8.6 spent two lessons inside, seen from the other direction. Fattening each box by m adds 2m to its own extent, so it adds 4m to the sum, and in a dilute scene the number of pairs follows the volume of the capture region:

\[ \frac{P(m)}{P(0)} \;\approx\; \left(\frac{E + 4m}{E}\right)^{3}, \qquad E = e_a + e_b \]

— the pair count scales as the cube of the fattened summed extent over the unfattened one.

Work one through. The harness's scene has a mean summed extent of 2.912 m. At a 25 cm margin the prediction is (2.912 + 1.0)/2.912 = 1.3434, cubed, = 2.425. Measured: 1,918 pairs against 833, a ratio of 2.303. Within 5%, and the whole sweep is within 12% at every margin from a centimetre to half a metre: 

  margin    pairs   ratio  predicted  contains
       m                              margin 0
    0.00      833   1.000      1.000  yes
    0.01      879   1.055      1.042  yes
    0.05     1005   1.206      1.221  yes
    0.10     1186   1.424      1.471  yes
    0.25     1918   2.303      2.425  yes
    0.50     3770   4.526      4.800  yes

Read the last column too, because it is the one 8.9 will rely on. At every margin the reported set contains the unfattened set: a margin never loses a pair, which is the conservative direction and the reason a margin is safe to add and unsafe to remove.

And read the second column as a warning. Half a metre of margin on 1.5 m crates costs 4.5× the pairs, every one of which becomes a narrow-phase call. A margin is not free insurance; it is 432 ns of insurance per extra pair, and the cube means its price climbs faster than the intuition that sets it.

10. The teapot in the stadium

Every spatial structure has an input it is bad at, and a uniform grid's is not exotic. It is the floor.

The name comes from ray tracing, where the classic failure of a uniform grid is a scene containing a detailed teapot and an enormous stadium: choose a cell that suits the teapot and the stadium claims millions of them, choose one that suits the stadium and the teapot is entirely inside a single cell. A physics scene has exactly the same shape, with a ground plate playing the stadium.

A 200 × 0.2 × 200 metre plate at a one-metre cell occupies 201 × 2 × 201 = 80,802 cells. Two thousand crates in the same scene occupy 30,225 entries between them. So one proxy — the floor — wants nearly three times as many insertions as the entire rest of the scene, and it is alone in almost every one of them, because the crates are piled in one region and the plate covers everything. 

        scene            entries   pairs    ms   big
  crates only             30225    1090  0.316     0
  + plate, guard off     111027    1127  1.150     0
  + plate, guard on       30225    1127  0.324     1

Adding one object multiplies the work by 3.7, and the cliff is cubic in the reciprocal of the cell size: 

  cells the 200 x 0.2 x 200 m plate wants:
    cell  4.00 m                              5202
    cell  2.00 m                             20402
    cell  1.00 m                             80802
    cell  0.50 m                            321602
    cell  0.25 m                           1283202

A uniform grid has no structural answer to this. That is not a shortcoming of this implementation; it is the definition of a uniform grid, which is that every cell is the same size, and an object far larger than the cell will always claim an enormous number of them. The structural answers are hierarchical — a BVH, an octree, a two-level grid — and this engine is not going to build one in this lesson.

10.1 A guard is a diagnosis, not a cure

What the grid can do is notice. Past max_cells_per_proxy, a proxy stops being gridded at all and goes on an oversized list, which is tested against everything by brute force.

Read that carefully, because it is the contract again. The oversized proxy is not dropped, not clamped, not approximated — it is tested against every other proxy, which is the most conservative thing available. The pair sets with the guard on and off are compared exactly, and they are equal: the guard changes the cost and not the answer.

The cost is linear in (number of oversized proxies) × n, which is fine for the one floor and one terrain chunk a real scene has, and is a disaster if half the scene trips it: 

  the guard's own limit: k big proxies, 2000 crates
      k    oversized pairs      ms
      1            1       37   0.256
      4            4      670   0.242
     16           16     4207   0.319
     64           64     6647   0.421
    256          256     6647   0.791

At k = 1 the guard is free. At k = 256 it is most of the broadphase, and the last two rows find no additional pairs at all for the extra cost. What that table is really saying is: when this number gets large, the grid is telling you to use a tree. A guard that turns a catastrophe into a linear cost is worth having precisely because it converts an invisible performance cliff into a number you can watch — broadphase_stats::oversized is in the demo's panel for that reason.

11. The budget, and the crossover

Two measurements to finish, and the first is the uncomfortable one.

An AABB overlap test is six comparisons on data already in registers. A grid build touches every proxy twice, chases a hash table, writes an entry array and walks it again. So there is a scene size below which the quadratic wins, and it is not as small as you would guess: 

      n     brute      grid   winner
      8     0.00 us     0.21 us   brute
     16     0.04 us     0.50 us   brute
     24     0.12 us     0.62 us   brute
     32     0.29 us     0.96 us   brute
     48     0.71 us     1.42 us   brute
     64     1.17 us     2.08 us   brute
     96     3.29 us     2.71 us   grid
    128     5.00 us     4.00 us   grid
    192    17.83 us     5.58 us   grid
    256    36.54 us     9.08 us   grid

Around a hundred bodies. Below that, shipping this whole stage is a net loss — slower, more code, more state, and one more thing that can be wrong. The crossover lands on 96 or 128 depending on the run, because the two are within twenty per cent of each other either side of it; read it as “about a hundred” rather than as a threshold.

This is worth knowing because almost nobody measures it. A broadphase is the kind of thing that gets added early, on principle, to a project that will never have two hundred dynamic bodies — and it makes that project slower while looking like diligence.

Above the crossover the two curves separate and never come back. From §2's table: 17.4× at 512 bodies, 61.4× at 2,048, 137.7× at 8,192, and every one of those measurements comes with the two pair sets checked equal.

11.1 The frame

  2000 proxies, 983 candidate pairs
    broadphase                             0.091 ms  (17.7%)
    narrow phase on what it found          0.426 ms  (82.3%)
    narrow phase on ALL pairs, implied     843.6 ms

  allocations over 2000 rebuilds               0

Half a millisecond for collision detection on two thousand bodies, of which the broadphase is under a fifth. The implied 844 ms is what the same narrow phase would cost on all 1,999,000 pairs — fifty frames of budget, for one frame.

And the last line is a claim about a structure that is rebuilt from scratch sixty times a second. Every vector inside uniform_grid is clear()ed rather than reconstructed, and clear on a vector of trivially destructible elements keeps the capacity — so after the first few frames the grid never asks the allocator for anything. That is checked with a counting operator new rather than asserted, because an allocation per frame is exactly the kind of cost that hides from a timing measurement: the allocator is fast when it is warm, and the pause arrives later, in somebody else's profile.

12. Writing it: four linear passes

Everything above is decided. What remains is the loop, and it is shorter than the argument.

12.1 What goes in

Six floats and an index:

Not a shape, not a body, not a pointer to either — and that is deliberate rather than minimal. The stage whose entire value is being cheap is the stage that must not be able to look inside anything. Give it a rigid_body* and within two lessons somebody will dereference it, and the broadphase will have a dependency on the solver.

index is whatever the caller uses to name a body — an ECS entity index, a slot in an array, the index half of a 5.4 handle. The grid never dereferences it. It copies it into a pair and compares two of them for order.

a < b is a contract, not tidiness

8.7's pair_key(a, b) is order-independent; its manifold is not. Generate a manifold for (7, 3) on one frame and (3, 7) on the next and the normal reverses, the reference face moves to the other shape, and every warm start on that pair is lost — a stack that mysteriously will not settle, three lessons downstream from the line that caused it. Sorting the pair here, once, at the only place pairs are made, is the cheapest possible place to fix that.

12.2 Pass 1: classify

Fatten each box by the margin, compute its cell range, and count the cells it wants. A proxy past max_cells_per_proxy goes on the oversized list — and still goes into boxes_, because the oversized list is tested against every proxy including the other oversized ones, and dropping it here would be the one thing the contract forbids.

12.3 Passes 2 and 3: a counting sort

Here is the piece worth reading twice, because the obvious data structure is wrong.

The obvious structure is a vector per bucket — a hash map from cell to a list of proxies. It allocates once per occupied cell, scatters the grid across the heap, and makes the pair loop chase a pointer per cell.

Instead: walk every proxy's cell range once and only count how many entries land in each bucket; prefix-sum the counts into offsets; then walk the ranges a second time and scatter each entry into its slot. That is a counting sort, and what comes out is one contiguous array in which every bucket's members are adjacent, with starts_[b] and starts_[b+1] bracketing bucket b.

Two cheap passes over contiguous memory beat one pass that allocates, every time — and the repeated triple loop that looks wasteful is the cheapest part of either.

12.4 Pass 4: the pairs

For each bucket, every pair of entries inside it, filtered by three tests in an order chosen by cost:

  1. Same cell? Two entries in one bucket may be from unrelated cells that happened to hash together. Rejecting is safe: if the two proxies really do share a cell, that cell's own bucket will produce them.
  2. Is this the owner cell? §6's rule. Three max calls, and the duplicates are gone.
  3. Do the boxes actually overlap? Sharing a cell means being within a cell of each other, which is not the same thing. Six comparisons to avoid a 432 ns narrow-phase call.

Each rejection is counted separately in broadphase_stats, which is what lets the demo show you why a candidate died rather than only how many did.

12.5 Why rebuilt, and not updated

An incremental grid — moving only the proxies that changed cell — sounds strictly better and is not. It needs a per-proxy record of which cells it is in; removal is the expensive operation in every open-addressing scheme; and in a physics scene most proxies move most frames anyway. A rebuild is two linear passes over contiguous memory with no removals, no tombstones and no pointer chasing, and it is O(n) rather than O(n log n).

It is the same argument 8.7's manifold_cache makes one lesson earlier, in the same words: a structure that is thrown away every frame never rots.

13. What this does not do

Five things, named rather than discovered.

It does not handle the teapot. §10's guard turns a cubic explosion into a linear cost and reports the count; it does not make a uniform grid good at non-uniform scenes, because nothing can. The answer is a hierarchy, and Module 9's optimisation lessons are where a two-level grid or a BVH would go.

It does not exploit frame coherence. Everything is rebuilt. Sweep and prune exists precisely because a sorted list barely changes between frames, and there is a real measurement to be made about which wins on a scene that is mostly asleep. 8.10 adds sleeping, at which point that measurement becomes worth taking.

It does not distinguish static from dynamic. Every real engine does: static geometry goes into a structure built once, dynamic bodies into one rebuilt per frame, and the static–static pairs are never generated at all. This grid generates them and then rejects them on the AABB test, which is correct and wasteful. Exercise 4.

It does not filter by layer. A real scene wants bullets not to collide with bullets and triggers not to collide with each other, and a broadphase is the cheapest place to enforce that — a bitmask per proxy, tested before the AABB. It is genuinely two lines, and it is not here because 8.9's solver has not yet defined what a layer means.

And it does not consume its own margin. margin exists, is measured, and defaults to zero, because the decision about how far ahead to speculate belongs to the solver that will act on it. That is 8.9.

16. Common pitfalls & debugging

Nine, in the order you are likely to meet them. A broadphase bug is unusually nasty because the symptom appears in a different subsystem, several frames later, intermittently — so each of these is written symptom first.

Things fall through the floor, but only sometimes

Cause: a false negative. Almost always one of two things: the proxy was inserted by its centre cell rather than its whole range (§5.1, and it loses two thirds of the pairs), or the world AABB handed to the broadphase is stale — computed before the integrator moved the body rather than after. Fix: run brute_force_pairs beside the grid and diff the sets, which is what the demo's [B] does. If they agree, the bug is in the boxes, not the grid.

The pair count is right but the stack will not settle

Cause: the pair order is not canonical. 8.7's manifold is not symmetric in its two arguments, so a pair reported as (3, 7) one frame and (7, 3) the next has its normal reversed and its reference face moved, and every warm start on it is lost. Fix: sort at the point of emission, as emit does. The tell is that raising the solver's iteration count helps a little and never fixes it.

Every pair arrives two, four or eight times

Cause: the owner-cell rule is missing or wrong. Fix: §6. The count of duplicates is predictable — the product of the overlap widths minus one, summed over pairs — so if you are seeing a number other than zero, compute the expected one and see whether they match; if they do, the rule is absent, and if they do not, the rule is applied in the wrong place (it must be tested against the current cell, not against either proxy's own minimum).

A frame occasionally takes eighty milliseconds

Cause: either the grid is allocating (a vector per bucket, or starts_ reconstructed rather than assigned), or one proxy has become enormous — a body that got a NaN in its transform has an AABB spanning the universe, and at a 1 m cell that is an infinite loop in the range walk rather than a slow frame. Fix: watch stats().entries, which is the number that spikes, and keep max_cells_per_proxy on. The guard's real job is turning that particular hang into a slow frame with a counter attached.

The grid is slower than testing every pair

Cause: the scene is small (§11 — below about a hundred bodies this is expected and correct), or the cell size is wrong by a decade. Fix: read entries and bucket_tests. If entries is many times the proxy count the cell is too small; if bucket_tests is approaching n(n−1)/2 the cell is too large and you have rebuilt brute force with a hash table in front of it.

It works in the first octant and breaks near the origin

Cause: not the truncating cell index, which §5.2 measured at zero lost pairs. It is almost always a cell coordinate used as an array index without an offset — a negative index into a vector, which is undefined behaviour and usually a crash or silent corruption. Fix: hash the coordinate rather than indexing on it, which is what hash_cell is for, and note the cast to uint32_t inside it: multiplying a negative int32 by a large prime is signed overflow and therefore undefined.

Hashing looks expensive in the profile

Cause: quite possibly a function call. §8.2 measured hash_cell at 0.115 ns inlined and 0.692 ns behind a call the compiler could not inline — six times, for three multiplies. Fix: put trivially small functions that cross a translation-unit boundary in the header. And before concluding your hash is slow, check the other explanation: a hash that is not spreading produces long buckets, and what is expensive is the quadratic loop inside them.

The A/B comparison says the more complicated arm is faster

Cause: the two arms differ in something other than the thing under test. In §8.2's first draft one arm crossed a translation-unit boundary and the other did not. Fix: ask what else differs before believing the difference you were looking for. A result that contradicts the instruction count is the instrument talking, not the code.

Changing the cell size changes the physics

Cause: a real bug, and a serious one — the cell size is a performance decision and must not be observable in the output. Fix: the check is one line (§7.3: sweep the cell size and compare pair sets), and the usual culprit is a pair emitted from a cell test rather than from a box test, so that “within a cell of each other” has leaked out as “touching”.

17. Exercises

Exercise 1 — the crossover on your machine

§11's crossover is a property of this machine and this compiler. Run the harness and find yours, then change one thing — build without -O2, or make the proxies ten times larger so each spans more cells — and find it again. Hint: the cost model of §7 predicts which direction each change moves it, and predicting before measuring is the point of the exercise.

Exercise 2 — the sweep-and-prune comparison

Implement a one-axis sweep and prune: sort the proxies by box.min.x, walk the sorted list, and for each proxy test it against the following ones until one starts beyond its box.max.x. Check it against brute_force_pairs, then measure it against the grid on three scenes — the scattered one, the lattice, and one where every body is on a floor at y = 0.5. Solution sketch: sweep and prune wins on scenes that are long in the sweep axis and loses badly when many bodies share a coordinate on it; the floor scene should be its worst case if you sweep on y, and you should try that on purpose.

Exercise 3 — the cost model as a tuner

Write the model from §7.1 into the engine: sweep three or four cell sizes on the first frame of a level, read entries and bucket_tests from each, solve for a and b once at start-up, and pick the cell size that minimises the model. Compare against auto_cell_size. Hint: the two constants are machine properties, so they only need finding once per platform — which makes this a build-time question rather than a runtime one, and that may be the better answer.

Exercise 4 — static and dynamic

Add a bool is_static to proxy and skip pairs where both are static — they can never move relative to each other, so they will either always collide or never. Measure on a scene that is 90% static, which is what a level is. Solution sketch: the saving is not the pair tests but the entries: a static proxy's cell range never changes, so the real prize is a second grid that is built once and queried rather than rebuilt. Doing that properly means the query walks a dynamic proxy's cells against the static table, which is a different loop from the one in build.

Exercise 5 — swap the hash, end to end

§8.2 measured the textbook hash as 25.7% above chance on a lattice and worth 9.4% of the inner loop. Replace hash_cell with the mixing function from the harness and measure the whole broadphase, not the hash — on the lattice, on the scattered scene, and on the crowd. Hint: predict the answer first from the two numbers in §8.2, then see whether the prediction survives; a 9.4% saving on the inner loop is not a 9.4% saving on the stage, because the entry pass does not get faster and the hash itself gets slower.

Exercise 6 — the layer mask

Add a std::uint32_t layer and a std::uint32_t mask to proxy, and reject a candidate when (a.layer & b.mask) == 0 or the reverse. Put the test where it belongs in the three-test order of §12.4 and justify the position you chose. Solution sketch: it is cheaper than the AABB test and more selective than the owner rule, but it must come after the owner test, or a pair rejected by the mask in its owner cell would still be emitted from another one — which is a duplicate that only appears when layers are in use.

Exercise 7 — an engine challenge: the two-level grid

§10 says the structural answer to the teapot is a hierarchy. Build the smallest one that works: a coarse grid for proxies that trip max_cells_per_proxy and the existing fine grid for everything else, with pairs generated within each level and across the two. Keep the contract — check against brute_force_pairs at every step — and measure against §10's guard on the k = 64 scene. Hint: the cross-level pass is the interesting one, and the honest version of this exercise ends with a measurement of whether it was worth it, because for one floor it will not be.

18. Recap & next

The engine can now find, in a scene of thousands of bodies, the handful of pairs worth asking the narrow phase about — and it can prove that it has not missed any.

What was built: proxy and broadphase_pair, a uniform_grid that hashes cell coordinates and rebuilds itself every frame in four linear passes, brute_force_pairs as both oracle and baseline, overlaps(aabb, aabb) as the six-comparison test that makes the whole arrangement pay, and a stats struct that reports where the frame went.

What was measured, and what it changed:

  • The quadratic wall is at 278 bodies, not at some large number.
  • The grid is 137.7× faster at 8,192 proxies and slower below about a hundred — and both halves of that sentence are worth knowing.
  • The famous floor-versus-cast bug loses zero pairs in a range-walk grid, because monotonicity is all the range walk needs. The mistake that does lose pairs — one cell per proxy, by its centre — loses 66.7% of them.
  • The owner-cell rule removes every duplicate exactly, for three max calls, and the closed form for how many it removed agrees with the counter at 3271 and 3271.
  • The cell-size rule of thumb is a two-term cost model whose constants (8.1 and 5.4 ns) are the same on scenes of different density, and whose minimum moves as the sixth root of the crowd.
  • The textbook spatial hash is 25.7% worse than chance on the input a physics scene actually produces, worth 9.4% of the inner loop, and half the price of the hash that fixes it — so it stays, for a reason.
  • A ground plate wants 80,802 cells to itself at a 1 m cell, nearly three times the whole scene, and a uniform grid can only notice.

And the promise this lesson makes to the two after it: pairs arrive with a < b, always, so pair_key indexes 8.7's manifold_cache and collide_manifold is called with the same shape as a every frame. Warm starting depends on it, and 8.10 is where that debt is collected.

Next: 8.9 — Impulse Response: Restitution and Friction. The pipeline is now complete up to the point of doing something about it. A pair is found, a manifold is generated, four contact points share a normal — and nothing has yet applied a single impulse. 8.9 derives the normal impulse from the relative velocity you want afterwards rather than from a force, adds restitution as a coefficient on that velocity, and then Coulomb friction as a tangential impulse clipped to a cone. It also decides the question this lesson deliberately left open: how much speculative margin a solver wants, which is the moment broadphase_config::margin stops being a knob nobody turns.

19. Further reading

  • Matthias Teschner, Bruno Heidelberger, Matthias Müller, Danat Pomeranets and Markus Gross, “Optimized Spatial Hashing for Collision Detection of Deformable Objects” (VMV 2003) — the source of the three-prime hash in hash_cell, and worth reading for its cell-size analysis, which reaches the same “about the object size” conclusion by a different route.
  • Christer Ericson, Real-Time Collision Detection, chapter 7 — the standard treatment of broadphase structures: uniform grids, hierarchical grids, sweep and prune, and the trade-offs between them. Section 7.1.6 covers the duplicate problem and arrives at a different fix from ours, which is worth comparing against §6.
  • David Baraff, “Dynamic Simulation of Non-Penetrating Rigid Bodies” (thesis, 1992), chapter 2 — where sweep and prune enters the literature, and the coherence argument that this lesson's grid deliberately does not make.
  • Erin Catto, “Dynamic Bounding Volume Hierarchies” (GDC 2019) — the structure this grid is not, explained by the author of Box2D, including why a physics engine's BVH wants fattened AABBs and what that has to do with §9's margin.
  • Randall Munroe's teapot, and A. S. Glassner, An Introduction to Ray Tracing — the “teapot in a stadium” problem is a ray-tracing idiom before it is a physics one, and reading the original framing makes clear that it is a property of uniform subdivision rather than of any particular application.
  • Gino van den Bergen, Collision Detection in Interactive 3D Environments, chapter 6 — broadphase from the author of GJK's most-cited implementation, with a careful treatment of why AABBs and not spheres, and when that flips.
