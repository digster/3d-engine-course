Module 8 — Physics · Lesson 8.4

Collision Primitives: Spheres, AABBs, OBBs, and the SAT

Eighty-six lessons in, nothing in this engine has a size the simulation knows about. A body has a mass, an inertia tensor, a position and an orientation, and every one of those describes how it moves; drop two of 8.2's crates on the same spot and they arrive at the same spot and stay there, sharing one cubic metre of the world forever. This lesson gives them shapes, and then answers the one question every physics engine is organised around: are these two things touching? The answer is one genuinely beautiful idea. Hold two objects up to a lamp: if their shadows are apart, the objects are apart, and that half needs no argument at all. The other half — that if they really are apart then some lamp position shows you the gap — is the Separating Axis Theorem, it is true only for convex shapes, and it turns an infinite search over directions into fifteen candidates for two boxes. We derive where the fifteen come from, and then build the configuration that proves six of them are not enough: two planks crossed in mid-air with 5 cm of daylight between them, where all six face normals report an overlap of up to 3.13 metres and the only direction that can see the gap is the cross product of one edge from each. On random pairs that case is 4.6% of everything six axes call a collision — and when boxes genuinely do overlap, the shallowest way out is an edge-edge axis 49.8% of the time. Then the things nobody tells you. The minimum translation vector is the shallowest escape, which stops being the right escape the moment an object is half buried: two cubes 10 cm out of alignment are corrected by 1.9 metres. The clamp that finds the closest point on a box collapses 27 cases to three lines — and returns a zero normal on the 8,829 of 100,000 spheres whose centre is inside, which is exactly what a sphere looks like the frame after it tunnels through a wall. Two crates standing on the same floor have parallel up axes at every yaw, so one of the nine cross products is degenerate in the most common arrangement a game ever produces — and the folklore about guarding it turns out to be true of a formulation we are not using: with the guard removed our version produces zero false separations, because an axis made of pure rounding error is still a direction and no direction separates overlapping bodies. The formulation in the books fails 188 times in 400 on the same fixture. And a 1 mm gap measured a kilometre from the origin is wrong by 2.3%, at 10 km is unresolvable, and at 100 km reports exactly zero — the algorithm unchanged, the boxes unchanged, only where they are. Every separated pair in this lesson is checked by re-proving its own certificate in double precision rather than by trusting a second implementation.

1. The problem: an engine in which nothing has a size

Here is a program you can write today with everything Module 8 has built. Make two crates. Give them mass, give them inertia tensors, give them gravity. Drop them from the same height, a metre apart in x, and give the second one a sideways nudge so that it drifts into the first.

They pass through each other.

Not dramatically — there is no glitch, no jitter, nothing that looks like a bug. They occupy the same volume for a while and then continue on their separate parabolas, exactly as Newton says two points should. The simulation is doing precisely what it was told. It has simply never been told that a crate is a metre wide.

The gap is visible in a function signature we have been using for three lessons: 

rigid_body make_box(vec3 position, float mass, vec3 half_extents);

That takes half extents, uses them to build an inertia tensor, and throws them away. The body it returns knows exactly how hard it is to spin a 0.5 × 1.0 × 1.5 metre box, and has no idea that it is 0.5 × 1.0 × 1.5 metres. Everything the simulation knows about a body — inv_mass, inv_inertia_local, state.position, orientation, angular_velocity — answers the question how does this thing respond to being pushed? Nothing answers where is its surface?

So this lesson is in two halves, and the second one is much larger than the first. The small half is a type: a shape, which is geometry, deliberately separate from the body, which is dynamics. The large half is the query — are these two touching? — which turns out to rest on one of the most satisfying arguments in computational geometry, and on a pile of consequences that nobody warns you about.

A shape is not a mesh, and the two are separate assets in every engine that ships. A barrel drawn with 4,000 triangles collides as a cylinder, because a cylinder is six floats and a convex primitive and 4,000 triangles are neither. This is not a corner being cut: a player cannot see the difference, and the solver in 8.10 can. The visual model and the collision model diverge on purpose, and the moment you accept that, the whole of collision detection becomes a question about a small number of simple shapes rather than about arbitrary geometry.

2. Building intuition: hold it up to the light

Before any formalism, an experiment you can perform with two objects on your desk.

Pick them up, hold them in front of a lamp, and look at the shadows on the wall. Sometimes the two shadows are separate — a strip of light between them. Sometimes they touch or overlap. Move around the room, changing where the light comes from, and the shadows change with you.

Now the observation that starts everything:

If the shadows are apart, the objects are apart.

That is not an approximation and it is not a heuristic — it is a proof, and a one-line one. If some point belonged to both objects, it would cast a point belonging to both shadows, and the shadows would meet. They do not meet. So no such point exists. Done, with no arithmetic and no conditions on the shapes: it works for a teapot and a bicycle as well as it works for two boxes.

A single lamp position that shows a gap is therefore a complete and checkable proof that two objects do not touch. Hold on to the word "checkable", because it becomes this lesson's testing strategy in §7 and its verification strategy throughout.

2.1 From a lamp to a direction to a number

Strip the physical picture down. The lamp's position is really just a direction: what matters is which way the light travels, not where the bulb is. And a shadow on a wall perpendicular to that direction is exactly what you get by taking every point of the object and recording how far along the direction it sits.

"How far along a direction" is a dot product — Lesson 1.6 built it as precisely this, the length of a vector's shadow on another. So for a unit direction L, an object's shadow is the set of numbers { p · L : p in the object }, and for any shape that is not full of holes that set is an interval: a lowest value and a highest one, with everything in between.

Two objects, one direction, two intervals. And two intervals on a line either overlap or they do not, which is a comparison a nine-year-old can do:

overlap = min(a.hi, b.hi) − max(a.lo, b.lo), and they are apart exactly when that is negative.

Figure 1's left panel is that, for two convex shapes and a direction that happens to see the gap. Its middle panel is the same two shapes under a different lamp, where the shadows overlap and the test tells you nothing — not that they are touching, only that this particular direction has failed to prove otherwise.

That asymmetry is the whole subject. A gap is evidence; an overlap is the absence of evidence. Which raises the question the rest of the lesson is about: how many directions do you have to try before the absence of evidence becomes evidence of absence?

3. The theorem, and the word “convex”

The answer, for the shapes we care about, is one of those results that sounds too good to be true the first time you read it.

The Separating Axis Theorem. Two convex sets are disjoint if and only if there is some direction L on which their projections do not overlap.

The "if" half is the lamp argument from §2, and we have already proved it. The "only if" half is the one doing real work, and it says: if they really are apart, a direction that sees the gap exists. A search that tries every direction and finds nothing is therefore not an inconclusive search — it is a proof of contact.

3.1 Why convexity is not decoration

A set is convex when the straight line between any two of its points stays inside it. A ball is convex. A box is convex. A banana is not; neither is a cup, a doughnut, a chair, or the letter C.

The sketch of the "only if" half runs like this. Take two disjoint convex sets and find the closest pair of points, one in each — call them p and q. Since the sets are apart, p ≠ q. Now take L to be the direction from p to q, and consider the plane through p perpendicular to L. Convexity is what guarantees that the whole of the first set stays on one side of that plane: if any point of it were on the far side, the segment from p to that point would be inside the set (that is what convex means) and would contain points closer to q than p is — contradicting that p and q were the closest pair. The same argument pins the second set on the other side. So the two projections onto L are separated by the distance |pq|, and L is a separating axis.

That is the ninety-percent picture. The missing ten percent is that "take the closest pair" needs the sets to be closed and at least one of them bounded for the minimum to be attained, and the general statement is the hyperplane separation theorem, proved for arbitrary convex sets without ever mentioning distance. Boyd and Vandenberghe's Convex Optimization §2.5 does it properly. Nothing in this engine needs the general version: every shape we collide is closed, bounded and convex by construction.

Figure 1's right-hand panel is the counterexample, and it is worth staring at. A ball resting in the mouth of a cup, touching nothing. They are genuinely apart — you could slide a sheet of paper between them everywhere — and yet on every direction their shadows overlap, because the cup's shadow is solid while the cup is not. The gap between them is not between the shapes in any direction; it is tucked inside the concavity of one of them, and a projection cannot see inside a shape.

This is why every collision system you will ever meet is built on convex pieces. Concave geometry is handled by decomposing it — a level's collision mesh is a list of convex hulls, a character is a handful of capsules, a chair is four boxes and a slab. The decomposition is an offline problem and often a hard one; the runtime problem is always convex-against-convex.

3.2 An infinite search, made finite

There is still something impossible about the theorem as stated. It says a separating direction exists; it does not say how to find one, and there are infinitely many directions to try.

The bridge is a change of view that we will use twice more in this module. Consider the set of all differences a − b with a in the first shape and b in the second. That set is called the Minkowski difference, it is itself convex, and it contains the origin exactly when some a equals some b — that is, exactly when the shapes overlap. So "do these two shapes touch?" is the same question as "does this one shape contain the origin?", and a separating axis is a direction in which the whole Minkowski difference sits to one side of zero.

For polytopes — boxes included — the Minkowski difference is itself a polytope, with finitely many faces. And a direction that separates a polytope from the origin can always be taken to be one of that polytope's face normals: if a face's plane does not have the origin on its outside, no direction near it does either. So the infinite search collapses to a finite list, and the only question left is where the faces of A ⊖ B come from. §7 answers that, and the answer is fifteen.

Lesson 8.5 takes the same picture in the other direction: instead of enumerating the faces of the Minkowski difference, GJK searches it, using nothing but a support function, and gets the actual distance rather than just a yes or no. It is the same object seen twice, which is why the two lessons sit next to each other.

4. Three primitives, and what each of them knows

Before any tests, the shapes. We ship three, and they are not an arbitrary starter set — they are three points on one curve, and the curve is how much does this shape know about which way round it is?

PrimitiveStorageKnows about rotationOverlap test
Sphere 4 floats Nothing, and does not need to One comparison
AABB 6 floats The world's axes only Three interval comparisons
OBB 6 floats + an orientation Its own axes, exactly The SAT, fifteen candidates

A sphere is rotation-invariant: turn it and it is the same sphere, so its test stays correct however wildly the body tumbles, and its bounding volume needs no rebuilding. It is the cheapest true answer in collision detection and the reason every broadphase in every engine starts with one.

An AABB knows the world's axes and nothing else. Its test is three comparisons, its storage is two corners, and it is what a uniform grid, a BVH and a frustum culler all actually index — because "which cells does this occupy?" is a question about world axes. Its weakness is precisely its strength: it cannot turn. Rotate the object inside it and the box has to be rebuilt, larger. Lesson 6.8 wrote that down when the type was born: transformed() returns the box around the transformed box, which under a 45° rotation is up to √2 wider and under a general one up to √3.

An OBB is an AABB that brought its own axes. It is exactly as tight as the object under any rotation, and the price is that its overlap test is no longer three comparisons. That price is §7.

4.1 The type already existed, in the wrong folder

We do not need to write an AABB. engine::aabb has existed since Lesson 6.8, where a directional light's shadow frustum needed something to be fitted to, and 6.16 taught it to be culled against. It has an inside-out empty representation, an expand that needs no first-point special case, eight corners in a fixed order, and a bounding sphere conversion.

It lives at engine/include/engine/gfx/bounds.hpp, and physics wanting it exposes that as a mistake. Open the file: it includes math/vec3.hpp and math/mat4.hpp and nothing else. Every line of it is geometry. It sits under gfx/ because 6.8 is who needed it first, which is how most headers end up where they are.

That was free until now. Today phys/ wants the same type, and #include <engine/gfx/bounds.hpp> from a physics header would be the engine's first dependency from simulation on rendering — backwards, and entrenched two lessons later by 8.6's broadphase and 8.7's manifold cache. Check it for yourself: every #include in engine/phys/ today names math/ or core/, and that is a property worth keeping.

The alternative is a second aabb in phys/, which is exactly the duplication this file was created to end — 6.8 promoted it into a type because the same struct had been hand-written three times. So the file moves to engine/include/engine/math/bounds.hpp: six include lines and an include guard, once, against a layering inversion that would have been quoted as precedent forever. Nothing about the type changes, and the umbrella header's lint catches the move immediately if you forget a caller.

This is what §8's “physical design” means in practice, and it is why Module 5's refactor was taught as architecture rather than chores. The question is never “where would this file be nice?” — it is “which direction do I want the arrows to point?”. Here the arrows say simulation may depend on maths, and rendering may depend on maths, and neither of them may depend on the other. A header in the wrong folder is not a tidiness problem; it is a dependency waiting to be created by the next person who needs it.

4.2 A shape knows what it weighs

shape.hpp's one dependency on the rest of physics is a function: 

[[nodiscard]] mat3 inertia_of(const shape& s, float mass);

which dispatches to 8.3's closed forms. The direction matters and must keep mattering: inertia.hpp was written knowing nothing about collision geometry and must go on knowing nothing about it, because plenty of bodies get their tensor from an artist's number or a compound assembly rather than from a primitive.

What it buys is that make_box's two arguments stop being two independent facts a caller has to keep in agreement. Before it, a crate's tensor and a crate's collider were set separately — and setting one without the other gives you something that collides like a crate and spins like something else, a bug with no symptom at all until something lands on a corner.

4.3 Where the shape is not stored

You will notice that rigid_body gains no shape member this lesson, and that is a decision rather than an omission.

Collision detection is a query over geometry. Every function in collide.hpp takes shapes and placements and returns an answer, and none of them has any business knowing what the geometry weighs or which way it is moving. Keeping the arrow pointed that way is what lets 8.5's GJK take the support function it needs, 8.6's broadphase take the bounds it needs, and 8.7's manifold take the contact it needs, without any of them dragging the dynamics along.

The thing that pairs a shape with a body is a collider, and it arrives in 8.6, because a pair is only useful once something walks a list of them looking for candidates — and because a real collider also carries an offset from the centre of mass, a material, and a filter mask, none of which has a caller yet.

5. Two spheres, and the shape of an answer

Start with the easy case, because it settles a question that has nothing to do with spheres: what should a collision test return?

Two spheres, centres ca and cb, radii ra and rb. They touch exactly when the distance between the centres is at most the sum of the radii, which is one subtraction:

depth = (ra + rb) − |cb − ca|

Positive means overlapping by that much; negative means apart by that much. Our unit spheres 1.5 m apart give 2.0 − 1.5 = +0.500000, which the harness prints and which you can do in your head — the point of including it.

Now notice something. Project both spheres onto the x axis and you get the intervals [−1.0000, +1.0000] and [+0.5000, +2.5000]. Their overlap, min(hi) − max(lo), is +0.500000 — bit-identical to the distance formula's answer, which the harness checks rather than asserts. The sphere test is not a special case sitting beside the SAT. It is the SAT, on the one direction that can possibly matter for two spheres, and the algebra collapses because a sphere's projected radius is its radius in every direction.

5.1 Why the answer is not a bool

Every test in this file returns a separation: a direction, a signed distance along it, and which candidate produced it. That is a lot more than "are they touching", and each part earns its place somewhere downstream.

  • When they are apart, the axis is a certificate. Anyone handed the two shapes and the axis can check the claim in four dot products, without re-running the algorithm, and without trusting whoever produced it. This turns out to be the single most useful property in the lesson, and §10 builds the entire verification strategy on it.
  • When they overlap, the axis and depth are the fix. The shortest translation that ends the overlap — Lesson 8.7 turns it into a contact normal and 8.9 pushes along it. "They are touching" without a direction is a fact a solver can do nothing with.
  • The axis index is what makes caching possible. Two crates resting on each other separate — or fail to — on the same axis frame after frame, so 8.6 can try last frame's axis first and 8.7 can key a persistent manifold on it. That is temporal coherence, and it is worth more than any micro-optimisation in this lesson.

One field carries both cases, and that is deliberate: depth is positive for penetration and negative for a gap, with hit() derived from its sign rather than stored beside it. It is not a trick to save four bytes. A separating axis and a penetration axis are the same object measured in two directions, which is why the same fifteen candidates answer both questions and why the search does not change shape when two objects touch.

5.2 The degenerate case, and a made-up answer that is correct

Two spheres at exactly the same point have no direction between them. The subtraction gives a zero vector, and normalising it gives 0/0.

We return +y with the full depth. That is invented, and it is the right thing to do: every caller downstream will multiply by this vector and some will divide by its length, so a legal unit vector pointing somewhere keeps the whole chain finite, while a zero or a NaN poisons it silently three functions later. A made-up direction is the correct answer to a question that has no answer, provided it is made up in one place, on purpose, with a comment.

And the case is not hypothetical — two objects spawned at the same transform is the most common way a level designer produces it, and "the physics NaN'd on level load" is a genuinely awful afternoon.

5.3 Exactly touching, and a sign change you cannot straddle

The harness's control here is the pair at exactly 2.0 m: depth +0.0000000, and hit() reports yes, because the comparison is depth >= 0. Move them a tenth of a millimetre further and it is −0.0000999 and no.

That is a choice, not a fact — "touching" has to fall on one side or the other and we put it with "colliding" — and the reason to point at it is that the exact boundary is not decidable in floating point anyway. Two objects a nanometre apart will be reported either way depending on rounding, and the sensible engineering answer is not to try harder but to stop asking the question exactly: real engines test against a small positive margin, so contacts are created slightly before objects touch and the solver has a frame to react. That is speculative contact, and 8.6 builds it.

6. Projecting a box: the radius of its shadow

Everything past this point needs one formula, so let us derive it properly rather than quote it.

A box is a centre c, three unit axes u₀, u₁, u₂ and three half extents h₀, h₁, h₂. Every point of it can be written

p = c + s₀ h₀ u₀ + s₁ h₁ u₁ + s₂ h₂ u₂, with each sᵢ in [−1, 1]

— which is worth reading slowly, because it is the whole definition of a box and it is where the formula comes from. Walk from the centre, at most h₀ along the first axis, at most h₁ along the second, at most h₂ along the third. The corners are the eight points where every sᵢ is ±1.

Now project onto a unit direction L, which means dotting with it — and the dot product distributes over the sum:

p · L = c · L + s₀ h₀ (u₀ · L) + s₁ h₁ (u₁ · L) + s₂ h₂ (u₂ · L)

The first term is fixed: it is where the centre lands, the middle of the shadow. The other three are each free to range over ±hᵢ|uᵢ · L| and — here is the step that matters — they are independent. Choosing s₀ does not constrain s₁. So the largest value of the whole expression is obtained by choosing each sᵢ to match the sign of its own term, and the shadow's radius — half its width — is

r(L) = h₀|u₀ · L| + h₁|u₁ · L| + h₂|u₂ · L|

Three dot products, three absolute values, three multiply-adds. The box's shadow is [c·L − r, c·L + r], and that is the only fact about boxes this lesson needs.

6.1 A worked example, by hand

Take a box with half extents (2, 1, 0.5), turned 30° about z, and project it onto the world x axis. Its axes are then u₀ = (cos30, sin30, 0), u₁ = (−sin30, cos30, 0), u₂ = (0, 0, 1), so the three dot products with L = (1, 0, 0) are 0.866025, −0.500000 and 0.000000:

r = 2 × 0.866025 + 1 × 0.500000 + 0.5 × 0 = 1.732051 + 0.500000 = 2.232051

The harness prints exactly that, and prints bounds_of()'s half width for the same box as 2.232051 too — because computing an AABB around an OBB is this formula run three times, once per world axis, and nothing else. Figure 2 does the arithmetic on the page.

Two things are worth pulling out of that example. The second term contributes +0.5 even though u₁ · L is negative: the absolute value is doing its job, picking the corner on the helpful side. And the third term is zero because u₂ is perpendicular to L, not because the box is flat — turn it out of the page and that 0.5 starts contributing.

6.2 What the absolute values are worth, measured

Drop them and you get |Σ hᵢ (uᵢ · L)|, which is a perfectly sensible-looking expression and is the projection of one particular corner — the (+,+,+) one — rather than the radius of the shadow. It is right only for directions that happen to point at that corner.

The harness measures it over 20,000 random boxes: wrong on 15,089 of them, by up to 100%. Which is to say it is wrong three quarters of the time, and when it is wrong it can be wrong by everything. Meanwhile the correct formula, checked against the farthest of the eight corners over 100,000 random boxes and directions, disagrees by at most 7.1526e−07 m — float rounding, and nothing else.

The control that fails to be exact is the interesting one. Project a box onto its own axis and the answer must be that half extent exactly: the other two dot products are zero, so the other two terms vanish. Measured on a box with half extents (0.3, 1.7, 0.9), the middle axis comes back bit-exact and the other two do not — 0.3000001 against 0.3000000. Nothing is wrong. The axes came out of mat3_from_quat, so they are orthonormal only to float precision, and the two "zero" dot products are actually 10⁻⁸. It is a useful reminder before §11: in this arithmetic, exactly is a word you have to earn.

6.3 The support function, quietly introduced

The same argument, with "the largest value" replaced by "the point that achieves it", gives the support function: the farthest point of a shape in a given direction. For a box it is three sign tests — go to the + end of each axis when the direction leans that way and the − end when it leans the other. 

[[nodiscard]] vec3 support(const obb& box, vec3 d);
[[nodiscard]] vec3 support(const engine::sphere& s, vec3 d);

Nothing in this lesson needs it; the harness checks it lands on the farthest of the eight corners for 20,000 random directions and otherwise leaves it alone. It is here because Lesson 8.5's GJK is written entirely in terms of it and needs nothing else — no corners, no faces, no case analysis, no knowledge of what kind of shape it is holding. Adding it now, next to the derivation it comes from, is cheaper than introducing it cold next lesson.

7. Fifteen candidates, and why six is not enough

§3 reduced the infinite search to "the face normals of the Minkowski difference". So: where do those faces come from?

Slide one box around the outside of the other, keeping them touching. The set of positions its centre can take is the surface of A ⊖ B, and there are exactly two ways to be in contact while sliding freely:

  1. A face of one box is flat against something on the other. The face's plane is the contact plane, and its normal is the direction to test. A box has six faces but only three distinct normals: opposite faces differ only in sign, and L and −L cast the same shadow. Three from each box, six in total.
  2. An edge of one box crosses an edge of the other. Two skew lines touching at a point — think of two pencils laid across each other. The contact plane here contains both edge directions, so its normal is perpendicular to both, which is exactly their cross product. A box has twelve edges but only three distinct directions, for the same reason it has three distinct face normals. Three from each box gives 3 × 3 = nine pairs.

Six plus nine is fifteen. Figure 3 draws both cases and the arithmetic.

And that is the algorithm, complete:

For each of the fifteen candidate directions L: if |t · L| > rA(L) + rB(L), where t is the offset between centres, the boxes are apart and L proves it. If none of the fifteen says so, they overlap.

Nine multiply-adds per candidate, a comparison, and an early exit. It is hard to overstate how unreasonable it is that this works — we are answering a question about two infinite point sets by testing fifteen numbers.

7.1 The offset, and a subtraction that matters later

Notice the test is written with t = cB − cA rather than by comparing two absolute intervals. Algebraically it is the same; numerically it is not, and §11 is about the difference. Subtracting the centres first means the arithmetic that follows is on numbers the size of the boxes; comparing absolute intervals means it is on numbers the size of the world.

7.2 An AABB test is the same algorithm with twelve candidates deleted

Before the interesting case, the boring one — because it is not really a different algorithm.

Give both boxes the world's axes. Now A's three face normals and B's three face normals are the same three directions, so six candidates collapse to three. And every cross product between them is either zero (an axis with itself) or one of the same three axes again (x × y = z), so the nine edge-edge candidates contribute nothing new. Three candidates, each of which is a component read rather than a dot product.

That is a claim, so the harness checks it: 200,000 random AABB pairs put through the specialised collide(aabb, aabb) and through the general collide(obb, obb) with identity axes agree bit for bit on all 200,000 — same verdict, same depth, same axis, same index. Not "to within a tolerance". Identical.

The classic bug, and how often it fires. The AABB test is "the intervals overlap on every axis". Write "on some axis" — which is what you get by reaching for || when you meant &&, and which reads perfectly naturally as "do they overlap anywhere?" — and on those same 200,000 pairs it is wrong 149,779 times, 74.9%. It reports a collision between a crate and a box stacked three metres above it, because their shadows overlap in x and y. The reason to quote the number is that it is not a subtle bug and it does not behave like one: an engine with this mistake has everything colliding with everything, which looks like a broadphase problem rather than a comparison.

7.3 Where six axes fail, built rather than found

Now the case this lesson exists for.

The face normals are the obvious candidates, and if you reason "a box is bounded by its faces, so a gap must show up looking along a face normal" you will write six tests and ship. It is not a straw man — it is a natural argument and it is wrong, and the way to see that it is wrong is to construct a counterexample instead of hunting for one.

The construction is three lines, and it is the argument:

  1. Take two long thin planks, 4.0 × 0.3 × 0.3 m, the second one turned by a generic rotation — 0.7π about (1, 2, 3) normalised, which is 8.3 §6's rotation, chosen because nothing in it is parallel or perpendicular to anything by accident.
  2. Compute L = normalise(a₀ × b₀), the cross product of one long edge from each. This is the direction we intend to be the separating one.
  3. Put the second plank's centre at L × (rA(L) + rB(L) + 0.05) — along that direction, just far enough to clear both shadows with 5 cm to spare.

By construction L separates them. The question is whether anything else does — and Figure 4 is all fifteen candidates for exactly this configuration, straight out of the harness.

One bar crosses the line. The six face normals report overlaps from 0.11772 m to 3.12792 m — not marginal, not "nearly apart", deeply overlapping by any reading. The single edge-edge axis a₀ × b₀ reports a gap of +0.05000 m, and re-proving it in double precision from the box data gives +0.0500000.

Delete the nine cross products and this is a collision. Two planks with five centimetres of daylight between them, reported as touching, by an engine that will then generate a contact, solve an impulse, and shove them apart — objects visibly jumping away from each other with a gap you can see.

7.4 The same thing, one keystroke apart

The collide demo is built around that configuration, because the argument is much more convincing watched than read. Its right panel draws all fifteen candidate gaps as bars against a zero line; its left panel draws the boxes, green when the test says apart and red when it says touching. Pressing [F] throws away the nine edge-edge candidates and keeps the six face normals.

Same geometry, same frame, one key. The bars do not move — the algorithm is still computing all fifteen for the display — but the verdict flips, and the only bar on the right-hand side of the line is the one the face-only test is not allowed to look at.

7.5 How often it matters, on pairs nobody chose

A constructed counterexample proves that six axes are insufficient. It says nothing about how often that bites, and a fair objection to everything above is "yes, but you built that". So: 200,000 random OBB pairs, random orientations, random half extents from 0.2 to 1.5 m, centres scattered through a 3 m cube.

One pair in twenty-two that a face-only test calls a collision is not one. That is not an edge case; it is a steady background of objects being pushed apart that were never touching, which in a game reads as jitter, as objects refusing to pass through gaps they fit through, and as a character catching on scenery.

And there is a second number, which surprised me more. When boxes genuinely do overlap, which candidate wins the minimum — which one is the shallowest way out?

An edge-edge axis, on 58,550 of 117,583 overlapping pairs — 49.8%.

Worth one caveat: these are uniformly random orientations, and a real scene is not uniformly random — it is full of things resting on floors and stacked squarely, where face contacts dominate. Take 49.8% as the upper end of the range and 4.6% as the number that survives into practice.

7.6 Two controls, both necessary

The first asks what the measurement would say if the thing being measured were completely fine. Run the same comparison on 200,000 pairs with both boxes axis-aligned, where §7.2 argued that the nine cross products contribute nothing: disagreements between face-only and the full fifteen, 0. If that number were not zero, the 5,727 above would be measuring a bug in one of the two implementations rather than a property of geometry.

The second asks what it would say if the implementation were completely broken. We ship two independent formulations of this test — the explicit one in collide, which builds each axis and normalises it, and the matrix one in overlaps, which never forms a cross product at all (§10.2). They were written from different derivations. On the same 200,000 pairs they agree 200,000 / 200,000.

7.7 What a certificate is worth

That last table has a row in it that does more work than the others: every one of the 5,727 re-proved in double precision. It is worth being precise about what that means, because it is the strongest form of testing available in this lesson and it is available because of the shape of the answer rather than because of any cleverness in the harness.

When collide reports a gap it hands back the axis. Checking that claim does not require re-running the search, or trusting a second implementation, or comparing against a reference that might share the same bug. It requires four dot products: 

double gap = |t · L| − (r_A(L) + r_B(L));      // in double, from the box data alone

If that is positive, the boxes are apart. Full stop — not "probably", not "the two implementations agree". The axis is a proof, and the harness checks proofs rather than answers. That is why §A, §D and §H each report a "certifies" count and why those counts are the ones to look at first.

Overlap does not certify itself so cheaply, and the honest thing is to say so. The harness's other half is a witness hunt: walk a 41³ grid over the first box and look for a point inside the second. Finding one is a proof of overlap; not finding one proves nothing. On 365 overlapping pairs it found a witness for 364, and the one it missed has a depth of 4.485e−03 m — a 4 mm overlap, sampled on a grid whose spacing is centimetres. The instrument ran out of resolution, which is a different thing from the answer being wrong, and reporting the depth of the miss is what lets you tell the two apart.

8. The minimum translation, and where it stops being an answer

When the search finds a separating axis it returns immediately, because one witness is a complete proof and nothing examined later could change the answer. When it does not, all fifteen have been examined and we have fifteen overlap depths — and the smallest of them is the minimum translation vector: the shortest push that would end the overlap.

It is the natural output of the search rather than an extra calculation. Every candidate already computed its overlap on the way to failing to separate; keeping the smallest costs a comparison.

8.1 Which way does it point?

A candidate axis has no inherent direction — L and −L cast the same shadow and produce the same overlap. So the sign has to be chosen, once, in one place, and this engine chooses:

Convention. collide(a, b) returns an axis pointing from a toward b. Translating b by +axis × depth separates them; translating a by −axis × depth does too. Written on conventions.html §9e, and checked rather than assumed.

"Checked rather than assumed" is the part that matters. A contact normal pointing the wrong way produces a solver that pulls objects together instead of pushing them apart, and that bug does not look like a sign error — it looks like an instability. Things stick, vibrate, and sink slowly into the floor, and you go looking at the integrator.

So the harness takes 84,586 overlapping pairs, moves the second box by its own reported axis and depth, and asks again:

84,586 overlapping pairs, pushed by their own answerResult
Separated afterwards84,586 / 84,586
Worst residual overlap1.431e−06 m
CONTROL — pushed only half the depth, still overlapping84,586 / 84,586
CONTROL — pushed the same distance the wrong way, still overlapping84,586 / 84,586

The first control says the depth is not generous — half of it is never enough, so the answer is not merely "some number big enough". The second says the direction is not arbitrary.

And the second control is provable, which is the nicest thing in this section. Push b by −depth·L. On the winning axis the overlap doubles. On any other candidate M, moving a distance depth along L changes the centre offset along M by at most depth × |L · M| ≤ depth — and M's overlap was at least depth to begin with, because depth is the minimum. So no candidate's overlap can be driven below zero, and the boxes cannot come apart. The measurement matching a bound you derived first is worth more than the measurement alone.

8.2 The shallowest way out is not the way in

Two unit cubes, centres (1.5, 0.2, 0.1) apart. The overlaps are 0.5 m on x, 1.8 m on y and 1.9 m on z, so the MTV is 0.5000 m along +x.

Suppose the second cube got there by falling: it came in travelling mostly downward. The minimum translation does not care. It pushes sideways, because sideways is shorter. For a shallow overlap that is exactly what you want — it is the smallest correction that fixes the problem, and it is what a resting contact needs to stay stable.

Now push the same two cubes nearly on top of each other: centres (0.1, 0.05, 0) apart. The shallowest escape is now 1.9000 m, and applying it teleports a cube most of two metres in one frame.

This is the MTV's real limit, and it is a regime rather than a bug. The minimum translation is the right answer while an object is shallowly embedded and becomes nonsense once it is more than about half buried — and past halfway it can push an object out the side it never entered, or straight through to the other side. Three things follow, and they are Module 8's remaining shape:

  • Do not let overlaps get deep. 8.6's speculative contacts create a contact slightly before objects touch, so the solver acts while the depth is still millimetres.
  • Do not correct position with a teleport. 8.9 applies an impulse, and 8.10 removes residual penetration gradually with Baumgarte stabilisation rather than all at once.
  • Prefer the direction of motion when you have it. Continuous collision detection asks "when did they first touch?" rather than "how do I get them apart now?", and it is the only correct answer for a bullet.

9. A sphere against a box, including from inside

A sphere against a box is the one test in this file that is not a SAT, and its idea is worth more than the code.

Carry the sphere's centre into the box's own axes — where the box is an AABB — and find the closest point on the box. If that point is farther than the radius, they are apart; if it is closer, the direction to it is the normal and the difference is the depth. The whole thing is one geometric question: where is the nearest bit of this box to that point?

9.1 The clamp, and the 27 cases it does not enumerate

A point can be in one of 27 regions relative to a box: inside, beyond one of six faces, beyond one of twelve edges, or beyond one of eight corners. The closest point is on a face, on an edge, at a corner, or is the point itself — four different answers, and a case analysis over 27 regions is an afternoon and a bug. 

const vec3 closest{std::clamp(p.x, -h.x, h.x),
                   std::clamp(p.y, -h.y, h.y),
                   std::clamp(p.z, -h.z, h.z)};

Three lines, no cases. The reason it works is worth saying out loud, because the same argument appears in §6 and will appear again in 8.6: a box is a product of three intervals, so its axes do not interact. Each coordinate of the closest point depends only on the same coordinate of p, and "which of the 27 regions is this?" is a question the clamp answers without ever asking it. Clamp on two axes and you get an edge; clamp on three and you get a corner; clamp on none and you get the point back.

Carrying the point into the box's axes is Lesson 2.5's identity doing real work: dot(p − c, uᵢ) is the i-th coordinate of p in the box's frame, which is the transpose of the axis matrix applied to the offset — and the transpose is the inverse because the axes are orthonormal. Three dot products in, three scaled adds out.

9.2 The case that is not an edge case

And then the centre is inside the box. The clamp does nothing, the distance is zero, and the direction is 0/0.

It is tempting to file that under "degenerate input" and move on. Do not: it is what a fast sphere looks like on the frame after it tunnels into a wall, which is a thing that happens in every game, every session. Of 100,000 random sphere/box pairs in the harness's fixture, 8,829 have the centre inside the box — 8.8%, and those are random placements rather than adversarial ones.

The version that stops at the clamp — which is what you write if you stop at the obvious version — returns a zero normal on every one of them. Not a NaN, which would at least be loud. A zero vector, which a solver will happily normalise into a NaN four functions later, or silently apply as an impulse of no direction, producing a sphere that sits inside a wall being pushed nowhere.

The answer is the nearest face: find the axis on which the centre is least deeply inside, push out along it, and add the radius on top. It is the same minimum-translation rule from §8 applied to six candidates instead of fifteen. A 0.25 m ball with its centre 5 cm inside a 0.2 m wall comes back with depth = 0.3000 m along the near face's normal — which is 0.25 + 0.05, and which you can check by hand.

10. When two edges are parallel

Now for the part of this lesson where the folklore and the measurement disagree, and the measurement wins.

The nine edge-edge candidates are cross products of unit vectors, and |u × v| = sin θ. When two edges are parallel the cross product is zero, and there is a well-known piece of advice that goes with that: guard the cross products or objects will fall through each other.

10.1 The degenerate case is the common case

First, why anyone cares. It is easy to read "when two edges happen to be parallel" as a rare coincidence. It is not rare — it is the default:

Two objects standing on the same floor have parallel up axes, whatever their yaw. A crate on a floor, two crates side by side, a character on a platform, every prop in every room: one of the nine cross products is degenerate in the single most common arrangement a game ever produces.

And it is never exactly degenerate, which turns out to matter more. Both axes arrive through a quaternion-to-matrix conversion, so two "identical" up axes differ in the last couple of bits, and their cross product comes out around 1.22e−07 rather than zero. Figure 8 measures exactly that.

10.2 Two formulations of the same fifteen tests

The engine ships two. They answer the same question and they respond to degeneracy completely differently, which is the whole of this section.

collide normalises. It builds each candidate axis, divides by its length, and tests. Normalising costs a square root per cross axis — up to nine of them — and it is not optional here, because the depth has to be comparable across candidates. The overlap test is invariant to the axis's length (both sides scale together), but an unnormalised depth comes out in units of |L| = sin θ, so comparing an edge-edge depth against a face depth compares metres against metres-times-sine and picks the wrong winner.

overlaps does not. It builds R = Aᵀ B — the nine dot products between the two boxes' axes — takes the element-wise absolute value, and expresses all fifteen tests in those nine numbers plus the offset in A's frame. No cross product is ever formed and no axis is ever normalised, which is correct for a boolean and costs no square roots at all. It is the formulation you will find printed in Ericson's Real-Time Collision Detection §4.4.1, and in most engines.

The nine edge-edge entries of that table look arbitrary in print. They are not — they fall out of two identities, and we write them as a loop over cyclic indices rather than nine transcribed blocks:

For L = Aᵢ × Bⱼ: Aₖ · L = det(Aₖ, Aᵢ, Bⱼ), which is zero for k = i and ±R[·][j] otherwise; and Bₖ · L = Aᵢ · (Bⱼ × Bₖ), which is ±R[i][·] because B is a right-handed orthonormal frame.

So each entry is a pair of |R| terms picked out by the cyclic index, and the table in the books is this loop unrolled. Deriving it rather than copying it is also how you check it: §7.6 put the two formulations against each other on 200,000 random pairs and they agreed on all 200,000.

10.3 Which one the folklore is actually about

Here is the fixture. Two crates meeting at a corner, sharing an up axis, yawed 0.65 rad about it, overlapping on two axes, sliding along the third — 400 configurations, all of them genuinely overlapping (ground truth is the same fifteen axes computed in double, where the direction error of a cross product is 10⁻¹⁶/sin θ and stays below 10⁻⁸ everywhere in the sweep). Then break the shared axis by a tilt, and sweep the tilt down to zero.

The counts are false separations: solid objects, interpenetrating, reported as not touching.

The normalised form never fails — at any angle, with the guard removed entirely. That is not luck, and it is not that the fixture was too kind. It is a theorem, and it is the same theorem the whole lesson is built on read in the other direction:

If two convex bodies overlap then no direction separates them — not merely none of the fifteen. So an axis whose direction is pure rounding error is still a direction, and it reports an overlap exactly like a good one would. Rounding cannot invent a gap, because there is no direction in which one exists.

The unnormalised form fails badly, and the mechanism is specific. Its test on axis aᵢ × bⱼ compares quantities that all scale with |aᵢ × bⱼ| — the left-hand side is built from R entries that are O(sin θ), and so is the right. As the edges approach parallel, both sides shrink toward zero while the rounding error in them does not, because that error comes from dot products of O(1) vectors. Below about 10⁻⁷ the comparison is noise against noise, and it comes out either way.

Adding a small positive number to every |R| term lifts the right-hand side back above the noise floor. That biases the comparison toward reporting an overlap, which is the safe direction: a false overlap costs one narrow-phase call, and a false separation costs an object falling through the floor. With the epsilon: zero false separations on the same fixture.

10.4 So what is our guard for?

collide still skips cross axes shorter than sqrt(k_parallel_sin2), and now we can say honestly what that is buying, which is three smaller things rather than correctness:

  • It skips redundant work. A parallel edge pair spans no face of the Minkowski sum — the face it would generate has no area — so anything it could separate is already separated by a face normal.
  • It keeps the MTV honest. A near-zero cross normalises to an arbitrary direction, and an arbitrary direction that happened to win the minimum would be a contact normal pointing nowhere in particular.
  • It avoids 0/0. An exactly-zero cross normalises to NaN. Every comparison against NaN is false, so even that fails safe — but relying on that is relying on an accident rather than on a decision.

And the control at the far end is the one that makes the point stick: an exactly degenerate axis is the harmless case. Two axis-aligned cubes, where every cross product is a true zero, give the right answer in all three formulations — the unnormalised test compares 0 > 0, which is false, which is correct. The damage is done by the axis that is almost zero.

The control that explains why this bug survives. On 200,000 pairs at random orientations, the degeneracy guard fired 0 times out of 1,800,000 candidate axes. Random test data does not generate this bug — the probability of two uniformly random axes being parallel to within 10⁻³ is essentially zero — and the configuration that does generate it is the one every scene is full of. A test suite built on random inputs and a game built on floors disagree completely about which inputs are typical.

11. Where the precision floor is

One more thing nobody warns you about, and it has nothing to do with the algorithm.

Take two 1 m cubes with a 1 mm gap between them and ask for the gap. Then move both of them away from the origin — together, keeping everything about their relationship identical — and ask again.

At a kilometre the gap is wrong by 2.3%. At 10 km the error has reached the size of the thing being measured. At 100 km the answer is exactly zero: two boxes a millimetre apart are reported as touching.

Nothing about the algorithm changed and nothing about the boxes changed. Only where they are.

The cause is in the first line of the test, and it is 8.3's lesson arriving for the third time in this module: a derivation says what to compute, not how. The offset t = b.centre − a.centre subtracts two numbers of the size of the world to produce one of the size of a crate. A float has 24 bits of significand, so at 10 km its neighbours are 0.98 mm apart — the millimetre gap is no longer a representable difference, and it was gone before collide was called.

The control confirms which step loses it: recomputing the same gap in double, from the same float centres, still gives 9.765625e−04. The centres themselves cannot express the configuration. 10000 + 1.001 rounds to 10001.000977, and no amount of careful arithmetic afterwards can recover the 0.000023 that was discarded on the way in.

Three consequences, all of them things real engines do:

  • Large worlds recentre. A "floating origin" periodically shifts everything so that the player is near zero. Renderers do it for depth precision; physics needs it for exactly this reason.
  • Solvers work in contact-local coordinates. 8.9's impulses are computed from offsets to the contact point, never from world positions, so the subtraction happens once at the smallest scale available.
  • Manifolds store offsets, not points. 8.7 keeps contact points as vectors from each body's centre, which stays accurate as the body travels — and which is also what makes them re-projectable next frame.

The engine-facing summary is one sentence: every quantity in a collision test should be a difference taken as early as possible. gap_on_axis takes it in its first line, which is why the numbers above degrade gracefully instead of collapsing at 100 m.

12. The budget

Collision detection is called more often than anything else in a physics engine — a scene with 500 bodies has 124,750 possible pairs before a broadphase touches it — so the costs here matter more than the costs in 8.3 did.

Two populations, 20,000 pairs each, because the distribution of the answers turns out to matter more than the arithmetic. SPREAD scatters boxes through a 6 m cube, where 6% overlap; CROWDED puts their centres within 0.6 m, where all of them do.

12.1 A sphere test is sixty times cheaper than the answer it approximates

1.206 ns against 73.323. That is the entire justification for the shape of every broadphase ever written: ask the cheap conservative question first, and only ask the expensive exact one about the survivors.

Measured directly on the spread population, with a bounding-sphere reject in front of overlaps: 10.090 ns → 2.202 ns, a 4.58× speedup, with both arms producing identical counts. The pre-test rejected 17,325 of 20,000 pairs outright, and the control that matters is that it rejected none of the 1,265 real overlaps — a conservative filter may keep as many pairs as it likes, and may never throw away a real one.

It also keeps 1,410 pairs that do not overlap, each of which costs a full test. That is the price of conservatism and it is the number a broadphase is tuned against, not the speedup.

12.2 The early exit is worth more than the arithmetic

The same function is 16.858 ns on spread pairs and 73.323 ns on crowded ones — 4.3×, from identical code. The difference is entirely how many candidates get examined before the answer is known.

12.3 The square roots are 88% of the cost

Everything that buys is square roots and cross products: up to nine sqrt calls per query, which exist only so that the depth is comparable across candidates. If you do not need the depth, do not pay for it — which is exactly the split the two functions represent, and exactly why a broadphase confirmation pass should call overlaps and a contact generator should call collide.

13. What this does not do

Four honest limits, each of which is a later lesson.

  • It does not give you a distance. When two boxes are apart, the reported gap is the gap on one axis, which is a lower bound on the true distance and usually not equal to it — two boxes offset diagonally have a gap on both x and y, and the real distance is neither. The SAT never computes it because a proof of separation does not need one. Lesson 8.5's GJK is the algorithm that does, and this is the honest reason it exists.
  • It does not give you contact points. A normal and a depth are not enough to make a box rest on a floor: a face-face contact needs several points to resist toppling, and one point makes a crate balance on a corner and spin. Lesson 8.7 builds the manifold and keeps it across frames.
  • It does not scale. Every function here answers a question about two shapes. Asking it about every pair in a scene is quadratic, and at 500 bodies that is 124,750 calls per step. Lesson 8.6 is the broadphase that stops you asking.
  • It does not see motion. Everything here is a snapshot. A bullet that is in front of a wall this frame and behind it the next never overlaps anything at any moment we looked, which is tunnelling, and no amount of accuracy in a static test fixes it. Continuous detection asks "when did they first touch?", and it is 8.6's other half.

16. Common pitfalls & debugging

Everything collides with everything

Symptom: objects several metres apart report contacts; the scene behaves as though every body were touching every other.
Cause: the AABB test written as "overlapping on some axis" rather than "on every axis" — || where && was meant. It reads naturally and is wrong 74.9% of the time (§7.2).
Fix: the test is an and over axes and an or is a different predicate entirely. Check against collide(as_obb(a), as_obb(b)), which agrees bit-for-bit with a correct AABB test.

Objects are shoved apart with a visible gap between them

Symptom: two long objects crossing at an angle jump away from each other although you can see daylight between them.
Cause: the six face normals only. §7.3 is this exact configuration, and §7.5 measures it at 4.6% of everything a face-only test calls a collision.
Fix: the nine cross products. Print all fifteen gaps for the failing pair — if exactly one is positive and it is an edge-edge candidate, this is your bug.

The contact normal is a zero vector, or a NaN appears later

Symptom: a sphere sits inside a wall being pushed nowhere; or a NaN shows up in the solver several functions away from anything suspicious.
Cause: a sphere-versus-box test that stops at the clamp. When the centre is inside the box the clamp returns the centre unchanged, the distance is zero, and the direction is 0/0. 8.8% of random placements in §9.2, and every tunnelled projectile.
Fix: the nearest-face branch. And assert that the normal is unit length at the point where the contact is created, not where it is consumed.

The MTV teleports objects across the level

Symptom: an object that sinks into geometry is flung out sideways, sometimes through the other side.
Cause: applying the minimum translation to a deep overlap (§8.2). At half buried, the shallowest escape is most of the object's own width and may be out the face it never entered.
Fix: do not let overlaps get deep — smaller steps, speculative contacts — and remove residual penetration gradually rather than in one frame. Clamp the correction to a few centimetres per step and let it converge.

Two crates on a floor behave differently from two crates in mid-air

Symptom: stacking is jittery or objects occasionally fall through a floor, while the same shapes tested in isolation are fine; a unit test suite is entirely green.
Cause: a degenerate cross-product candidate, in a formulation that does not normalise. Two objects on one floor share an up axis at every yaw, and the shared axis is never exactly shared (§10.1).
Fix: the epsilon on the absolute matrix if you are using the unnormalised form. And note why the tests were green: on 200,000 random orientations the guard fires zero times, so random test data cannot reach this bug. Test the arrangements your game actually makes.

Contacts get unreliable far from the origin

Symptom: physics that is solid near the centre of a large world becomes jittery and imprecise at the map edges.
Cause: §11. The centre difference is a subtraction of world-sized numbers, and at 10 km a float's neighbours are a millimetre apart.
Fix: recentre the world, or keep physics in a local frame. No change to the collision code helps — the information is gone before the test is called, which the double-precision control demonstrates.

The depth is wrong but the boolean is right

Symptom: collisions are detected correctly, but contact normals sometimes point in strange directions and penetration depths are too small.
Cause: unnormalised cross-product axes. The overlap test is invariant to the axis's length; the depth is not, so an edge-edge depth comes out scaled by sin θ and wins the minimum-depth contest it should have lost (§10.2).
Fix: normalise the axis before comparing depths, or divide the depth by the axis's length, which is the same thing done later.

17. Exercises

  1. Check a certificate by hand. Run the harness and take §D.1's winning axis and the two planks' parameters. Compute |t · L|, rA(L) and rB(L) yourself — on paper or in Python — and confirm the 0.05000 m gap. Then do the same for one of the losing candidates and satisfy yourself that its negative number is not a proof of anything.

    Hint: the whole point is that the check needs nothing but the box data and the axis. If you find yourself re-implementing the search, you have answered a harder question than the one asked.

  2. Add a capsule. A capsule — a segment with a radius — is the shape every character controller in every game is made of, and it is nearly free here: the closest point between a point and a segment is a clamp, exactly like §9's. Add shape_kind::capsule, then collide(capsule, sphere) and collide(capsule, capsule). inertia_capsule already exists from 8.3.

    Hint: capsule-versus-capsule is "closest point between two segments", then one sphere test. Do not reach for the SAT — a capsule's surface is curved and the theorem's candidate list is finite only for polytopes.

  3. Measure the cost of the axis ordering. §12.2 tests face normals before cross products because 91% of separated pairs exit within three axes. Reverse the order — cross products first — and measure the spread population again. Then try a third ordering: remember the winning axis from the previous call and test it first.

    Hint: the third one is temporal coherence and it is what 8.6 and 8.7 will do for real. To measure it properly you need pairs that persist across calls, so animate one box slowly rather than generating fresh random pairs.

  4. Find the deep-overlap failure yourself. Write a loop that starts two cubes concentric and moves one of them out along a diagonal in small steps, printing the MTV each time. Identify the step at which the axis jumps discontinuously, and explain from §8.2 why the jump is there and why no implementation choice removes it.

    Hint: the jump happens when two candidates swap which is smaller. Plot all fifteen depths against the offset and it becomes obvious.

  5. Engine challenge: speculative margin. Give collide an optional margin: report contact when depth > −margin rather than depth > 0, and keep the true signed depth in the result. Then re-run §E's measurement with a 5 mm margin and describe what changes — how many pairs now report contact, and what fraction of them have a negative depth. This is the mechanism 8.6 uses to stop fast objects tunnelling, and doing it now is a two-line change to a function you understand.

    Hint: the interesting question is what a solver should do with a contact that has not happened yet. The answer — allow the objects to approach at exactly the speed that closes the gap this step, and no faster — is 8.9's, and the margin is what makes it possible.

18. Recap & next

The engine now knows how big things are, and can answer the question every physics engine is built around.

  • A shape is geometry and a body is dynamics, and they are separate types because collision detection is a query over geometry that has no business knowing what anything weighs. The one arrow between them runs from shape to inertia — a shape knows what it weighs — and it runs one way.
  • The Separating Axis Theorem: two convex sets are disjoint if and only if some direction's projections are disjoint. One half is a lamp and a wall; the other half needs convexity, and a ball resting in a cup shows exactly what it buys.
  • Fifteen candidates for two boxes: three face normals each, and nine cross products of one edge direction from each. Six is not enough, provably — two crossed planks with 5 cm of daylight between them where all six face axes report overlaps up to 3.13 m — and not rarely: 4.6% of everything a face-only test calls a collision, with edge-edge axes supplying the contact normal on 49.8% of random overlapping pairs.
  • An answer that is a certificate. A separating axis can be re-checked in four dot products by anyone, which is why every separated pair in this lesson is verified by re-proving its own axis in double precision rather than by trusting a second implementation.
  • The minimum translation is the shallowest escape, points from the first argument toward the second, and stops being the right answer once an object is half buried — 1.9 m to correct two cubes that are 10 cm out of alignment.
  • Three clamps find the closest point on a box without enumerating 27 regions, because a box is a product of three intervals — and the case where the point is inside is not an edge case, it is 8.8% of random placements and every tunnelled projectile.
  • The degeneracy folklore is about a formulation, not about the algorithm. The normalised form cannot be broken by a garbage axis, because no direction separates overlapping bodies; the unnormalised form fails 188 times in 400 without its epsilon — on two crates meeting at a corner, which is a configuration random test data never produces and every scene is full of.
  • The precision floor is set by where you are, not by what you are doing: a 1 mm gap is 2.3% wrong at a kilometre and unresolvable at ten, because the centre difference subtracts world-sized numbers.

Next: 8.5 — GJK. Everything in this lesson answers yes or no, and §13's first limit is that we cannot say how far apart. The next lesson takes §3.2's Minkowski difference and, instead of enumerating its faces, searches it — using nothing but the support function added in §6.3. The result works for any convex shape, gives a real distance rather than a bound, and does it in a handful of iterations. It is also the reason papers can assume you have it: once GJK exists, "convex" stops being a restriction and starts being an interface.

19. Further reading

  • Christer Ericson, Real-Time Collision Detection, Chapter 4 (§4.4.1) and Chapter 5. The standard reference, and the source of the unnormalised OBB test in §10.2. Read §4.4.1 alongside this lesson's §10.3: the epsilon he adds to the absolute matrix is the one we measured at 188 failures in 400 without it.
  • Stefan Gottschalk, Ming Lin and Dinesh Manocha, “OBBTree: A Hierarchical Structure for Rapid Interference Detection” (SIGGRAPH 1996). Where the fifteen-axis OBB test comes from, with the derivation of why the list is complete.
  • Dirk Gregorius, “The Separating Axis Test Between Convex Polyhedra” (GDC 2013). The best available treatment of what happens when boxes are not enough: the same theorem for arbitrary convex hulls, including the Gauss-map pruning that keeps the edge-edge list from exploding.
  • Stephen Boyd and Lieven Vandenberghe, Convex Optimization, §2.5. The hyperplane separation theorem done properly, for anyone who found §3.1's sketch unsatisfying. Free from the authors.
  • Gino van den Bergen, Collision Detection in Interactive 3D Environments. The other standard reference, and the one to read before Lesson 8.5 — its treatment of the Minkowski difference and support mappings is where GJK stops being mysterious.
  • David Goldberg, “What Every Computer Scientist Should Know About Floating-Point Arithmetic” (1991). §11's background. The relevant idea is that absolute precision is a function of magnitude, which is the whole of why a millimetre disappears at ten kilometres.
