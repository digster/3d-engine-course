Module 3 — Software Rasterizer II · Lesson 3.2

Perspective-Correct Interpolation

Lesson 3.1 proved that depth interpolates exactly across a triangle, and quietly noted that nothing else does. This is the bill for that sentence. We put a checkerboard on a floor, watch it buckle, and fix it with a division that costs one instruction per pixel and follows directly from a fact we already proved.

1The Problem

Put a checkerboard on the ground and look along it. Every square is the same size in the world; on screen they should shrink smoothly toward a vanishing point, in the way that is so familiar it is invisible. Draw that floor with the rasterizer we have and you get Figure 1, left.

The squares are the wrong size everywhere except at the corners. Worse, the pattern kinks along the diagonal the quad happens to be split on — a line that exists nowhere in the geometry, nowhere in the texture, and only in our choice of how to cut a rectangle into two triangles. Split it the other way and the crease moves with it.

You have seen this before

This is the defining visual signature of the first generation of 3-D consoles. The PlayStation had no perspective correction in hardware at all, so its floors and walls warped exactly like this, and the warping swam as the camera moved — because the error depends on where the triangle is, not on what is painted on it. If you have ever wondered why those games look the way they do in a way that emulators reproduce so faithfully, this is most of the answer.

Notice what is not wrong. The triangle is in the right place. Its edges are correct. The three corners have exactly the right texture coordinates. The depth buffer is untouched and was right in Lesson 3.1 and is still right now. The bug is confined to the interior of a triangle, which is the one place we have not looked since Lesson 2.4 — and the reason it survived that long is that Module 2's demo triangles were all roughly parallel to the screen, where, as we are about to prove, the error is exactly zero.

So: what does barycentric interpolation actually promise, and why is that promise not what we want?

2Building Intuition

Stand on a tiled floor and look along it. The tile at your feet fills a large part of your view; the tile ten paces away is a sliver. They are the same tile. Nothing about the tiles changed — what changed is how much screen each one is entitled to, and that is decided by the divide by depth.

Now run that backwards, which is what a rasterizer does. You are standing at some pixel, halfway down the screen between the near edge of the floor and the far edge, and you want to know which tile you are looking at. The tempting answer — halfway between the near tile and the far tile — is wrong, and Figure 2 shows how wrong.

Here is the key move, and it is worth pausing on because the whole lesson turns on it. That crowding is exactly what dividing by w does — it is the same division, applied to the same points. Lesson 3.1 proved that 1/w is a perfectly well-behaved, affine function of the screen position: it is the one quantity that does march in equal steps as you walk across the screen.

So we have a quantity that behaves (1/w) and a quantity that does not (the attribute). The trick, and it is the whole trick, is to multiply the badly-behaved thing by the well-behaved one, interpolate that, and undo the multiplication at the end. It should not be obvious that this works. The next section shows that it does, and why.

3The Theory

3.1 What barycentric interpolation actually promises

It is worth being precise about what we have been using, because the bug is not in the machinery — the machinery does exactly what it says. Given three values at three screen positions, barycentric interpolation produces the unique affine function of the pixel position that agrees with them (Lesson 2.3 §3.7). That is the guarantee. Not “the value on the surface”, not “the right answer” — an affine function of x and y, on the screen.

So barycentric interpolation of a quantity is correct exactly when that quantity happens to be affine in screen space, and wrong otherwise. Lesson 3.1 asked that question about depth and got a surprising yes for device depth and a firm no for view-space z. Now we ask it about everything else.

3.2 An attribute is linear over the surface

First, what is true of a texture coordinate. When a modeller assigns uvs to a triangle, the uv at an interior point is defined to vary linearly across the surface — that is what it means to texture a flat triangle. The same holds for a vertex colour, and for an interpolated normal. So any such attribute a can be written as an affine function of the view-space position:

\[ a(x_v, y_v, z_v) = \alpha x_v + \beta y_v + \gamma z_v + \delta \] a = alpha*x_view + beta*y_view + gamma*z_view + delta

We never need to know the four constants — they are fixed by the three corner values and the triangle's plane, and they will cancel out of everything below. What matters is only that such constants exist, which is precisely the statement “the attribute is linear over the surface”.

3.3 The derivation: a/w is affine too

Take the projection relations from Lesson 2.10, in the form Lesson 3.1 rearranged them into, writing w for -z_view:

\[ x_v = \frac{a_r}{f}\,x_n\,w, \qquad y_v = \frac{1}{f}\,y_n\,w, \qquad z_v = -w \] x_view = (aspect/f) * x_ndc * w, y_view = (1/f) * y_ndc * w, z_view = -w

Substitute all three into the attribute:

\[ a = \left(\alpha\frac{a_r}{f}x_n + \beta\frac{1}{f}y_n - \gamma\right)w \;+\; \delta \] a = ( alpha*(aspect/f)*x_ndc + beta*(1/f)*y_ndc - gamma ) * w + delta

That is not affine in the screen position — there is a w sitting in the middle of it, and w is a hyperbola across the screen. But now divide the whole thing by w:

\[ \frac{a}{w} = \underbrace{\alpha\frac{a_r}{f}x_n + \beta\frac{1}{f}y_n - \gamma}_{\text{affine in }(x_n,\,y_n)} \;+\; \delta\cdot\underbrace{\frac{1}{w}}_{\text{affine, by 3.1}} \] a/w = ( alpha*(aspect/f)*x_ndc + beta*(1/f)*y_ndc - gamma ) + delta * (1/w)

Read the right-hand side. The first group is a constant plus constants times the screen coordinates — affine, by inspection. The second is a constant times 1/w, which Lesson 3.1 §3.4 proved is affine in the screen position. A sum of affine functions is affine. Therefore:

The result

a/w varies affinely across the screen, for any attribute a that is linear over the surface. And 1/w does too. So both can be interpolated barycentrically — exactly, not approximately — and the attribute recovered by dividing one by the other:

\[ a \;=\; \frac{\;\sum_i f_i \, (a_i / w_i)\;}{\;\sum_i f_i \, (1 / w_i)\;} \] a = ( f0*a0/w0 + f1*a1/w1 + f2*a2/w2 ) / ( f0/w0 + f1/w1 + f2/w2 )

where f0, f1, f2 are the same unbiased barycentric weights the fill already computes. Three multiply-adds for the numerator, three for the denominator, one divide. That is the entire technique, and every piece of it was already sitting in the loop.

Why this is one derivation and not four

Nothing above mentioned texture coordinates. α, β, γ, δ were never given values and cancelled from the conclusion, so the result covers a uv, a colour channel, a normal component, a bone weight — anything linear over the surface, which in practice means every attribute a vertex has ever carried. The GPU calls them varyings and applies exactly this to all of them, which is why the hardware documentation can describe interpolation in one paragraph.

3.4 A worked example, by hand

Take the exact edge Lesson 3.1 used, so the two lessons can be read against each other: near = 1, far = 100, a horizontal field of view of 90° so that f / aspect = 1, and an edge with x_view = 1 at both ends running from z_view = −1 to z_view = −100. Paint a texture coordinate along it: u = 0 at the near end, u = 1 at the far end.

Step 1 — which point is under the middle pixel?

Because f/aspect = 1 and x_view = 1, the screen x of any point on this edge is just 1/w. So the two ends sit at x_ndc = 1.00 and x_ndc = 0.01, and the pixel halfway between them is at x_ndc = 0.505.

That pixel sees the point with 1/w = 0.505, i.e. w = 1.9801980 — 1.98 units away, out of a 99-unit span. The middle pixel is looking at something barely past the near end.

The true u there is the fraction of the way along the edge in view space: (1.9801980 − 1) / 99 = 0.00990099.

Step 2 — what affine interpolation says

The pixel is halfway across the screen, so the weights are 0.5 and 0.5:

u = 0.5 × 0 + 0.5 × 1 = 0.500000

Against a truth of 0.0099. That is 50.5× too far along the texture. If the texture were a checkerboard with cells one unit wide, this pixel would be showing a cell fifty cells away from the one it is actually looking at.

Step 3 — what the correction says

Pre-divide both corners by their own w:

  • u/w at the ends: 0 / 1 = 0 and 1 / 100 = 0.01
  • 1/w at the ends: 1 / 1 = 1 and 1 / 100 = 0.01

Interpolate both with the same weights, then divide:

  • numerator: 0.5 × 0 + 0.5 × 0.01 = 0.005
  • denominator: 0.5 × 1 + 0.5 × 0.01 = 0.505
  • u = 0.005 / 0.505 = 0.00990099

Exactly the truth. Not close — equal, to every digit we computed.

Notice the denominator. 0.505 is the interpolated 1/w, and its reciprocal is 1.98 — the w at that pixel, which we never computed directly and never needed to. The division that recovers the attribute recovers the depth as a side effect, which is why one divide serves every attribute at once.

Measured over 199,241 random triangles

scratch/verify_32.cpp generates random triangles in the demo's frustum, gives each one a random attribute linear over its surface, picks an interior point in screen space, and compares both interpolations against the true value at the surface point the ray actually hits:

  • worst error, perspective-correct: 1.3 × 10−12 — double-precision noise, i.e. exact;
  • worst error, affine: 9.89, on attributes whose whole range is about ±13.

Figure 3 shows the same failure spatially rather than at one point: where the rows of the checkerboard should fall, and where affine interpolation puts them.

And Figure 4 is the same thing as a graph, deliberately drawn to match Lesson 3.1's Figure 4 — because it is literally the same curve. Depth was the quantity that came out straight after the projection had finished with it; every other attribute is still on the curve.

3.5 The one attribute that must not be corrected

Having learnt a trick this good, the natural instinct is to apply it to everything. Do not apply it to depth.

Lesson 3.1 §3.4 proved that device depth is already affine in screen space, for the precise reason that z_ndc = −A + B × (1/w) and 1/w is affine. Depth arrives pre-corrected: the projection matrix's third row did this exact job in advance, which is what that row is for. Push it through the correction a second time and you get the interpolation of z_n/w divided by the interpolation of 1/w, which is not z_n and is not anything.

Why this one is nasty

It does not crash, it does not look obviously wrong, and it is self-consistent — every triangle is corrupted the same way, so surfaces still occlude each other plausibly. What you get is subtly wrong occlusion that worsens with distance, which reads like a depth precision problem and sends you off to change your near plane. The regression test is trivial and worth having: the contents of the depth buffer must not change at all when perspective correction is switched on. The harness checks exactly that — over a triangle whose colours change on 20,590 pixels, the depth buffer differs on 0.

So the rasterizer's inner loop treats depth and everything else differently, on purpose, and the code says so where it happens. That asymmetry is not a wart; it is the shape of the problem.

3.6 What it costs, and the escape route people took instead

One divide per pixel. That is the honest headline, and it is a real cost: the depth test in Lesson 3.1 was three multiply-adds and a comparison, and this is arithmetic of a different class. On the hardware of 1994 it was decisive; a divide was tens of cycles and there were only a few million of them per second to spend.

Two things soften it. The divide is paid once per pixel, not once per attribute — the same reciprocal scales the uv, every colour channel, and in Lesson 3.6 the normal too. And the pre-multiplication by 1/w is per vertex, hoisted out of the loop entirely, which is the same hoist that made the linear-light colour blend affordable in Lesson 2.4.

But suppose you cannot afford it. There is an escape route, and an entire console generation took it: subdivide. Affine interpolation is exact at the corners, so if you cut a big triangle into small ones, each small triangle spans a smaller range of w and the chord sits closer to the curve. Chop finely enough and nobody can see the difference.

The demo makes that measurable. T subdivides the floor and the harness reports what each level buys:

Two things in that table are worth more than the headline.

The pixel count is a bad metric, and its badness is instructive. It sits near 48% for the first four rows and then falls off a cliff. That is not the error behaving oddly — it is the metric saturating: once the uv error exceeds half a checker cell the pattern is effectively scrambled, and two scrambled two-colour images disagree on about half their pixels no matter how much more wrong one of them gets. You cannot be more wrong than a coin flip. This is a general hazard when you measure a continuous error through a quantised output, and it is why the last column exists.

The convergence is second order, and it never arrives. The improvement ratios climb 2.31, 2.42, 2.62, 2.84, 3.10 — heading for 4, which is what “halving the interval quarters the error” means for a chord under a smooth curve. (They fall short at the coarse end because the nearest quads still span an enormous range of w; uniform subdivision in world space is a blunt instrument, which is why the near quads are the ones that need it.) But look at the last row: at 2,048 triangles the floor is still wrong on 381 pixels. Subdivision converges. It does not terminate.

4Implementation

Four changes, and three of them are small. A vertex learns its 1/w; the fill's growing pile of trailing parameters becomes an object; the inner loop pre-divides and divides back; and the demo gets a floor worth looking at.

4.1 A vertex learns 1/w

The correction needs the clip-space w at each corner. We have been computing it and throwing it away for two lessons: perspective_divide consumes w and does not hand it back. So project() keeps it, and vertex stores it — pre-inverted.

Stored inverted, not as w. Two reasons, both about the inner loop. It is 1/w that gets interpolated, so storing w would mean a divide per vertex and then the same divide again per pixel. And the “divide back” at the end becomes a multiply by a reciprocal we had to compute anyway.

The default is load-bearing

inv_w = 1.0f is not a placeholder — it is the correct value for the 2-D case. With w = 1 at every corner the numerator and denominator both scale by 1, the division is by exactly one, and the perspective-correct path returns precisely the affine answer. Which is right: affine interpolation is correct under an orthographic projection, and a flat 2-D fill is orthographic. Every triangle drawn in Modules 1 and 2 goes through the new code and comes out bit-identical — the harness checks 17,275 covered pixels and finds 0 differences.

4.2 Four trailing parameters become a pipeline object

fill_triangle ended Lesson 3.1 with a blend_space parameter. This lesson wants to add two more — the interpolation mode and how a pixel turns (u,v) into a colour — and Lesson 3.6 will want a fourth. Four trailing enums at every call site is where an API starts to rot.

This is tidiness, but it is not only tidiness — it is the shape the hardware has. A GPU does not take render state as arguments to a draw call. It bakes state into a pipeline object built once and bound before drawing, because validating and compiling that state per draw would be ruinous. SDL_GPUGraphicsPipelineCreateInfo is this struct, several times over. Module 4 makes the argument properly; adopting the shape now means Lesson 3.6 costs one field instead of one more parameter at every call site.

Every field defaults to the correct value, so fill_triangle(fb, a, b, c) is right and each deliberately-broken mode has to be asked for by name. That is the same discipline as blend_space::encoded and draw_line_naive: keep the failure, make it opt-in.

C++ in place — designated initialisers

C++20 lets you name the fields you are setting: fill_style{.interp = interpolation::affine}. The rest take their defaults, and the reader does not have to count commas or remember the order. Two rules that catch people: the initialisers must appear in declaration order (unlike C), and you cannot mix named and positional. Both restrictions exist so that a struct's field order stays a private matter, which is exactly what you want here — 3.6 will insert a field and no call site should care.

The same feature cleans up the 2-D call sites in the demo, where vertex now has seven fields and only two of them are interesting: vertex{.x = vx[0], .y = vy[0], .colour = red}.

And immediately above it, untouched, sits the depth test — interpolating v.z directly, with no correction, for the reason §3.5 gave. The comment in the source says so at the point where somebody would otherwise "fix" it.

4.4 Something to paint on it

A uv that nothing reads proves nothing, and we have no textures until Lesson 3.9. So the rasterizer gains one procedural pattern:

std::floor, not a cast

A cast to int truncates toward zero, so -0.5 and +0.5 both land in cell 0 and the pattern grows a doubled cell straddling the origin — a seam that appears only where a uv goes negative, which is exactly what happens the moment a mesh is tiled or a uv is offset. Our floor's uvs run from -3 to +3 across, so this one would have shipped visible. std::floor is the function that means “which cell” for negative numbers too.

A shading enum inside the rasterizer is frankly a placeholder for a fragment shader, and the header says so. A real renderer lets the caller supply the function; Module 4 does exactly that and calls it a fragment shader. Lesson 3.6 will add a lighting term and this enum will start to strain — which is the point at which the right structure will have earned itself, rather than being asserted three lessons early.

4.5 A floor, and the viewport that finally moves

The demo needs a surface with a violent depth gradient. The floor runs from about a unit in front of the camera to thirty-seven units away — a 37:1 range of w — because that ratio is precisely what decides how loud the artifact is.

That immediately broke something. Every scene since Lesson 2.10 has drawn into an inset 172×96.75 rectangle with the HUD beside it, and a ground plane cannot fit in a sub-rectangle: measured across every extent worth having (scratch/fit_floor.cpp), the near edge always projects outside the NDC box. That is not a tuning failure. A surface you are standing on fills the bottom of your view — which is the same property that makes it a good subject for this lesson.

So the floor draws through a viewport that is the whole framebuffer. This costs nothing and needs no new projection matrix, because 320×180 is already 16:9 — the same aspect the projection bakes in. It is also the first time this course has changed the viewport at all, which is the entire reason Lesson 2.11 made it a struct with a rectangle in it rather than three constants. The change was to thread it through project, line3, draw_world, draw_mesh and collect_triangles as a parameter — which is what it always should have been.

The floor itself is built at runtime, and that is worth a paragraph because it is the first mesh in this course that is:

An engine::mesh is a pair of non-owning spans (Lesson 2.12). Every mesh so far has viewed inline constexpr arrays with program lifetime, so nobody had to own anything. A floor whose tessellation changes at runtime does, and the demo ends up holding three vectors and a view() that manufactures a mesh over them. That awkwardness is not a design failure — it is the exact pressure that produces Module 5's asset system, and it is better felt than described.

One detail in build_floor is load-bearing: the uvs are computed from the world position, not from the grid index. That is what makes the checker pattern bit-identical at every tessellation level, so pressing T changes only how many triangles the interpolation has to span. Without it you would be comparing two different pictures and learning nothing.

4.6 Measuring it

Lesson 3.1 built a comparison harness into the demo: render the scene twice with one thing changed and count the pixels that disagree. That generalises for free — the only question is which thing to change.

On the solid scenes the second render changes the hidden-surface strategy (3.1's number); on the floor it changes the interpolation (3.2's). One scratch framebuffer, one extra pass, and the claim “this is wrong” becomes a number that moves while you orbit.

7Common Pitfalls & Debugging

Occlusion goes subtly wrong with distance after adding the correction

Cause. You applied the correction to depth as well. Device depth is already affine in screen space (Lesson 3.1 §3.4) — the projection matrix's third row did that job in advance. Correcting it a second time produces z/w over 1/w, which is not depth and is not anything.

Why it is nasty. It is self-consistent: every triangle is corrupted the same way, so surfaces still occlude each other plausibly. The symptom reads like a depth-precision problem and sends you off to change your near plane.

Fix, and the test. Interpolate vertex::z directly. Then assert the thing that must be true: the depth buffer's contents must not change at all when perspective correction is toggled. Over a triangle whose colours change on 20,590 pixels, the depth buffer must differ on zero.

The texture is right at the corners and wrong in the middle

Cause. This is the lesson's own artifact — affine interpolation. But it is worth knowing as a signature, because it identifies the whole family: an error that is exactly zero at every vertex and maximal in the interior is a chord drawn under a curve.

Why it survives review. Every check you would naturally run — print the corner uvs, verify the mesh, look at the wireframe — passes perfectly. The error lives only where nothing is printed.

Fix. Interpolate a/w and 1/w, then divide. And add the diagnostic that would have caught it: sample a point in the middle of a triangle and compare against the ray-plane intersection.

Everything looks perfect on your test scene and breaks in the game

Cause. Your test scene faces the camera. If a triangle's plane is parallel to the screen then w is constant across it, 1/w is constant, and affine and perspective-correct interpolation agree exactly. Sprites, UI quads, billboards and the front faces of axis-aligned boxes are all in this family.

Why it matters beyond this bug. The same blind spot hid Lesson 3.1's view-space-depth error, for the same reason. When something "works on my test scene", the useful question is not "what is different about the game" but "what family of input does my test scene structurally exclude?"

Fix. Put a steeply-angled surface in the test scene permanently. Ours is the floor, and it is one keypress away.

A seam of doubled cells where the texture coordinate crosses zero

Cause. A procedural pattern using static_cast<int> instead of std::floor. The cast truncates toward zero, so -0.5 and +0.5 both land in cell 0 and that cell is twice as wide as every other.

Why you may not hit it. Only if your uvs go negative — which they do the moment a texture is tiled, a uv is offset, or (as here) a floor is centred on the origin.

Fix. std::floor. The same trap appears in tile lookups, grid hashing and spatial partitioning, and Module 7's broadphase will meet it again.

Subdividing helps, so you conclude the bug is fixed

Cause. Affine error falls with the square of the subdivision, so a few rounds of tessellation make it invisible on your test camera — and it comes straight back when someone walks closer, or the field of view widens, or a level designer builds a long corridor.

The measurement that settles it. At 2,048 triangles our floor is still wrong on 381 pixels. Convergence is not termination.

Fix. Pay the divide. If you genuinely cannot — and on 1996 hardware people genuinely could not — then subdivide adaptively, by the range of w a triangle spans rather than uniformly in world space, because the near triangles are the only ones that need it.

8Exercises

3.2.1Move the seamwarm-up

In affine mode the checker kinks along the diagonal each quad is split on. Change build_floor to split its quads the other way — (0,1,2)+(0,2,3) becomes (0,1,3)+(1,2,3) — and predict what happens before you run it.

Solution

The kink moves to the other diagonal, and the pattern is wrong in a mirrored way. This is worth doing because of what it proves: the artifact depends on a choice that is not in the geometry, not in the texture, and not in the camera. Two renderers that triangulate a quad differently produce different images of the same scene. That is a decisive argument on its own — a correct renderer's output cannot depend on how you happened to cut a rectangle in half.

Check the perspective-correct mode too: it is identical under both splits, to the pixel. It must be, because it reconstructs the surface rather than the triangulation.

3.2.2Hoist the dividemedium

When all three corners share the same inv_w — every 2-D fill, every screen-parallel surface — the denominator is constant across the triangle and the per-pixel divide is pure waste. Detect that case once per triangle and hoist it. Measure the difference on the triangle demo (Tab) and on the floor.

Hint

iw0 == iw1 && iw1 == iw2 is an exact float comparison, and here that is correct rather than sloppy: the values are either copied from the same source or they are not. Think about what the reciprocal should be in that case — it is not 1.

Solution sketch

The hoisted reciprocal is 1 / iw, not 1 — the weights sum to one, so the denominator is iw itself. You will find the 2-D demos get measurably faster and the floor does not change at all, which is the honest result: you have optimised the case that was already cheap. The interesting question the exercise leaves you with is whether a branch per triangle is worth it for a divide per pixel, and the answer depends entirely on how many of your triangles are screen-parallel — in a UI-heavy application, most of them.

3.2.3Find the worst pixelmedium

Our counter says how many pixels are wrong, which saturates (§3.6). Write a diagnostic that reports how wrong the worst one is: for each covered pixel compute both the affine and the corrected u, and track the maximum difference. Report it in the HUD in checker cells.

Hint

You already have both numbers inside the loop — the affine value is the numerator before the divide-back, scaled differently. Easier: compute both explicitly in a debug build. Also ask where the worst pixel is; the answer is not where most people guess.

Solution sketch

You should reproduce the harness's numbers: 4.23 cells at 1×1, falling to 0.03 at 32×32. The worst pixel is not at the far end — out there both schemes agree because everything is compressed into nothing. It is in the middle of the biggest triangle, where the chord is furthest from the curve, which is exactly what Figure 4 shows and exactly where nobody looks.

3.2.4Adaptive subdivisionhard

Uniform tessellation is a blunt instrument: the near quads span an enormous range of w and the far ones span almost none, yet they get the same treatment. Subdivide a triangle only while the ratio w_max / w_min across it exceeds some threshold. How many triangles does it take to match 16×16 uniform, and where do they go?

Solution sketch

Far fewer, and they all pile up near the camera. This is the algorithm the better fifth-generation engines actually shipped, and playing with the threshold gives you a feel for why it was a losing battle: the triangle budget needed depends on the camera, so it has to be recomputed every frame, and a player who walks up to a wall can bankrupt it. Meanwhile one divide per pixel costs the same no matter where anyone stands. Write down the threshold that makes the two approaches cost the same on your machine — that number is the whole history of the technique.

3.2.5What else is on the curve?open

The derivation never mentioned texture coordinates. Give the floor's vertices per-corner colours instead of uvs, switch the shading to vertex_colour, and toggle I. Then reason about what happens in Lesson 3.6 when we interpolate a normal.

Solution sketch

Colour is wrong in exactly the same way and for exactly the same reason — but it is far harder to see, because a colour gradient has no sharp features for your eye to register the distortion against. This is a general and slightly uncomfortable lesson: how visible a bug is depends on the frequency content of what it is corrupting, not on how big the error is. The checkerboard is a good test pattern precisely because it is nearly all edge.

Normals are worse, for a reason worth anticipating: an interpolated normal must also be renormalised after interpolation, because the average of two unit vectors is not a unit vector. So a normal gets both the perspective divide and a normalise — two corrections, in that order, and Lesson 3.6 has to get both right before any lighting means anything.

9Recap & Next

What the engine can do now that it could not before

  • Carry any attribute across a triangle correctly, at any angle, at any depth — texture coordinates today, normals in 3.6, anything a vertex ever holds.
  • Draw a plane running to the horizon with two triangles. Not two thousand.
  • Reproduce the failure on demand, with a live count of how wrong it is, and a subdivision control that shows exactly what the alternative would have cost.
  • Render state as an object rather than a growing tail of parameters — the shape Module 4's pipelines have.

And the ideas worth taking beyond this engine

  • Interpolate the quantity that is affine in the space you are walking. You are walking pixels, so ask what is affine in pixels. Depth already was; a/w and 1/w are; a is not. This one sentence covers Lessons 3.1 and 3.2 together.
  • An error that is zero at the corners and maximal in the middle is a chord under a curve. Recognise the shape and you know where to look, and why every vertex-level check passed.
  • Ask what your test scene structurally cannot show. Screen-parallel geometry cannot reveal either of Module 3's interpolation bugs. That is not bad luck; it is a property of the test set, and it is findable in advance.

Next: Lesson 3.3 — Near-Plane Clipping. You have already seen its motivation: orbit the floor and it disappears. Our projection guard drops any triangle with a vertex closer than w = 0.05, whole — which is survivable when the geometry is a small object in the middle of the view and catastrophic when it is the wall you are walking into. The fix is not a bigger guard, it is to cut the triangle against the near plane and rasterize the part that is in front, which turns one triangle into one or two. That is Sutherland–Hodgman, and it is the last thing standing between our rasterizer and geometry it cannot be trusted with.

10Further Reading

  • Kurt Akeley & Tom Jermoluk, “High-Performance Polygon Rendering” (SIGGRAPH 1988), and Paul Heckbert & Henry Moreton, “Interpolation for Polygon Texture Mapping and Shading” (1991). The primary sources for rational-linear interpolation. Heckbert’s treatment is the one to read if you want the projective-geometry framing rather than the substitution we did.
  • Chris Hecker, “Perspective Texture Mapping” (Game Developer Magazine, 1995–96, five parts). Written while this was still an open engineering problem, on hardware where a divide per pixel was unaffordable. The best possible illustration of §3.6: watch a very good engineer spend five articles on the escape routes we can now decline in one line.
  • Real-Time Rendering, 4th edition, §23.1–23.2. How the hardware does it, and why the reciprocal is computed once per fragment for all varyings at once.
  • Scratchapixel, “Rasterization: a Practical Implementation”. Covers depth and perspective-correct interpolation together, with the same first-principles bent as this course.
  • Fabien Sanglard, Game Engine Black Book: DOOM and Wolfenstein 3D. Both engines dodge this problem entirely by restricting geometry — axis-aligned walls and floors of constant height — which is the third escape route: make the case that breaks impossible to author. Worth knowing as an option; it is why those games look the way they do.
  • The PlayStation’s GTE and GPU documentation (the psx-spx community reference). The hardware that made this artifact famous, described in enough detail to see that omitting perspective correction was a deliberate, costed decision rather than an oversight.
