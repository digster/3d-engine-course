Module 5 — Becoming an Engine · Lesson 5.3

Logging, Assertions, and Errors Without Exceptions

This engine has 197 logging calls and every single one of them is SDL_Log — one category, one level, no way to turn any of it down. It also has five different answers to "how does a function report failure", each defensible on its own and none of them the same. Today we fix both, and the two turn out to be one problem: a message and a return value are the same statement told to two audiences. Along the way we will measure exactly what a release build removes — and find a real bug in a macro written earlier in this very lesson.

What this lesson is not going to do

It is not going to invent a sixth. §3.4 is about noticing that the engine already converged on a good answer twice, independently, and that naming that answer is cheaper and truer than designing a new one. The road not taken — a hand-rolled result<T> in the shape of C++23's std::expected — is discussed honestly and then made Exercise 9.5, with the argument for why this codebase does not need it yet.

2Building Intuition

2.1   A log is a letter to your future self

Here is the situation logging exists for, and it is worth picturing concretely because every design decision below follows from it.

Somebody you have never met runs your engine on hardware you do not own. Something looks wrong — a texture is black, the frame rate halves after ten minutes, the program exits immediately on their machine and never on yours. They cannot attach a debugger, you cannot reproduce it, and the only channel between the two of you is whatever the program said while it was running.

That is the audience. Not you, at your desk, with a breakpoint available. You, in six months, reading a paste from a stranger. And it changes what a good message is:

  • Facts beat narration. "Loading texture…" tells you nothing. "uv_grid.png 256×256, 3→4 channels, 262144 bytes from 1359 on disk" tells you the file was found, was a PNG, had no alpha, and cost a quarter of a megabyte.
  • The reason travels with the failure. A line that says "could not create texture" and a line that says which texture, at what size, and what the driver said, are different tools.
  • Volume destroys value. A thousand lines a frame is not a log, it is a haystack. The stranger will paste the last twenty lines, and if those twenty are all "drew a triangle" you have learned nothing.

The last one is why this lesson exists at all. Everything else about logging is a detail; the thing that makes a log useful is being able to choose what is in it, at run time, without a rebuild, by somebody who is not you.

2.2   Two axes, not one

So we need to filter. Filter on what?

The instinct is a single dial: a "verbosity level" from 0 to 5. Turn it up, get more. That works for a program with one subsystem, and it breaks the moment you have two — because the interesting question is almost never "how much output do I want in total". It is "I am debugging the GPU path; show me everything it says and nothing else." A single dial cannot express that. Turn it up for the GPU and you get the asset system's chatter too.

So there are two independent questions about every message, and they are genuinely independent:

QuestionAnswer is a…Ours
Who is speaking?categorycore, platform, gfx, gpu, asset
How much does it matter?priority / leveltrace … critical

A filter is then a table: one threshold per category. "Show me GPU at debug, and everything else only if it is an error" is gpu=debug,*=error — a sentence a person can type, and one that a single dial cannot say at all.

The mistake that looks like the answer

The classic failure is to put severity into the category list: LOG_ERROR, LOG_WARNING, LOG_DEBUG as categories. It feels tidy and it destroys the whole mechanism, because you can then never say "errors from the GPU but not from assets" — the two axes have been collapsed back into one and you are holding a dial again.

A category answers who. It is a noun, and it names a part of your program. If one of your categories is an adjective, it is a level wearing a disguise.

2.3   An assertion is not an error

The third piece, and the one most often muddled. Two things go wrong in a program and they are different in kind:

An error is something the world does to you. A file is missing. A GPU refuses a format. A user typed a path that does not exist. None of this is your program being wrong — it is your program meeting a world that is under no obligation to cooperate. Errors are expected: not welcome, but anticipated, and the correct response is to hand the caller a failure they can act on. They survive into release builds, because the world does not become friendlier when you ship.

An assertion is a claim about your own code. This index is in range. This pointer is not null. This function was called after start(). If one of these is false, the program is in a state its author did not anticipate — and there is nothing sensible to do about it, because the code that would do the sensible thing was written by the same person who was already wrong.

One question separates them, and it settles almost every case:

The test

Could a correct program, running on a working machine, encounter this?

Yes → it is an error. Return it.  ·  No → it is an assertion. Stop.

load_image("missing.png") — yes, of course a correct program can ask for a file that is not there. Error. framebuffer::row(-1) — no correct caller does that; the only way to get there is a bug above. Assertion.

The reason this matters practically is that the two want opposite treatment in a shipping build. An error path must stay: delete it and your program corrupts data instead of reporting a bad file. An assertion may go: it is a development tool, its cost is paid on every call, and by the time you ship you have either fixed the bug or you have not. Figure 4, later, is this decision as a picture.

3The Theory

3.1   Levels as promises, and who makes them

A level is only useful if it means the same thing everywhere. "Warning" that sometimes means "fatal" and sometimes means "hmm" is a level nobody can filter on, because filtering it out might lose something important. So a level is a promise, and the promise has to be written down. Figure 2 is ours, with the count of how many of the engine's 92 messages landed at each:

Read the six meanings in that figure carefully; two of them do real work.

warn means we recovered. That is the whole distinction from error. If the operation happened — perhaps differently, perhaps worse — it is a warning. If it did not happen, it is an error. This is a mechanical test, not a judgement call, which is exactly what you want from a rule that 92 call sites have to follow.

debug means "what I did", info means "what I am". A texture reporting its size once at load is info — it is a fact about the program that somebody might want on a bug report. A texture reporting each load in a level with four hundred of them is debug: correct, useful when you are investigating loading, and intolerable otherwise.

3.1.1   Who logs a failure?

Now the question that is genuinely contested, and which the engine had been answering inconsistently. A failure happens deep — SDL_CreateGPUTexture returns null — and is reported upward through three or four layers. Which of them writes the log line?

There are two coherent answers, and it is worth stating both before choosing.

Answer A: the caller logs. Whoever decides what the failure means writes the line. Low-level code returns quietly. The argument: only the caller knows whether a missing texture is fatal or a fallback, so only the caller can pick a level honestly.

Answer B: the deepest point that knows why logs, and callers propagate silently. The argument: only the deepest point has the reason — the path, the byte count, the string in SDL_GetError() that is about to be overwritten by the next SDL call. Push the logging upward and every layer boundary loses detail, until the top-level line reads "startup failed" and helps nobody.

We take B, and the deciding argument is about which failure mode is worse. Under A, detail is lost quietly — you cannot tell from the log that it is missing, because the line you are reading looks fine. Under B, the cost is that a caller who recovers from a failure still gets an error line it did not deserve — which is visible, annoying, and fixable by demoting that particular call to debug. A cost you can see beats a cost you cannot.

The rule, and its corollary

Log the failure once, at the deepest point that knows why. Callers propagate silently, and add only the consequence — the thing they know that the callee does not.

In practice that reads like this, and the second line is the whole of what the demo adds:

load_image:  ERROR  "load_image: cannot read assets/uv_grid.png (No such file)"
the demo:   INFO   "texture: uv_grid.png did not load — [T] will do nothing"

Two lines, no repetition, and each says something the other could not. Compare the version where four layers each log "failed": four lines, one problem, and the informative one is at the top of a scroll.

3.2   Why the default costs nothing

Here is the part that decided the whole design, and it came from reading SDL rather than from a design meeting.

SDL keeps a per-category priority table, and it initialises that table from a documented default (SDL_HINT_LOGGING in SDL3/SDL_hints.h, implemented in src/SDL_log.c):

Read *=error again. Every category SDL does not know about defaults to error — and SDL does not know about ours, because SDL_LOG_CATEGORY_CUSTOM is precisely where SDL stops and applications begin.

So the behaviour we want — engine quiet, game loud — is not something we have to build. It falls out of moving the engine's messages off SDL_LOG_CATEGORY_APPLICATION. Figure 3 is the whole mechanism.

This is worth dwelling on because of what it says about the wrapper question. Lesson 5.2 settled a policy — a wrapper that hides a library you have no intention of replacing is cost with no benefit — and here is that policy paying out. A hand-rolled logging layer would have had to reimplement per-category thresholds, an environment-variable override, and a default table. It would have got a different default table, and the free behaviour above would have been a feature somebody had to think of.

What is left for us is genuinely small: names for our own categories, a compile-time floor SDL cannot provide, and a flag parser. That is the entire content of log.hpp and log.cpp.

3.3   SDL's assertions, and the loop inside them

Figure 4 is §2.3's test drawn as a decision, together with the three macros that follow from it.

C's assert() prints a message and calls abort(). SDL's does something considerably more interesting, and it is worth understanding before using it, because one of its behaviours will otherwise look like a bug.

An SDL assertion asks a handler what to do, and the handler may answer five ways (SDL3/SDL_assert.h):

SDL_AssertStateWhat SDL then does
SDL_ASSERTION_RETRYre-evaluate the condition, right now
SDL_ASSERTION_BREAKtrip the debugger's breakpoint
SDL_ASSERTION_ABORTterminate
SDL_ASSERTION_IGNOREcarry on, this once
SDL_ASSERTION_ALWAYS_IGNOREcarry on, and never ask about this assertion again

RETRY is the surprising one, and it is not a metaphor. Look at what the macro actually expands to:

It is a while loop over the condition. So RETRY genuinely re-tests: stop in a debugger, fix the state by hand, continue, and the program proceeds as though the bug had not happened. On an assertion that fires on frame 40,000 of a session you cannot reproduce, that is worth a great deal.

And note the static struct SDL_AssertData. It is per expansion site, it carries always_ignore and trigger_count, and SDL threads every one of them onto a list you can walk with SDL_GetAssertionReport(). Two consequences follow, one convenient and one to be careful about:

  • Assertions are testable. Install a handler that returns IGNORE, run the code that should trip, and read the report. §7's harness does exactly this — it proves an assertion fires on the input that should fire it, which most people assume is impossible and therefore never check.
  • ALWAYS_IGNORE latches. It sets a flag in that static, so the assertion fires once and then goes quiet for the rest of the run. That is a feature when you are grinding through a level with a known glitch, and a trap when you are counting.

3.3.1   Which survive a release build — and the gate that decides

SDL picks an assertion level for you unless you set one, and the condition it picks on is not NDEBUG:

__OPTIMIZE__ is defined by GCC and Clang whenever any -O above zero is in effect. So -O2 alone takes you to level 1, where SDL_assert is disabled and only SDL_assert_release survives — with no -DNDEBUG anywhere in sight.

That is the correct behaviour and it is also a trap, because most projects gate their own debug machinery on NDEBUG. Two gates, two different switches. §4.5 is where that mismatch produced an actual bug in this lesson's own code, and §5 measures the four resulting build configurations rather than reasoning about them.

3.4   The shape the engine already had

Back to §1.1's five conventions. The temptation is to design a sixth and convert everything to it. Before doing that, it is worth looking at the five properly, because two of them are the same thing — and they were written a module apart, by nobody trying to match the other.

obj_report (Lesson 3.5) and gpu_report (Lesson 4.2) are both: a status enum that names the failure precisely, plus the facts you ask for next, plus a bool ok(). That convergence is evidence. Two independent attempts at the same problem reached the same shape because the shape fits the problem, and a design that a codebase keeps rediscovering is a design worth naming rather than replacing.

So the plan is not a new error type. It is:

  1. Name the pattern — status enum, facts, ok() — and write it down as the engine's answer for anything whose failure a caller might branch on.
  2. Convert the odd one out. image_status is the same problem solved worse: a bare enum, with the interesting numbers thrown away. It becomes image_report, and that is this lesson's one end-to-end conversion.
  3. Leave bool alone where it is honest. platform::set_vsync has exactly one failure mode and the caller's only sane response is to log and carry on. A report there is ceremony. A bool is honest when there is nothing more to say.
  4. Kill the fifth convention — "returns false and has already logged" — by making it a documented rule (§3.1.1) rather than a per-function surprise.
The road not taken, honestly

C++23 has std::expected<T, E>: a value or an error, in one return, with no out-parameter and no way to read the value without checking. It is the right long-term answer for a codebase with no exceptions, and we are on C++20, so we would hand-roll it — about 120 lines.

We are not going to, and the reason is not difficulty. It is that the engine has two working instances of a pattern that already solves the problem, and replacing them would mean touching 61 bool-returning functions to gain a type-system guarantee we can get today by reading a [[nodiscard]] warning. Adopt a new abstraction when the old one has failed you, not when the new one is elegant. Exercise 9.5 builds it anyway, because you should know what it costs — and because the day the engine does need it, you will want to have written one.

4Implementation

4.1   Categories, based where SDL says

Three decisions in eight lines.

Based at SDL_LOG_CATEGORY_CUSTOM, not at a number. SDL's header reserves everything from that enumerator upward for applications, and says so in a comment. Writing = 20 instead would work today and break the week SDL adds a category — silently, by making two different things share an integer.

Five, and they are nouns. Each names a part of the program. If one of your categories is an adjective you have written a level and mislabelled it (§2.2). Five is also deliberately few: a person can hold five in their head, and merging two later costs one grep while splitting one costs nothing at all.

log_category_count is not a category. It is the end marker that lets us iterate, and it is what makes this possible in log.cpp:

4.2   The compile-time floor, and why not #if

SDL's filtering is a runtime comparison. That is right for most messages and wrong for one kind: a trace call inside a loop that runs a million times a frame still costs a call and a branch a million times, even when it prints nothing. So the macros carry a compile-time floor as well.

The obvious way to write that is #if:

In a release build that discards the arguments without compiling them. So a trace call that names a variable somebody later renamed keeps compiling in release, and breaks the day a colleague builds in debug — a failure discovered by the person least able to explain it. Logging that only compiles in one configuration rots, quietly, in the configuration nobody builds.

C++ in place — if constexpr

if constexpr (cond) with a compile-time constant condition discards the branch that is not taken — the statement is removed before code generation, so nothing reaches the object file. What it does not do is skip checking it: for non-template code the discarded branch is still parsed, name-resolved and type-checked.

That is exactly the pair of properties we want. The deletion of #if, with the type-checking of ordinary code. §4.5's measurement (Figure 6) confirms both halves: a disabled ENGINE_LOG_TRACE compiles to the same four bytes as an empty function, and the object file does not even reference SDL_LogTrace.

The do { … } while (false) around it is the standard idiom that makes a multi-statement macro behave like a single statement, so that if (x) ENGINE_LOG_INFO(…); else … parses the way it reads.

4.3   A parser that changes nothing when it fails

set_log_levels("gfx=debug,*=warn") is forty lines of string handling, and one design decision worth more than the other thirty-nine: it validates the whole spec before applying any of it.

Consider the straightforward implementation — parse a token, call SDL_SetLogPriority, parse the next. Now feed it gfx=debug,gpu=nonsens. The first entry is applied, the second fails, and you return an error. The user now has a program in a state they did not ask for and did not choose: partially configured, with no way to tell which half took. They will spend the next twenty minutes wondering why gpu is quiet.

A rejected spec is now a no-op, which is the only behaviour a user can reason about: either the whole thing took effect or none of it did. The harness pins it (§7, §C) by setting a level, feeding a spec whose last entry is bad, and asserting the good first entry did not apply.

One smaller decision, stated because it is a choice and not an oversight: * means our categories, not everything. Silencing SDL's own diagnostics is not ours to do on a user's behalf — and if they want that, SDL_LOGGING is right there, documented, with the same grammar. Which is why we borrowed the grammar: two things a person might type should not need two mental models.

4.4   A file sink that tees

set_log_file(path) uses SDL_SetLogOutputFunction, and the interesting line is this one:

SDL_SetLogOutputFunction replaces. Install a file writer and call it done, and you have written a "log to a file" feature that silently costs the user their console — which is not what anybody means by "also log to a file". Capturing the previous hook and calling it makes the sink additive, which is what the word "also" has to mean.

Two details worth stealing. The file is written first and the console second: if the program dies inside the rest of the chain, the file already has the line, and log files exist for the runs that ended badly. And SDL holds a mutex across the output callback, so the file is written from one thread at a time even when Module 9's job system is pushing messages from several — a guarantee we get for free by living inside SDL's hook rather than beside it.

The general lesson, which is bigger than this macro

So the classification is a written rule applied by a program, scratch/convert_logs_53.py. Category comes from the file's directory, because that is what a category is. Level comes from an ordered list of patterns, and the order matters:

5.1   What is still on the wrong side

  • 61 bool-returning functions are untouched. Most are honest (§3.4, rule 3), but not all — gpu_texture::create_sampled has four distinct failure modes and returns one bit. Converting them is a lesson of its own, and it should happen when a caller actually needs to branch, not before.
  • obj_status, gpu_status and image_status are three enums, not one. Deliberate: a per-subsystem enum names its own failure modes precisely, and a single engine-wide status becomes a junk drawer where bad_format means six different things. The shape is unified; the vocabularies are not, and should not be.
  • No log rotation, no timestamps of our own, no structured output. The file sink is 30 lines and writes what SDL formatted. When a session log matters enough to want JSON lines and a size cap, that is the moment to add them.
  • log_gfx has no users. See above. Module 6.

8Common Pitfalls & Debugging

1 · Your new log line does not appear

Symptom. You add ENGINE_LOG_INFO(log_gpu, …), run, and see nothing.

Cause. Working as designed. Engine categories default to error (§3.2).

Fix. --log gpu=info. If you meant it to be visible always, you meant a different level — ask §3.1's question: did the operation happen?

2 · …and it still does not appear, at any level

Symptom. --log gpu=trace and still silence.

Cause. The compile-time floor. In a build with -DNDEBUG, ENGINE_LOG_TRACE and ENGINE_LOG_DEBUG are not compiled at all — no runtime setting can bring back a call that does not exist.

Fix. Build without NDEBUG, or pass -DENGINE_LOG_MIN_PRIORITY=1. The start-up line prints the floor: --log platform=info and read log floor.

3 · One problem, five log lines

Symptom. A missing texture produces a wall of "failed" messages from every layer.

Cause. §3.1.1's rule broken — each layer logged its own error on the way up.

Fix. Only the deepest point logs; callers propagate silently and add the consequence if they have one. When reviewing, the question is "does the caller's line say something the callee's could not?"

4 · An assertion stops firing partway through a session

Symptom. It trips once, you continue, and it never mentions it again even though the condition is still false.

Cause. The handler returned SDL_ASSERTION_ALWAYS_IGNORE — possibly you, in a dialog. It latches in a static inside that one macro expansion (§3.3).

Fix. Expected behaviour, not a bug. SDL_GetAssertionReport() still lists it with a trigger_count, which is often the number you actually wanted.

5 · Code inside an assertion stops running in release

Symptom. Works at -O0, misbehaves at -O2, and the difference is a line inside ENGINE_ASSERT.

Cause. ENGINE_ASSERT's condition is compiled but not evaluated when disabled — SDL wraps it in sizeof. And it disables at -O2 alone, not at -DNDEBUG (§3.3.1).

Fix. ENGINE_VERIFY, which guarantees evaluation. This is exactly the bug §4.5 shipped and then measured its way out of.

6 · --log silently does nothing

Symptom. The flag is accepted, no error, no change.

Cause. Something else parsed argv and your program never passed it to the config — app_config::argv is null, so platform::start has nothing to scan.

Fix. Set .argc / .argv in the config, or call engine::configure_logging(argc, argv) yourself. Under ENGINE_MAIN it is filled in for you.

7 · The log file exists but is empty

Symptom. set_log_file returned true and the file has zero bytes.

Cause. Almost always the level, not the file. SDL filters before the output hook, so a message below its category's threshold never reaches your sink at all.

Fix. Raise the level as well as opening the file — they are independent controls. verify_53 §E pins this behaviour deliberately.

9Exercises

  1. 9.1 — Find the level that is wrong. Run ./build/demos/sandbox --log '*=trace' 2>&1 | head -40 and read every line against §3.1's meanings. At least one is at the wrong level. Find it, argue why, and change it.

    Hint

    Ask the mechanical question, not the aesthetic one: did the operation happen? Look especially at messages that describe a fallback or a default being used.

    Solution sketch

    There is more than one defensible answer, which is the point — a level scheme is only worth having if disagreements about it are decidable. The clearest case is gpu_scene: depth target … at info: it fires once per resize as well as at start-up, which makes it closer to "what I did" than "what I am". Whatever you pick, write the reason next to the change; a level with no stated reason is the thing this lesson set out to remove.

  2. 9.2 — A category for your game. Add a sixth category — but for the demo, not the engine. Give pong its own PONG_LOG_GAMEPLAY based at engine::log_category_count, log a line when a rally ends, and confirm you can silence the engine while keeping it with --log '*=quiet'.

    Hint

    enum { pong_gameplay = engine::log_category_count }; and then SDL_LogInfo(pong_gameplay, …). Note what you will discover about --log '*=quiet'.

    Solution sketch

    It works, and the interesting part is why *=quiet does not silence it: our * deliberately means the five engine categories, not "everything" (§4.3). So a game's own categories are outside the engine's filter, which is correct — the engine has no business silencing the application — and it is also a limitation worth noticing. A real engine exposes a registration call so a game's categories can join the table. That is a good next thing to build, and it is why log_category_count is public.

  3. 9.3 — Prove the compile-time floor. Reproduce Figure 6's measurement yourself, then extend it: add a function containing ENGINE_LOG_TRACE inside a loop of a million iterations, and measure it at -O2 with and without -DNDEBUG. How much does a filtered-out trace call cost when it is compiled in?

    Hint

    scratch/measure_53.py's probe_sizes() is the harness; add a function to PROBE. For the runtime cost, time the loop rather than measuring its size — the interesting number is nanoseconds per suppressed call, and Lesson 3.10's profiler already knows how to measure that honestly.

    Solution sketch

    A suppressed SDL_LogTrace is a function call plus a table lookup plus a comparison — small, and not zero. Multiply by a million and it is a measurable slice of a frame, which is the entire justification for the compile-time floor existing at all. Note the honest conclusion this leads to: the floor matters for trace, is arguable for debug, and would be actively wrong for error.

  4. 9.4 — Assert something real. framebuffer::row(int y) currently trusts its caller. Add an ENGINE_ASSERT that y is in range, then write a harness section — modelled on verify_53 §F — that installs a counting handler and proves it fires for y = -1 and does not for y = 0.

    Hint

    SDL_SetAssertionHandler with a handler returning SDL_ASSERTION_IGNORE — not ALWAYS_IGNORE, or your second call will not be counted (§3.3).

    Solution sketch

    The mechanics are twenty lines. The interesting part is deciding whether it should be ENGINE_ASSERT or ENGINE_CHECK: row() is called once per scanline, which is 180 times a frame — cheap enough for either — but it returns a pointer that the caller then writes through, so a bad y corrupts memory rather than producing a wrong picture. That is the argument for ENGINE_CHECK, and it is the shape of argument §3.3's "only where continuing is worse than stopping" is asking for.

  5. 9.5 — The engine challenge: build the thing we declined. Write engine::result<T> — a value or an error, in one return, in the shape of C++23's std::expected. Then convert load_image to return result<image_data> and argue, in a comment, whether the engine should adopt it.

    Hint

    A union-like storage plus a discriminant, [[nodiscard]] on the type itself, operator bool, value() and error(). Keep the error payload allocation-free — a fixed char buffer, not a std::string — and notice what that forces you to decide about truncation.

    Solution sketch

    About 120 lines for a usable subset. What you will find: the type is pleasant at the call site and the conversion is mechanical, and then you hit the two real questions. What is E? One engine-wide enum makes the type simple and the errors vague; a per-subsystem enum makes result a template on two parameters and viral through every signature. And where does the diagnostic detail go? — because obj_report's twelve statistics are useful on success too, and expected has nowhere to put them.

    That second point is the honest answer to the whole exercise, and it is why the engine's convergent shape is a report rather than an expected: our loaders return facts, not just values, and they return them whether or not they worked. A defensible conclusion either way — but write down which problem your codebase actually has before adopting a type for the other one.

10Recap & Next

The engine gained:

  • Two axes instead of none. Five categories based at SDL_LOG_CATEGORY_CUSTOM, six levels with written meanings, and a --log flag that works on every program built on the engine.
  • Silence by default, for free — because SDL's own default table already says *=error and our categories are not SDL's.
  • A rule for who logs a failure, and the retirement of "returns false and has already logged" as an unwritten contract.
  • Three assertion macros whose survival across build configurations is measured rather than assumed — including one whose first version was wrong.
  • image_report, making the third instance of the shape the engine had already converged on twice.
The through-line

A codebase that has solved the same problem twice has told you something. Look for the pattern you already have before designing the one you do not. The satisfying version of this lesson invents result<T> and converts 61 functions to it. The useful version notices that obj_report and gpu_report were written a module apart, by nobody trying to match the other, and arrived at the same shape — and that the honest work is naming it and fixing the one case that was worse.

Next: Lesson 5.4 asks a question that has been quietly accumulating since Module 3. Every resource in this engine is handed around as a reference or a raw pointer — mesh_data, texture, gpu_texture, the objects in a scene. That works because everything is a local of main and nothing is ever destroyed. The moment an asset system can unload something, every pointer to it becomes a question nobody can answer: is this still valid?

The answer engines reach for is a handle — an index plus a generation counter — and it is one of those designs that looks like an indirection tax until you see what it buys: stale references become detectable instead of undefined, storage becomes relocatable so a pool can compact, and serialization becomes trivial because a handle is just two integers. It is also the last piece of scaffolding before the ECS, which is four lessons of Module 5 and is built entirely out of them.

11Further Reading

  • SDL3, SDL_log.h and src/SDL_log.c — the primary source for §3.2. Read the SDL_LogCategory enum down to SDL_LOG_CATEGORY_CUSTOM, then find the default-priority switch in the .c file. It is thirty lines and it decided this lesson's design.
  • SDL3, SDL_assert.h — read SDL_enabled_assert and SDL_disabled_assert in full. They are short, and the while loop and the sizeof are both load-bearing in ways the documentation does not emphasise.
  • SDL3, SDL_HINT_LOGGING in SDL_hints.h — the documented default table, and the grammar we borrowed for --log.
  • John Lakos, Large-Scale C++, vol. 1 §6 — on defensive programming and the distinction between contract violations and runtime errors. Lakos is more formal than we are and worth reading for the vocabulary: narrow contracts, and what a precondition actually promises.
  • Andrei Alexandrescu, "Systematic Error Handling in C++" (2012) — the talk that popularised the expected-style return in C++, and still the clearest account of why an error is a value. Watch it before doing Exercise 9.5.
  • P0709, "Zero-overhead deterministic exceptions" (Herb Sutter) — the proposal that explains why engine cores turn exceptions off, in more detail and more sympathetically than the usual folklore. Section 1 is the honest cost accounting.
  • Jason Gregory, Game Engine Architecture, 3rd ed., §3.2 — on assertions in shipping games, including the case for keeping some. The discussion of what a console certification process does to your assertion policy is the practical version of §3.3.1.
  • Bruce Dawson, "Zombie Processes Are Eating Your Memory" and the rest of Random ASCII — not about logging as such, but the best working example in the field of what a person can diagnose from a log they did not produce, which is the audience §2.1 asks you to write for.
