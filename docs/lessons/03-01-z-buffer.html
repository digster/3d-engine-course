The Painter’s Problem and the Z-Buffer

Module 2 built every transform that puts a triangle on the screen. It never asked which triangle you should actually see. This lesson shows why the obvious answer — sort them and paint back to front — cannot be made to work, derives the one that can, and turns the wireframe into a solid.

1The Problem

1.1 The solid you can see through

Lesson 2.12 ended with an icosahedron spinning in perspective, and with an honest list of what was still wrong with it. Top of that list: you can see straight through it. The edges on the far side of the solid are drawn over the edges on the near side, not because anything is transparent, but because nothing in the renderer has ever compared two surfaces and decided which one you are looking at.

We papered over it. draw_mesh dimmed distant edges so your eye could guess at the shape — a cue, and we said so at the time. Fill those triangles in instead of outlining them and the cue stops helping: whichever triangle happens to be drawn last simply wins, and the back of the solid paints over the front. The picture is not merely ugly, it is wrong, and it is wrong in a way that changes every time the object turns.

The failure mode

Draw filled triangles in index order and the image depends on the order the triangles happen to appear in the array. Rotate the mesh and the picture flickers between interpretations, because the array order never changes but which faces are in front does. There is no amount of cleverness in the fill routine that can fix this: the fill does not know what else is on screen.

1.2 The painter’s algorithm, and why it looks like the answer

The obvious fix is the one a painter uses. Paint the background first, then the middle distance, then the foreground; each layer covers what is behind it. In code: compute a depth for every triangle, sort back to front, draw in that order. This is the painter’s algorithm, and Exercise 2.12.5 asked you to try it precisely so that this lesson could take it away from you.

It is not a straw man. It was the standard technique in the early 1970s, it is what Newell, Newell and Sancha refined in 1972, and its descendant — BSP-tree ordering — is how Doom and Quake resolved visibility. It also still has a job today, which we come back to at the end of the lesson. And on our milestone scene it appears to work perfectly: sort the icosahedron’s twenty triangles by average view-space depth, draw them furthest first, and a convincing solid appears.

So what depth do you sort by? A triangle has three vertices at three different depths. The usual answer is the average of the three — the centroid’s depth. Hold on to that, because it is the first of the three cracks.

1.3 Three failures, none of them fixable

Failure one: a triangle does not have a depth. A long triangle running away from the camera spans a range of depths. Its average describes none of them. Two such triangles can each be in front of the other — over different parts of the screen — and any single number you compute for each is a summary that has thrown away the fact you needed.

You can attack this by splitting long triangles into shorter ones. That trades a correctness problem for an unbounded amount of geometry, and it does not touch the next two failures at all.

Failure two: the correct order can be a cycle. Take three flat panels and weave them, exactly as you would weave three sticks: each one passes over the next and under the one before. Figure 1 shows the arrangement. Panel A is in front of B at the corner they share; B is in front of C at theirs; C is in front of A at theirs. Now put those three panels in an order.

You cannot. “In front of” here is not a transitive relation, and a sort assumes it is. Any sorting algorithm you write — by centroid, by nearest vertex, by farthest vertex, by anything — produces some order, and at least one of the three overlaps will be painted the wrong way round.

Failure three: two triangles can pass through each other. This is the deepest of the three, and the one that ends the argument. When two triangles intersect, the correct answer changes halfway across one of them. On the left of the intersection line A is nearer; on the right, B is. Figure 2 shows it from above.

Read those three failures together and they say the same thing three ways. Visibility is a per-pixel question, and sorting answers a per-object one. Every repair to the painter’s algorithm — splitting long triangles, detecting cycles and splitting to break them, splitting at intersections — is the same repair: cut the geometry up until each piece is small enough that the per-object answer happens to be the per-pixel answer. Newell’s algorithm does exactly that, and it is expensive, fiddly, and produces a triangle count you cannot predict.

There is also a practical objection that has nothing to do with correctness. A sort is global: every triangle must be gathered up and compared against every other before anything can be drawn. That is O(n log n) per frame, it grows with the scene, it cannot start until the last triangle has been transformed, and it is exactly the shape of work a GPU — which wants to chew through triangles independently and in any order — is worst at.

So we want something that decides visibility per pixel, needs no ordering, and lets each triangle be drawn without knowing the others exist. Remarkably, that thing is one float per pixel.

2Building Intuition

Forget triangles for a moment and think about a single pixel. That pixel is a tiny window onto the scene, and out through it goes a ray from the eye. Everything the scene contains along that ray is a candidate for what you see; what you actually see is whichever candidate the ray reaches first.

That is the whole idea, and notice what it does not require. It does not require knowing the other pixels. It does not require knowing how many candidates there will be. It does not require them to arrive in any particular order. If you are told about candidates one at a time and you keep a note of “the nearest one so far”, then after the last one your note holds the right answer — and it held the right answer for the candidates seen so far at every point along the way.

Keeping a running minimum is the oldest trick in programming, and it is order-independent by construction. The z-buffer is that trick, run a hundred thousand times in parallel — once per pixel. Alongside the colour buffer we keep a second buffer, the same size, holding one number per pixel: how far away is the thing whose colour is currently sitting there. Figure 3 is the picture to carry around.

We are not going to trace rays; we are going to keep rasterizing triangles, exactly as Lesson 2.2 taught. But the accounting is the same. When a triangle covers a pixel, it is announcing a candidate. We work out how far away that triangle is at that pixel, compare it with the note, and keep the nearer one.

Where this comes from

Ed Catmull described the z-buffer in his 1974 doctoral thesis at Utah, and Wolfgang Straßer arrived at the same idea independently in his thesis the same year. At the time it was considered extravagant — a full extra buffer of memory, when memory was the most expensive thing in the building. Catmull himself noted the cost. Fifty years later every GPU on earth has one in hardware, with dedicated compression and early-rejection circuitry built around it. It is the clearest example in graphics of a brute-force answer winning because it is order-independent, and therefore parallel.

3The Theory

3.1 The algorithm, stated

In full, and it really is this short:

Three properties are worth naming out loud, because each one is a thing the painter’s algorithm cannot do.

It is order-independent. Swap any two triangles in the loop and the final image is identical, because min does not care about order. That is what makes it safe to hand triangles to a GPU in whatever order they finish being transformed — and, in Module 8, safe to rasterize them on several threads.

It is local. A triangle needs to know nothing about any other triangle. There is no gathering phase, no global sort, no O(n log n). Cost grows with the number of pixels covered, not with the square or the log of the triangle count.

It handles all three failures without noticing them. Intersecting triangles? Each pixel is decided on its own, so the intersection line appears for free — no one ever computes it. Cycles? There is no ordering to be cyclic. Long triangles? Depth is evaluated per pixel, so nothing is ever averaged away.

3.2 What to clear it to

Our depth convention is fixed and comes from SDL_GPU (Conventions §4): device depth runs 0 at the near plane to 1 at the far plane, and larger means further away. So “nothing has been drawn here yet” must be represented by the largest possible depth, 1.0, so that the first triangle to arrive beats it.

Clear to zero and you get a black screen

Zero is the near plane — the closest anything can possibly be. A depth buffer full of zeros is a claim that the entire screen is already covered by something pressed against the camera lens, and every triangle you submit loses its test. Not “most”: every single one. The symptom is a perfectly clean background and no geometry at all, which reads like a broken transform rather than a broken clear, and sends people looking in entirely the wrong file. Exercise 3.1.1 asks you to produce it deliberately, once, so you never have to diagnose it slowly.

And the clear must happen every frame, before anything is drawn. Skip it and last frame’s depths survive into this one, so this frame’s geometry is tested against surfaces that are no longer on screen. The image does not break all at once — it accumulates holes, as though the scene were slowly eating itself.

3.3 Which depth do we store?

Here is the question that makes this lesson more than an afternoon’s coding, and it has a genuinely surprising answer.

We have two plausible candidates for “how far away is this pixel”. There is view-space z — a real distance in world units, negative in front of the camera, the thing your intuition means by depth. And there is device depth — the [0,1] value that comes out of the projection matrix and the perspective divide, which Lesson 2.11’s viewport transform has been computing and throwing away for two lessons.

View-space z looks like the obvious choice. It is a physical quantity, it is what a human means by depth, and the numbers are readable. It is also wrong, and not by a little.

The reason is not about depth at all — it is about interpolation. We do not have the depth at every pixel; we have it at the three corners, and we get the interior by barycentric interpolation, which is the machinery Lesson 2.3 built and 2.4 generalised. Barycentric interpolation computes the unique affine function of the pixel position that agrees with the three corner values. If the quantity you are interpolating genuinely is an affine function of the pixel position, the answer is exact. If it is not, the answer is a straight line drawn under a curve, and the gap between them is your error.

So the question “which depth” becomes a precise one: which of these two quantities is an affine function of the position on the screen? The next section answers it, and the answer is the one that looks less physical.

3.4 Deriving it: device depth is affine in screen space

A triangle is flat. In view space its three vertices lie in a plane, and every point of its interior lies in that plane too. Write the plane in the usual way, with a normal n and a constant d:

\[ n_x x_v + n_y y_v + n_z z_v = d \] n.x * x_view + n.y * y_view + n.z * z_view = d

Now bring in the projection from Lesson 2.10. Writing w for -z_view — the positive distance in front of the camera, which is exactly what the projection matrix copies into the fourth component — the two screen coordinates and the device depth are

\[ x_n = \frac{f}{a}\cdot\frac{x_v}{w}, \qquad y_n = f\cdot\frac{y_v}{w}, \qquad z_n = \frac{A z_v + B}{w} \] x_ndc = (f/aspect) * x_view / w, y_ndc = f * y_view / w, z_ndc = (A*z_view + B) / w

with f = cot(fovy/2) and the depth-row constants A = far/(near − far), B = far·near/(near − far), both derived in Lesson 2.10 §3.5. Everything below is bookkeeping on those three lines.

Step one: rearrange the projection. Solve the first two for the view-space coordinates, and note that z_v = -w by definition:

\[ x_v = \frac{a}{f}\,x_n\,w, \qquad y_v = \frac{1}{f}\,y_n\,w, \qquad z_v = -w \] x_view = (aspect/f) * x_ndc * w, y_view = (1/f) * y_ndc * w, z_view = -w

Step two: substitute into the plane equation. Every term now carries a factor of w, so w comes straight out:

\[ w\left[\frac{a\,n_x}{f}x_n + \frac{n_y}{f}y_n - n_z\right] = d \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \frac{1}{w} = \frac{1}{d}\left[\frac{a\,n_x}{f}x_n + \frac{n_y}{f}y_n - n_z\right] \] w * [ (aspect*n.x/f)*x_ndc + (n.y/f)*y_ndc - n.z ] = d, so 1/w = (1/d) * [ same bracket ]

Look hard at the right-hand side. It is a constant, plus a constant times x_ndc, plus a constant times y_ndc. That is the definition of an affine function of the screen position. So:

The key fact

Over any flat triangle, 1/w varies affinely across the screen. Not approximately — exactly. This one sentence is the foundation of both this lesson and the next: Lesson 3.2 uses it to build perspective-correct interpolation for every attribute a vertex carries.

Step three: express device depth in terms of 1/w. Substitute z_v = -w into the depth row:

\[ z_n = \frac{A(-w) + B}{w} = -A + B\cdot\frac{1}{w} \] z_ndc = (A*(-w) + B)/w = -A + B*(1/w)

Device depth is an affine function of 1/w, and 1/w is an affine function of the screen position. An affine function of an affine function is affine. Therefore:

The result

Device depth varies affinely across the screen. And barycentric interpolation computes precisely the affine function that agrees with three given values at three given points (Lesson 2.3 §3.7). So interpolating z_ndc from the three corners with the ordinary barycentric weights is not an approximation that happens to be good enough — it is the exact answer.

Two footnotes on the derivation, both worth having.

The viewport transform does not break it. What we actually store is the third output of viewport::to_screen, which is min_depth + z_ndc * (max_depth - min_depth) — an affine function of z_ndc, hence still affine in the screen position. Interpolating the stored value is equally exact.

The one degenerate case is not a case. The derivation divides by d, which is zero exactly when the triangle’s plane passes through the eye. Such a triangle is edge-on: it projects to a line segment, has zero screen area, and is rejected by fill_triangle’s area == 0 test before any of this runs. There is no interior to interpolate over, so there is nothing to be wrong about.

And now the control: why view-space z fails

Run the same argument on z_v. We have z_v = -w, and from step two 1/w is affine, so

\[ z_v \;=\; -w \;=\; \frac{-1}{\text{(affine in }x_n, y_n)} \] z_view = -w = -1 / (an affine function of the screen position)

A reciprocal of an affine function is a hyperbola, not a line. Interpolating z_v linearly across the screen draws a chord where the truth is a curve, and near the far end of a deep triangle that chord is nowhere near the curve.

There is one exception, and it is the reason this bug is so good at hiding. If the triangle’s plane is parallel to the screen — n_x = n_y = 0 — then the bracket in step two is constant, 1/w is constant, and z_v is constant too. A flat wall facing the camera interpolates perfectly whichever depth you use. So a renderer with this bug looks completely correct on a test scene of boxes and floors, and falls apart on the first steeply-angled surface. If you ever find yourself writing “it works on my test scene”, this is the family of bug that sentence belongs to.

Measured, not asserted

scratch/verify_31.cpp generates 200,000 random triangles in the demo’s frustum, picks a random interior point in screen space, computes the true surface point by intersecting the eye ray with the triangle’s plane, and compares it against both interpolations. Over 199,273 well-conditioned samples:

  • worst error interpolating device depth: 8.2 × 10−13 — double-precision noise, i.e. zero;
  • worst error interpolating view-space z: 5.73 units, which is 243.6% of the true depth at that pixel.

3.5 A worked example, by hand

Abstractions deserve numbers. Take the projection from Lesson 2.10’s worked example — near = 1, far = 100 — and pick a horizontal field of view of 90° so that f / aspect = 1 and the arithmetic stays readable.

First the depth-row constants:

The constants
  • A = far / (near − far) = 100 / (1 − 100) = −100/99 = −1.0101010101
  • B = far × near / (near − far) = 100 / (−99) = −1.0101010101

Check the two anchors, using z_n = −A + B/w:

  • w = 1 (the near plane): 1.0101010101 − 1.0101010101 = 0 ✓
  • w = 100 (the far plane): 1.0101010101 − 0.0101010101 = 1 ✓
  • w = 2 (Lesson 2.10’s number): 1.0101010101 − 0.5050505050 = 0.5050505 ✓

Now an edge that runs away from the camera. Put both ends at x_view = 1, y_view = 0, one at z_view = −1 (on the near plane) and one at z_view = −100 (on the far plane). Because f/aspect = 1 and x_view = 1 along the whole edge, its screen x is simply 1/w:

The two endpoints
  • near end: w = 1, so x_ndc = 1.00 and z_ndc = 0
  • far end: w = 100, so x_ndc = 0.01 and z_ndc = 1

The screen midpoint is halfway between those two screen positions: x_ndc = (1.00 + 0.01) / 2 = 0.505. Which point of the edge actually lands there? Since x_ndc = 1/w, it is the point with w = 1 / 0.505 = 1.9801980, i.e. z_view = −1.98.

That is the whole story in one number. The pixel halfway across the screen is not halfway along the edge in depth — it is 1.98 units away out of a span of 99.

Now interpolate, both ways, and compare against the truth.

Device depth — exact

Linear interpolation of the endpoint values at the screen midpoint: 0.5 × 0 + 0.5 × 1 = 0.500000.

The true device depth at w = 1.9801980: −A + B/w = 1.0101010 − 1.0101010/1.9801980 = 1.0101010 − 0.5101010 = 0.500000.

They agree exactly. Not to four places — exactly, as the derivation promised.

View-space depth — wrong by a factor of 25

Linear interpolation of the endpoint values at the same pixel: 0.5 × (−1) + 0.5 × (−100) = −50.5.

The truth, from above: −1.98.

The naive answer places that pixel 25.5× further away than it is. Put a second surface at 10 units and the whole edge would vanish behind it, when in reality most of the edge is in front.

Figure 4 is that comparison plotted across the whole edge rather than at one point.

One more consequence worth noticing before we move on, because it is the seed of Lesson 3.2. The right-hand plot is what happens to any quantity that varies linearly along the surface: a texture coordinate, a vertex colour, a normal. Depth is special precisely because the projection has already put it into its screen-affine form. Everything else a vertex carries is still on the wrong side of that curve, and straightening it out is exactly what 3.2 is about.

3.6 Precision, and z-fighting

The z-buffer is exact in the sense that matters — the comparison it makes is the right comparison, at the right place, for any geometry. What it is not is infinitely precise, and the way its precision is distributed is deeply lopsided. This is where z-fighting comes from, and it is the one part of this lesson you will still be using in ten years.

Start from the relation we already derived, z_n = −A + B/w. Differentiate it to ask “how much real distance does one step of depth buy me?”

\[ \frac{dz_n}{dw} = -\frac{B}{w^2} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \Delta w = \Delta z_n \cdot \frac{w^2}{-B} = \Delta z_n \cdot w^2\left(\frac{1}{near}-\frac{1}{far}\right) \] dz_ndc/dw = -B/w² so Δw = Δz_ndc * w² * (1/near - 1/far)

Read that formula slowly; it contains three separate practical facts.

It is quadratic in distance. Twice as far away means four times coarser depth. Nothing else in a renderer degrades that fast.

Your near plane is the expensive one. The bracket is 1/near − 1/far, and for any sane scene 1/near dominates it completely. Halving near roughly halves your depth precision at every distance, everywhere in the scene.

Your far plane is nearly free. With near = 1, moving far from 100 to 1000 changes the bracket from 0.99 to 0.999 — nine tenths of one percent worse. Moving near from 1 to 0.1 changes it from 0.99 to 9.99: 10.09× worse. If you take one practical rule from this lesson, take this one: push your near plane out as far as your game can bear, and stop worrying about the far plane.

Figure 5 makes the lopsidedness visible. It takes eleven evenly spaced depth values — 0.0, 0.1, … 1.0 — and asks where each one lands in the world.

Now put a real format on it. A GPU depth attachment is a texture with a format, and SDL_GPU offers SDL_GPU_TEXTUREFORMAT_D16_UNORM, D24_UNORM and D32_FLOAT among others. A 16-bit unorm buffer has 65,535 evenly spaced codes between 0 and 1, so Δz_n = 1/65535, and the formula above turns that into real units:

Two surfaces closer together than one code store the same number. The comparison z < stored then fails, the second surface loses, and which one you see depends entirely on which was drawn first. As the camera moves, the rounding tips back and forth from pixel to pixel, and you get the shimmering interleaved mess everyone recognises on sight: z-fighting.

Reproduce it on demand

The demo’s fourth scene is two large panels one millimetre apart. Press C until you reach it and B to change the depth format. At D32_FLOAT and D24_UNORM the near panel wins every one of the 875 covered pixels. At D16_UNORM it loses 478 of them — 54.6%, in bands that crawl as you orbit. The arithmetic predicts it: at that distance one 16-bit code spans 0.00208 units and the gap is 0.001, so the two panels round to the same code slightly less than half the time. What professionals do about it

Three things, in order of how often they are the answer. Move the near plane out — free, and usually enough. Use a 24- or 32-bit depth format — costs bandwidth, and note from the table that D24 is roughly 256× finer than D16 everywhere. And reversed-Z: map near to 1 and far to 0, clear to 0, and test with >. That sounds like it should change nothing, and with a floating-point depth buffer it changes everything — float has its own precision crowded near zero, which cancels almost exactly against 1/w’s crowding near the near plane. It is close to free and it is what modern engines do. Exercise 3.1.4 has you build it.

3.7 What it costs

Memory: one float per pixel. At our 320×180 framebuffer that is 230 KB; at 1920×1080 with 32-bit depth it is 8.3 MB. That was an outrageous price in 1974 and is a rounding error now, which is the entire reason the technique won.

Time: per covered pixel, one multiply-add-multiply-add to interpolate the depth, one load, one compare, and a conditional store. Compare that against the painter’s algorithm’s per-frame sort of the whole scene and the z-buffer is not merely more correct, it is usually faster too.

And there is a bonus hiding in the code we are about to write. Look at the order of operations: the depth test runs before the colour is computed, so a pixel that loses never pays for its colour at all. With flat colours that saves almost nothing. With Module 6’s PBR shading it saves an enormous amount, and it is why hardware has a dedicated early-Z stage that rejects fragments before the fragment shader ever runs — and why drawing roughly front-to-back, even though the z-buffer does not require any order, is one of the cheapest optimisations in real-time rendering. Exercise 3.1.3 measures it.

4Implementation

Four pieces: a new type to hold the depths, a new field on vertex, four lines in the rasterizer’s inner loop, and a demo that can show you both algorithms side by side. We build them in that order.

4.1 Where the depth buffer lives — and where it does not

The obvious move is to bolt a std::vector<float> onto framebuffer and be done. Resist it, for three reasons, the third of which settles the matter.

Not every framebuffer wants depth. Pong, the 2-D triangle demos, the HUD overlay, and every post-processing intermediate Module 6 will introduce are colour-only. Fusing the two types makes each of them allocate a depth buffer it never reads — 8 MB of nothing at 1080p.

They are different formats. Colour is four 8-bit channels packed into a Uint32; depth is a single value with its own precision story. Their clear values are unrelated: a background colour, and far.

The hardware keeps them separate, and this is the one that decides it. Here is SDL_GPU’s render-pass entry point, from SDL3/SDL_gpu.h:

Colour targets are an array; the depth-stencil target is a separate, nullable parameter. They are two independent attachments with their own load and store operations. If we model that split now, Module 4 is a rename rather than a redesign — and we get the right answer for the right reason today as well.

So depth_buffer is its own type, deliberately shaped as a twin of framebuffer: same constructor arguments, same index = y * width + x, same clear, same clamped row() escape hatch for inner loops. If you can read one you can read the other.

Notice what is not there: a test_and_set. It is tempting — it would name the algorithm in one method — and it is the wrong place for it. On real hardware the comparison is pipeline state, not storage:

Three independent knobs, and different passes set them differently: a shadow pass writes depth and no colour; a transparent pass tests depth and does not write it. Baking “less-than, always write” into the buffer would make those unexpressible. The buffer stores and clears; the rasterizer compares.

The one thing depth_buffer does carry that framebuffer does not is a format, because §3.6 is not an aside — it is a decision the type should be able to express:

What we model, and what we do not

Storage stays float in every case; we round to the format’s grid on write. The behaviour — which is what z-fighting is — is therefore exactly right, while the memory saving of a genuine 16-bit buffer is the part we are not modelling. That is the ninety-percent picture: the missing ten percent is bandwidth, and it starts to matter in Module 4 where the depth attachment is a real GPU texture with a real format.

One detail that is not a simplification: 2^24 − 1 = 16777215 and 2^16 − 1 = 65535 are both exactly representable in a float, so round(z * codes) / codes lands on a genuine grid point rather than near one.

4.2 A vertex learns its depth

Lesson 2.4 built vertex and predicted its future in a comment: “Module 3 adds depth (z), texture coordinates and normals as the lessons that need them arrive — each one three more lines in the same loop.” Time to collect on the first of those.

Putting z inside vertex rather than passing three loose floats is the same argument Lesson 2.4 §4.2 made about colour, and it now pays a second time. fill_triangle reorients a backwards-wound triangle by swapping two vertices; every attribute must move with the position it belongs to. Because depth lives in the struct, the existing std::swap(v1, v2) carries it automatically. Had we passed z0, z1, z2 alongside, there would now be a fourth thing to remember to swap — and forgetting it produces a triangle with perfect geometry and inverted depth, which is a genuinely horrible bug to look at.

The compiler catches the call sites, and that is by design

Inserting z before colour breaks every brace-initialised vertex in the codebase — and breaks them loudly: 

error: constant expression evaluates to 4278190335 which cannot be
       narrowed to type 'float' [-Wc++11-narrowing]
   engine::vertex{vx[2], vy[2], engine::pack_argb(0, 0, 255)},

A Uint32 cannot silently become a float in list-initialisation, so the old three-argument form is a compile error rather than a colour quietly landing in the depth field. That is aggregate initialisation earning its keep. Had vertex had a constructor taking (int, int, Uint32), the same edit would have compiled and produced garbage.

4.3 Four lines in the inner loop

Now the rasterizer. The depth-tested fill is not a new function — it is the Gouraud fill from Lesson 2.4 with an attachment slot:

C++ in place — when a raw pointer is the right tool

This course has said since Lesson 1.5 that the engine core owns nothing through raw pointers. That rule is about ownership, and this parameter owns nothing. What a raw pointer gives us here that a reference cannot is optionality: a reference must refer to something, and “there is no depth attachment” is a state we need to express.

The alternatives were two overloads with duplicated loops (rejected — the fill’s set-up is subtle, and raster.cpp already argues that duplicating something subtle is how one bias ends up wrong in one of three places), or std::optional<std::reference_wrapper<depth_buffer>> (rejected — it is a mouthful that compiles to the same pointer). A non-owning nullable pointer is the honest spelling, and it is exactly what SDL_BeginGPURenderPass uses for the same idea.

Inside, one hoist and one test. The depth row comes out of the loop for the same reason the colour row does — the row index does not change across a scanline:

and then, per pixel, the whole of the z-buffer:

Five things are decided in those twelve lines, and none of them is arbitrary.

The weights are f0, f1, f2 — the unbiased ones. They are the same three numbers the colour interpolation uses, computed by subtracting the top-left rule’s bias back out (Lesson 2.4 §3.5). Leaving the bias in would shift the entire depth field by a fraction of a pixel and break the sum-to-one identity. It is exactly the mistake 2.4 warned about, made on a new attribute, and it would be far harder to spot here: a colour field displaced by a twentieth of a pixel is invisible, and a depth field displaced by the same amount produces a thin seam of wrong occlusion where two triangles meet.

We interpolate v.z, the device depth. Because of §3.4 this is exact and not an approximation. Three multiply-adds — the same three lines as a colour channel — is genuinely all it takes, and that is the payoff for having done the derivation.

Quantise, then compare. That is the order the hardware uses: the incoming fragment’s depth is converted to the attachment’s format and then tested against a value already in that format. Compare first and round afterwards and two surfaces that store identically would still order themselves, which is precisely the flicker we want to be able to reproduce on demand.

<, not <=. On an exact tie the pixel already there keeps it. This matters for coplanar geometry: with < the first triangle drawn wins every tie and the result is stable; with <= the last one does, and any surface that gets drawn twice flickers. It is the same choice as SDL_GPU_COMPAREOP_LESS, which is what essentially every renderer defaults to.

The colour is computed after the test. A pixel that loses never pays for its blend. That is early-Z, in miniature — see §3.7.

4.4 The demo: two algorithms, one scene, and a number

An engine that can only show you the right answer teaches half as much. The demo runs both strategies over identical geometry every frame and counts the pixels they disagree about.

First, the whole scene is projected once into one flat list. That flatness is not incidental: a painter’s algorithm has to sort across objects, because sorting each object’s triangles separately is wrong the moment two objects overlap.

The ground grid is drawn first, as plain lines, with no depth interaction at all. That is not laziness — it is the painter’s algorithm surviving as a legitimate special case. (Giving lines a real depth test is Exercise 3.1.2.)

Finally, the measurement. After drawing the scene in the selected mode, the demo renders the other mode into a scratch framebuffer over the same background and counts differing pixels inside the viewport rectangle. At 320×180 the second pass costs microseconds, and it turns “the painter’s algorithm is wrong” from a claim into a live number that moves as you orbit.

4.5 Building a scene that breaks it

The demo ships four scenes on the C key: the Module 2 solids, the cycle, the intersecting pair, and the near-coplanar pair. The cycle is the one worth reading the construction of, because it is Lesson 2.5’s claim used as a tool rather than admired.

A rotation matrix is its three columns, and its columns are where the basis vectors land (Lesson 2.5 §3.2). We want the quad’s own x axis to run across the plank, its y axis along it, and its z axis to be the face normal — so we build those three directions and hand them over as columns. No angle is computed anywhere, and no rotation_x/y/z is composed. The three are orthonormal by construction: axis_x lies in the ground plane and is perpendicular to the plank’s horizontal run, so it is perpendicular to axis_y whatever the tilt, and axis_z is their cross product. The harness checks the determinant is +1.000000 — a reflection here would flip every triangle’s winding and quietly matter in Lesson 3.4.

Three planks laid along the sides of an equilateral triangle, each tilted so that it ends +0.55 towards the camera at one end and −0.55 away at the other, give exactly the weave of Figure 1. The overhang lengthens each quad without moving its plane, so the depths at the two named corners are still exactly ±tilt — it exists only so the planks properly cross rather than merely touching, and a sliver of overlap is not a demonstration.

Verified, not asserted

scratch/verify_31_render.cpp builds the same transforms, reads the plank planes back out of the matrices, and reports:

at C2: plank A z = +0.5500   plank B z = -0.5500  -> A in front
at C3: plank B z = +0.5500   plank C z = -0.5500  -> B in front
at C1: plank C z = +0.5500   plank A z = -0.5500  -> C in front
plank A: width 1.0500, length 3.3516, axes at 90.000 deg
plank A basis determinant: +1.000000

A over B over C over A. And with the camera facing the weave, all three plank centres are equidistant from the eye, so their average depths are identical to the last bit — a renderer that sorts whole objects, as many do for transparency, has literally nothing to compare.

7Common Pitfalls & Debugging

Nothing draws at all — a clean background and no geometry

Cause. The depth buffer was cleared to 0 instead of 1. Zero is the near plane, so the buffer is claiming every pixel is already covered by something pressed against the lens, and every fragment loses its test.

Why it wastes an hour. The symptom looks exactly like a broken transform, so people go and check their matrices. The tell is that it is total — a broken matrix usually leaves something on screen, in the wrong place.

Fix. Clear to depth_buffer::k_far. Our constructor does it for you, which is why depth_.assign(…, k_far) rather than a default-initialised vector: the buffer cannot exist in the broken state.

The image slowly eats itself — holes appear as the camera moves

Cause. The depth buffer is not cleared every frame. Last frame’s depths survive, so this frame’s geometry is tested against surfaces that are no longer anywhere.

Symptom in detail. The first frame is perfect. Then, wherever something was previously close to the camera, new geometry cannot get in — so you get a growing stencil of holes shaped like the history of the scene. Stop moving and it stops getting worse, which misleads people into blaming the camera.

Fix. depth.clear() immediately next to fb.clear(), every frame, unconditionally. On the GPU this is the same idea spelled SDL_GPU_LOADOP_CLEAR in the depth target’s load_op.

Occlusion is right on walls and floors, wrong on anything steeply angled

Cause. You are interpolating view-space z instead of device depth. Section 3.4 shows why: view depth is a hyperbola in screen space, and interpolating it linearly draws a chord under a curve.

Why it hides. A surface parallel to the screen has constant depth, so both choices agree exactly. Test scenes are full of axis-aligned boxes and flat floors, which is precisely the family of geometry that cannot reveal the bug. It shows up on ramps, terrain and anything viewed at a glancing angle — and the error grows with how much depth the triangle spans, which is why it looks intermittent.

Fix. Store viewport::to_screen(ndc).z. If you want a linear depth for a debug view or a fog term, reconstruct it from the device value with w = B / (z_ndc + A) — do not interpolate it.

A one-pixel seam of wrong occlusion where two triangles meet

Cause. The depth was interpolated with the biased edge-function values — the ones carrying the top-left rule’s -1. That bias is a statement about who owns a boundary pixel, not about where the pixel is; leaving it in displaces the whole depth field by 1 / edge_length of a pixel and stops the three weights summing to one.

Why it is worse here than for colour. Lesson 2.4 met this bug on colour, where a sub-pixel shift is invisible. On depth, the same shift changes which of two adjacent triangles wins along their shared edge, and a visible seam appears in exactly the places your eye is drawn to.

Fix. Subtract the bias before dividing — (w0 - s.bias0) * inv_area — and use the same f0, f1, f2 for depth as for colour. If they are computed twice, that is the bug waiting to happen.

Two surfaces shimmer and interleave as the camera moves

Cause. Z-fighting: the two surfaces are closer together than one depth code at that distance, so they store the same number and the tie-break decides — differently from pixel to pixel as the rounding tips.

Diagnose it with arithmetic, not by staring. One code spans Δw = Δz · w² · (1/near − 1/far). Put in your numbers. If the answer is bigger than the gap between your surfaces, you have found it, and no amount of nudging geometry will help for long.

Fix, in order of what usually works. Push the near plane out — it is free and it is nearly always the real problem. Use a wider format. Move to reversed-Z with a float buffer (Exercise 3.1.4). Only then start biasing geometry, which is what shadow mapping will force us to do properly in Module 6.

The disagreement counter never reads zero, even on the scene where sorting is correct

This one is ours, and it is not a bug. On the solids scene the counter sits at a couple of dozen pixels rather than zero, in thin one-pixel runs along silhouettes.

Cause. We do not cull back faces yet, so a back face and a front face are both drawn along every silhouette edge — and along that shared edge they have exactly equal depth. fill_triangle reorients backwards-wound triangles so they still fill (Lesson 2.2), which means the shared edge is traversed in the same direction by both and the top-left rule lets both claim the boundary pixel. The painter gives it to whichever is drawn last (the front face); the z-buffer’s strict < gives it to whichever was drawn first (the back face). Neither is wrong — both triangles genuinely contain that point.

Verified. scratch/verify_31_render.cpp drops screen-space back-facing triangles and re-measures: with back faces culled: 6 of 12 tris kept, 0 px differ. The residue is entirely silhouette ties and nothing else.

Fix. Lesson 3.4. Back-face culling removes the offending triangles before they are ever rasterized, and it does so for a performance reason — the correctness improvement here is a side effect. Until then, read the counter as “a couple of dozen means agreement”.

8Exercises

3.1.1Break it on purposewarm-up

Before you run it, write down what you expect. Then change the depth clear from k_far to 0.0f and run. Then change the test from < to > (leaving the clear at k_far) and run again. Then set the clear to 0.5f.

Hint

For each case, ask one question: what does the buffer claim is already there, and can an incoming fragment beat it? That single question predicts all three.

Solution

Clear to 0: nothing draws. Zero is the near plane, so the buffer claims the whole screen is already occupied by the closest possible surface and every fragment loses.

Test with >: the scene turns inside out — you see the far side of every solid, because now the furthest surface wins each pixel. This is a genuinely useful mode, by the way: it is how you render the back faces of a volume for volumetric effects.

Clear to 0.5: everything nearer than the halfway depth draws normally and everything beyond it is cut away, as though a sheet of glass were sitting in the scene. Given §3.6 you can even say where it is: w = B / (0.5 + A), which for our near = 0.3, far = 100 puts the sheet about 0.6 units from the eye — so in practice nearly the whole scene vanishes. That is the crowding of Figure 5, felt rather than read.

3.1.2A depth-tested linemedium

The ground grid is drawn first and never depth-tested, so it is always behind everything. Give draw_line a depth-aware sibling that takes a start and end depth, interpolates between them along the line, and tests each pixel. Then draw the grid after the solids and confirm it is occluded correctly — and that grid lines in front of an object now draw over it.

Hint

Bresenham already walks the major axis one pixel at a time, so you know how many steps there will be before you start: steps = max(|dx|, |dy|). Interpolate the depth with a running add of (z1 - z0) / steps. Is that interpolation exact? Think about §3.4: a line is a degenerate triangle, and the same argument applies — device depth is affine along it in screen space.

Solution sketch

Yes, it is exact, for the same reason and with the same proof. The interesting part is what you see afterwards: hairline gaps where a line meets a surface it lies exactly on, because the line rasterizer and the triangle rasterizer disagree about which pixel centre a shared edge belongs to. That is the same class of problem as shadow-map acne, and the same family of fixes applies — a small depth bias on the line, or drawing lines with <=. Which is better, and why? There is no clean answer, which is itself worth knowing before Module 6 asks the question in earnest.

3.1.3Measure early-Zmedium

The z-buffer needs no order, but order still affects cost. Add a counter to fill_triangle for pixels that fail the depth test, and report it in the HUD. Then draw the scene sorted front to back (reverse the painter’s comparator) and compare the counts against back-to-front and against unsorted.

Hint

A file-scope counter reset each frame is fine for a measurement; do not ship it. Think about what you expect first: which order lets the buffer reject the most work, and why?

Solution sketch

Front-to-back rejects the most: the nearest surface arrives first and everything behind it fails immediately. Back-to-front rejects almost nothing — every fragment wins, so every one is shaded, and the buffer is written over and over. The images are identical; only the work differs. On our flat-coloured demo you will struggle to see this in the frame time, and that is an honest result worth recording: the saving scales with how expensive shading is, and shading is currently free. Come back to this exercise after Module 6 and the numbers will be dramatic. This is exactly why real engines sort roughly front-to-back for opaque geometry even though the z-buffer does not require any order at all.

3.1.4Reversed-Zhard

Map the near plane to depth 1 and the far plane to depth 0, clear to 0.0f, and test with >. Then measure the near-coplanar scene at D32_FLOAT and at D16_UNORM, both ways round. Predict the two results before you measure.

Hint

You do not need a new matrix function. Look at how perspective() derives A and B from near and far, and work out what happens if you pass those two arguments the other way round. Then check the two anchors by hand, as §3.5 does.

For the prediction: unorm codes are evenly spaced in [0,1]. Are float values?

Solution

perspective(fovy, aspect, far, near) — the two swapped — gives exactly A′ = near/(far − near) and B′ = far·near/(far − near), which map z_v = −near to 1 and z_v = −far to 0. Check both by hand before running anything.

With D16_UNORM, nothing changes. Unorm codes are evenly spaced, so reversing the mapping just relabels them; the precision at any given distance is identical. If you expected an improvement here, that is the misconception this exercise exists to remove.

With D32_FLOAT, the improvement is enormous. Floating point has its own precision distribution, crowded near zero: values just above 0 are spaced roughly 10−38 apart while values just below 1 are spaced 6 × 10−8 apart. Standard-Z puts the distant geometry — where 1/w crowding has already stolen your precision — up near 1, where float is at its coarsest, so the two effects compound. Reversed-Z puts it near 0 where float is at its finest, and the two effects very nearly cancel. This is why reversed-Z with a float depth buffer is close to standard practice in modern engines: it is almost free and it very nearly removes depth precision as a thing you have to think about.

Two things to watch: the clear value and the compare op must change together (clearing to 0 and testing with < reproduces Exercise 3.1.1’s black screen), and in Module 4 this becomes two fields — clear_depth on SDL_GPUDepthStencilTargetInfo, and compare_op on SDL_GPUDepthStencilState.

3.1.5Where the painter’s algorithm still livesopen

We have spent a lesson demolishing sorting. Now find the case where it is still mandatory. Add a partially transparent surface to the demo — blend the incoming colour with what is already in the framebuffer instead of replacing it — and try to make it look right using only the z-buffer. Then work out what you actually have to do.

Hint

The depth test answers “which one do I keep?”. Blending needs “what was behind this, and in what order did the layers stack?”. Ask whether one float per pixel can answer the second question. Then ask what happens if the transparent surface writes depth, and what happens if it does not.

Solution sketch

It cannot be done with the depth test alone, and the reason is precise: blending is not commutative, so the result depends on the order the layers arrive, and the z-buffer has thrown that order away by design. The standard answer is a hybrid every engine uses:

  • draw all opaque geometry first, z-buffered, in any order (roughly front-to-back, for the early-Z reason in 3.1.3);
  • then draw transparent geometry sorted back to front, with the depth test still on (so it is correctly hidden by opaque things) but depth writes off (so transparent surfaces do not occlude each other). That is enable_depth_test = true, enable_depth_write = false, and it is exactly why SDL_GPU exposes those as two separate flags.

So the painter’s algorithm is not dead — it has been demoted to the one job the z-buffer structurally cannot do, and it drags all three of §1.3’s failures along with it. Intersecting transparent surfaces still look wrong in shipped games today; if you have ever seen two smoke particles pop as the camera turns, you have watched a cycle being re-sorted. Order-independent transparency is an active research area precisely because this is unsolved. A good answer to this exercise ends with a paragraph on what you would try — per-triangle sorting, splitting, depth peeling, weighted-blended OIT — and what each costs.

9Recap & Next

What the engine can do now that it could not before

  • Hidden-surface removal, correctly, for any geometry. Intersecting triangles, cyclic overlaps and triangles stretched in depth all resolve without being special-cased, because visibility is decided per pixel rather than per object.
  • Filled 3-D. fill_triangle has existed since Lesson 2.2 and had never met the coordinate pipeline. It has now: the milestone mesh is opaque.
  • Order independence. Triangles can be submitted in any order, which is what makes the same algorithm work on a GPU in Module 4 and across threads in Module 8.
  • A depth attachment shaped like the hardware’s. Separate from the colour buffer, with its own format and clear value, and a comparison that lives in the rasterizer rather than in the storage — the same split SDL_GPU makes.
  • A precision budget you can compute. Δw = Δz · w² · (1/near − 1/far) predicts z-fighting in real units before you see it, and says which knob to turn.

And one thing worth taking beyond this engine

  • Device depth is affine in screen space; view depth is not. That derivation is not a detail of our rasterizer — it is the reason the GPU interpolates depth for free and the reason every other vertex attribute needs the correction Lesson 3.2 is about. The bracket it produced — 1/w varies affinely across the screen — is one of the two or three most useful facts in real-time rendering.

Next: Lesson 3.2 — Perspective-Correct Interpolation. Section 3.4 left a loose end lying in plain sight. Depth interpolates exactly because the projection had already converted it into a screen-affine quantity. Nothing else a vertex carries has had that done to it. Texture coordinates, vertex colours and normals are all linear along the surface, and the surface is 1/w-warped on the screen — so interpolating them the way we have been is the right-hand plot of Figure 4, applied to a checkerboard. In 3.2 we put a texture on a floor, watch it swim and buckle exactly where the theory says it must, and then derive the fix from the one bracket this lesson already proved: divide the attributes by w, interpolate those, and divide back at the end.
