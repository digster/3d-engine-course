Cook–Torrance PBR, Derived

Lesson 6.2 measured 1.1386 — a white surface with a white highlight reflecting more light than arrives — and Lesson 6.3 deliberately left it standing, because normalising the distribution does not touch it. The two lobes were independent, so the same photon was counted once as having bounced off the surface and once as having gone into it. Fresnel is the coupling, and that one sentence closes the door. Along the way we derive the 4(n·l)(n·v) denominator instead of quoting it, measure exactly how good Schlick's approximation is not, watch the metallic workflow arrive as a consequence rather than a checkbox, and break the reference render on purpose for the first time in fifteen lessons.

1The Problem, Which Is a Photon Counted Twice

Two lessons ago we gave the shading equation units, and the units immediately produced a number nobody wanted:

The door, still open

A white surface with a white highlight reflects 1.1386 of the light that hits it. Not 1.1386 units of something — 1.1386 of what arrived. The surface is emitting light it was never given.

Lesson 6.3 spent five hours on that surface and did not fix this. That was on purpose, and the reason is worth restating because it is the hinge the whole of today swings on. 6.3 replaced shininess with a statistical claim — a distribution of microfacet normals that integrates to one — and proved the claim by integrating it. It was a large improvement and it moved this number not at all.

Here is why. Normalising D makes the specular lobe well-behaved: the facets' projected areas add up to the area of the flat surface they stand on, no more. But the shading equation still reads

\[ f_r \;=\; \underbrace{\frac{\text{albedo}}{\pi}}_{\text{diffuse}} \;+\; \underbrace{\frac{D\,G}{4(n\!\cdot\!l)(n\!\cdot\!v)}}_{\text{specular}} \]

In prose: the BRDF is the diffuse lobe plus the specular lobe, added.

Look at that plus sign and ask what it claims. It says: whatever fraction of the light the surface mirrors, it also scatters the full albedo's worth. A white surface scatters all of the light back diffusely, and mirrors some of it on top. Both terms drew on the same photon and neither told the other.

So this is not a constant that is slightly wrong. It is a missing conversation between two terms, and no amount of tuning either one separately can fix it — which is exactly why 6.3 could not, and said so, and left the number where you could see it.

The fix is a physical fact so ordinary you have looked at it every day of your life, and it is the subject of the next section.

2Building Intuition: the Window at Night

Stand at a window in a lit room after dark and you see two things at once: the street outside, and your own reflection. The glass is doing both jobs with the same photons — some bounce off the surface toward your eye, and the rest go through. Every photon does one or the other. None does both.

Now walk sideways until you are looking along the glass at a shallow angle, and watch what happens: your reflection gets stronger and the street gets dimmer. Keep going and the window becomes a mirror. This is not a property of that particular window. Look along a tabletop, a sheet of paper, a puddle, a painted wall — at a shallow enough angle, everything is a mirror.

The whole of Fresnel, in two sentences

The fraction of light that reflects off an interface depends on the angle you meet it at. It is small when you look straight down at the surface, and it rises to exactly 1 as you approach grazing — for every material, without exception.

Call that fraction F. Two things follow immediately, and the second is the one the engine needs.

First, F is what the specular lobe should be scaled by, because the specular lobe is the light that bounced off the interface. Our specular has been scaled by specular::colour instead — a number somebody typed.

Second, and this is the sentence to carry out of the lesson:

The coupling

F is what bounces off, so 1 − F is what goes in. And only what goes in can scatter around under the surface and come back out as diffuse light. The two lobes stop being independent the moment you write that down.

That is the whole mechanism. The diffuse term is not "the albedo"; it is "the albedo, of the light that got past the front door". A surface with a strong mirror reflection has less light left to scatter, automatically, with nobody tuning anything.

It is worth pausing on how much of the trouble this removes without being asked. Consider the surface that produced 1.1386. Under the coupled model, the specular takes about 4% and the diffuse gets 96% — and the total lands just under 1 because that is what "each photon does one thing" means arithmetically. We will measure it in §7 rather than assert it, but the shape of the answer is already fixed by the physics.

3The Missing Third

Lesson 6.3 built two thirds of a microfacet BRDF and named the third:

  • D, the normal distribution function — what fraction of the microfacets face the right way to send this light at my eye?
  • G, the geometry term — and of those, what fraction can actually see both the light and the eye, rather than hiding behind their neighbours?
  • F, Fresnel — and of the light that reaches one, what fraction bounces off rather than going in?

Three questions, asked in order, each narrowing the last. That is genuinely all a microfacet specular BRDF is, and it is worth noticing that the three are independent questions about independent things: D is about the surface's shape, G is about the surface's shape seen from somewhere, and F is about the material's optical properties and not about its shape at all. That is why they multiply.

So the numerator is D · G · F. What is the denominator, and where on earth does 4(n·l)(n·v) come from?

This is, by some distance, the most quoted and least explained line in real-time graphics. Lesson 6.3 deferred it explicitly and promised it here. It is not hard — it is two elementary steps and a trigonometric identity you already know — and having done it once you will never have to take it on faith again.

4The Denominator, Derived

The problem to solve is a units problem, and stating it that way makes the solution almost inevitable.

D is a density over microfacet normals. It answers "how much of the surface is tilted this way", per steradian of facet-normal direction. But a BRDF is a density over light directions — it answers "how much light goes that way", per steradian of outgoing direction. Those are two different spaces, and converting a density from one to the other requires knowing how much the map between them stretches. That stretch factor is a Jacobian, and it is where the 4 comes from.

4.1The doubling

Hold the eye direction v fixed. Every microfacet normal h reflects v into some light direction l; as h sweeps around, l sweeps around too, and we want to know how fast.

Put spherical coordinates on v itself — that is the trick, and it is the only trick. Let θ_h be the angle between v and the facet normal h. Reflecting v about h turns it through θ_h to reach h, and another θ_h to get to the far side:

\[ \theta_{\text{out}} \;=\; 2\,\theta_h \]

In prose: tilt the mirror by an angle, and the reflected ray turns by twice that angle.

This is the same fact that makes a mirror on a galvanometer double its own deflection, and it is exact — no approximation anywhere. The azimuth is unchanged: φ_out = φ_h, because the reflection happens in the plane containing v and h.

4.2From facets to directions

Now write down the two solid angles. A patch of directions near h, in spherical coordinates about v, has solid angle

\[ d\omega_h \;=\; \sin\theta_h \; d\theta_h \, d\phi_h \]

and the patch of outgoing directions it maps to has

\[ d\omega_{\text{out}} \;=\; \sin\theta_{\text{out}} \; d\theta_{\text{out}} \, d\phi_{\text{out}} \;=\; \sin(2\theta_h) \cdot 2\,d\theta_h \, d\phi_h \]

In prose: substituting θ_out = 2 θ_h brings down a factor of 2 from dθ_out = 2 dθ_h, and turns the sine into sin 2θ_h.

Divide, and watch the double-angle identity do all the remaining work:

\[ \frac{d\omega_{\text{out}}}{d\omega_h} \;=\; \frac{2\sin(2\theta_h)}{\sin\theta_h} \;=\; \frac{2 \cdot 2\sin\theta_h\cos\theta_h}{\sin\theta_h} \;=\; 4\cos\theta_h \;=\; 4\,(v\!\cdot\!h) \]

There is the 4, and there is the cosine, and they came from the same place. One factor of two is the dθ_out = 2 dθ_h; the other is the 2 inside sin 2θ = 2 sin θ cos θ, which hands over the cos θ_h = v·h as change.

Worked example, at θh = 18°

The reflected ray comes off at 36°, exactly twice. The stretch factor is 4 cos 18° = 3.804226, and computing it the other way, 2 sin 36° / sin 18° = 3.804226 — the same to every digit a double has. Going the other direction, a patch of light directions maps to a patch of facet normals 1 / 3.804226 = 0.262866 as large.

verify_64 §B does not check this by re-deriving it a second way — that would only check the algebra. It measures it: build a tiny parallelogram of light directions on the sphere, push its three corners through h = normalize(l + v), and compare the areas. Over sixteen configurations of light and eye the worst relative error is 1.42 × 10⁻³, and that residue is the finite difference, not the formula.

4.3Putting the BRDF together

Now we can walk a beam of light through the surface and read the BRDF off the other end. Take a patch of macroscopic surface of area dA, lit from direction l.

  1. Which facets can help? Only those whose normal is h, the halfway vector. Their total area is D(h) dω_h dA — that is what D means.
  2. How much light do they catch? A facet presents its area to the light foreshortened by its own tilt, so the projected area is l·h times the above. And only a fraction G of them are unshadowed. So the flux they intercept is L_i dω_l · G D(h) (l·h) dω_h dA.
  3. How much of that leaves? A fraction F — the rest goes in.
  4. Where does it go? Into dω_out = 4(v·h) dω_h, by §4.2.
  5. What radiance is that? Radiance is flux per unit solid angle per unit projected area, so divide by dω_out and by dA (n·v).

Assemble, and cancel:

\[ L_o \;=\; \frac{F \, G \, D(h)\,(l\!\cdot\!h)\,d\omega_h \; L_i \, d\omega_l} {4(v\!\cdot\!h)\,d\omega_h \cdot (n\!\cdot\!v)} \;=\; \frac{F\,G\,D\;L_i\,d\omega_l}{4\,(n\!\cdot\!v)} \]

The dω_h cancels, and so does the l·h against the v·h — because h bisects l and v, so those two dot products are the same number. That cancellation is precisely why the finished formula carries no v·h and looks like it fell out of the sky.

One step left. A BRDF is defined per unit irradiance, and irradiance already carries its own cosine (Lesson 6.2 §2.3), so divide by L_i (n·l) dω_l:

\[ \boxed{\;f_{\text{spec}} \;=\; \frac{D \, G \, F}{4\,(n\!\cdot\!l)(n\!\cdot\!v)}\;} \]

Every factor now has a reason, and none of them is a convention: the 4 is the Jacobian, the vanished (v·h) cancelled against the facets' projected area, the (n·v) is radiance's definition, and the (n·l) is the BRDF's. That is the line you were going to have to take on trust, and now you do not.

5Fresnel, From the Physics

We have used F for two sections without saying what it is. Time to fix that — and to do it in the order this course always uses: the shape first, from physics you can check by looking out of a window, and only then a formula.

5.1The shape, before any formula

When light meets a boundary between two materials — air and glass, air and water, air and paint — some reflects and some crosses. Augustin-Jean Fresnel worked out how much, in 1823, from the wave picture of light and the requirement that the electric and magnetic fields match up on both sides of the boundary. The answer depends on the angle and on the polarisation, and for unpolarised light you average the two polarisations:

\[ r_s = \frac{\cos\theta_i - n\cos\theta_t}{\cos\theta_i + n\cos\theta_t},\qquad r_p = \frac{n\cos\theta_i - \cos\theta_t}{n\cos\theta_i + \cos\theta_t},\qquad F = \tfrac{1}{2}\left(r_s^2 + r_p^2\right) \]

In prose: two amplitude ratios, one per polarisation, each squared to turn an amplitude into a power, then averaged. θ_t is the transmitted angle, from Snell's law.

You do not need to memorise that, and we are not going to ship it. What matters is the shape it produces, and the shape has two features that are worth more than the formula:

  • At normal incidence it is small, and it is a property of the material — 4% for glass, 2% for water, 17% for diamond.
  • At grazing incidence it is exactly 1, always, for every material. Not "close to 1"; exactly 1. That is the window at night, and it is why it does not matter what the window is made of.

Look at where the curves spend their time. All three sit almost flat at their normal-incidence value for the first forty-five degrees, then turn upward, and then shoot to 1 in the last few degrees before grazing. Most of a rendered image lives in that flat part — which is why a single number per material gets you most of the way, and why the last few degrees are where everything interesting happens visually (wet roads at sunset, the rim of a face against a window).

5.2F0, and where 0.04 comes from

Set θ_i = 0 in the equations above. Then θ_t = 0 too, both cosines are 1, and the two polarisations collapse onto the same expression:

\[ F_0 \;=\; \left(\frac{n_1 - n_2}{n_1 + n_2}\right)^{\!2} \;\;\xrightarrow{\;n_1 = 1\;}\;\; \left(\frac{1 - n}{1 + n}\right)^{\!2} \]

That is one line of algebra and it is the single most useful equation in material authoring, because it turns a number you can look up (an index of refraction) into a number the renderer wants (a reflectance).

Worked example: glass

Window glass has n = 1.5. So F0 = ((1 − 1.5) / (1 + 1.5))² = (−0.2)² = 0.04.

That is where the 0.04 in every PBR renderer comes from. It is not a constant somebody liked; it is window glass, and most dielectrics are close enough to window glass that one number serves.

The transferable habit

A parameter that can be round-tripped into a physical quantity can be audited. An error a parameter can absorb is invisible until you ask the parameter what it claims about the world — and then it is obvious in one line. This is the second time in three lessons that trick has found something.

5.3Schlick, fitted and measured

The exact equations need Snell's law, a square root, two polarisation terms and an average: call it twenty operations, per light, per pixel. In 1994 Christophe Schlick proposed replacing all of it with

\[ F(\cos\theta) \;=\; F_0 \;+\; (1 - F_0)\,(1 - \cos\theta)^5 \]

Notice what this gets right by construction, before any fitting happens. At cos θ = 1 the second term vanishes and it returns exactly F0. At cos θ = 0 it returns F0 + 1 − F0 = 1 — exactly 1, for every material, which is the physical fact from §5.1 built into the algebra rather than approximated. Schlick's actual contribution is the fifth power in between, and that is the only part that is a fit.

So: how good a fit? The folklore says "very accurate" and moves on. Lesson 6.3 gave the s = 2/α² − 2 mapping the same treatment and found that the folklore was quoting one answer to a different question, so let us measure rather than repeat.

Worked example: glass at 60°

cos 60° = 0.5, so (1 − 0.5)⁵ = 0.03125 and Schlick gives 0.04 + 0.96 × 0.03125 = 0.0700.

The exact equations give 0.0892. Schlick is 21% low.

And yet we are going to ship it, so the defence had better be honest. It is this: a 21% error in a quantity that is itself 0.04 is an error of 0.019 in a reflectance, which is well below what an 8-bit display can show and far below what anything else in this renderer is wrong by. Schlick is not accurate; it is cheap, exact at both ends, and wrong by an amount that does not matter at dielectric F0. Those are different claims, and only the second one survives contact with a measurement.

Note the last row, too: the absolute error doubles for diamond. The approximation gets worse as F0 rises, which is exactly the regime where metals live — and where you might care. Exercise 15.3 asks you to find out whether it is visible.

6Metals, and Where the Colour Lives

We now have everything needed for a claim this course has been making informally since Lesson 3.7 — that a plastic has a white highlight and a metal has a coloured one — and we can finally say why, rather than asserting it as a rule of thumb.

Follow the light. It arrives at the surface, and by §2 it does exactly one of two things.

For a dielectric — plastic, paint, skin, wood, glass — the fraction that bounces off does so at the clear boundary, which has no pigment in it. So the mirrored light keeps the lamp's colour: F0 is grey. The rest goes in, rattles around among the pigment particles, loses whichever wavelengths they absorb, and comes back out. So the diffuse light carries the colour. A dielectric's colour lives in its albedo and its F0 is grey.

For a conductor — gold, copper, iron — the light that crosses the interface meets a sea of free electrons and is absorbed within a few atomic layers. Nothing comes back out. So there is no diffuse lobe at all, and the only light leaving the surface is the part that bounced off. If a gold surface is going to look gold, the colour has nowhere to live except in the reflection itself. A metal's F0 is coloured and its albedo is irrelevant.

Gold's F0 is about (1.00, 0.71, 0.29): it reflects all the red, most of the green, and less than a third of the blue. Copper is (0.95, 0.64, 0.54), silver very nearly white at (0.97, 0.96, 0.92).

Now look at what that does to the material representation, because this is the part that usually arrives as a pipeline convention and it should not:

The metallic workflow, as a consequence

A dielectric needs a coloured albedo and a grey F0. A metal needs a coloured F0 and no albedo. So one colour field serves both, and a single number decides which question it is answering:

\[ F_0 = \text{lerp}(0.04,\; \text{albedo},\; \text{metallic}), \qquad \text{diffuse albedo} = \text{albedo} \times (1 - \text{metallic}) \]

That is not a compression of two workflows into one. It is the physics, written down.

Two details worth pinning, because both look like sloppiness and are not.

Why is metallic a float when nothing is half a metal? Because a texture that says "this pixel is the painted part of the robot and that one is the bare steel" has to be filtered, and a filtered switch is a float. The in-between values are not materials; they are the blend across a boundary. (They are also, in practice, where artists make a mess — see the pitfalls.)

Why keep f0 as a separate field at all, if it is 0.04 for nearly everything? Because "nearly" is doing real work: water at 0.02 and gemstones at 0.17 are both things a scene may contain, and a parameter with a physical meaning is cheap to keep and expensive to reintroduce later. It defaults to 0.04, so a material that has nothing to say about it says nothing.

7The Coupling, and Where It Fails

Back to the door. We have F, so we can write the sentence from §2 as arithmetic:

\[ f_r \;=\; \underbrace{\frac{D\,G\,F}{4(n\!\cdot\!l)(n\!\cdot\!v)}}_{\text{what bounced off}} \;+\; \underbrace{k_d \cdot \frac{\text{albedo}}{\pi}}_{\text{what got in and came back}} \]

…and the only question left is what k_d should be. The obvious answer is 1 − F. The question is F at which angle, and it turns out to matter enormously.

Nearly every real-time renderer answers k_d = 1 − F(v·h), reusing the Fresnel already computed for the specular. It is one line, it costs nothing extra, and the glTF 2.0 reference BRDF is defined this way. ⚠ VERIFY: check Khronos glTF 2.0 specification, Appendix B, "BRDF Implementation", before relying on this for asset interchange in Lesson 6.7. So let us measure it, using Lesson 6.2's hemispherical-reflectance integrator, unchanged for the third lesson running.

couplingworst R(v) over the sweepreciprocal?verdict
none (Lessons 3.7 – 6.3)1.4300yesemits light

(1−F(n·l))(1−F(n·v))0.9255yesships

The standard coupling does not conserve energy. It closes the door at normal incidence beautifully — a white furnace reads 0.9999, better than the form we are about to ship — and then reaches 1.34 for a smooth surface viewed near grazing.

This was genuinely surprising, so the first thing to do was doubt the instrument. Refining the integration grid from 200 to 1600 steps moved the answer from 1.0921 to 1.2031 and then stopped: it converges on the excess rather than shrinking toward 1. Lesson 6.3's rule settles it — quadrature error shrinks with the grid; arithmetic error does not — so the excess is real.

Localising it took one more measurement. Integrate the two lobes separately at roughness 0.10 with the eye at 75°: the specular takes 0.2523 and the diffuse gives up only 0.0521. The books do not balance, and the reason is visible once you say it:

The diagnosis

1 − F(v·h) is the fraction of light that got IN. It says nothing about the light that fails to get OUT. A diffuse ray leaving toward an eye 75° off the normal meets the interface at 75°, where a quarter of it reflects back inside — and that form lets all of it out.

That is a diagnosis, which means it makes a prediction: bolt an exit factor on and the excess should vanish. It does — (1−F(v·h))(1−F(n·v)) drops the grazing 1.2002 to 0.9574 and the worst case to 0.9628. The mechanism was right.

And then that repair fails a different test, which is the part worth slowing down for. A BRDF must be reciprocal: swapping the light and the eye must not change the answer, because light does not know which way it is travelling. Measured on one configuration, that form gives 0.262327 one way and 0.293236 the other. It is not a BRDF at all.

Fix the asymmetry and the answer falls out. If light crossing the interface on the way out is attenuated, so is light crossing on the way in — at the light's own angle, not the half-vector's. Two crossings, one each:

\[ k_d \;=\; \bigl(1 - F(n\!\cdot\!l)\bigr)\bigl(1 - F(n\!\cdot\!v)\bigr) \]

Symmetric in l and v, so reciprocal. Motivated by the physics rather than fitted. And measured never to exceed 1 anywhere in a full sweep of roughness against view angle: worst 0.9255. That is what this engine ships.

The honest cost, stated

It is darker. At normal incidence a white surface reads 0.9144 where the half-vector form reads 0.9966 — about 8.5% less light. That light is not lost to a bug: it is the portion that reflects back inside the surface at the exit boundary, which a real material would eventually re-emit and this model simply forgets. It is the same class of loss as Lesson 6.3's missing 69%, and §10 names the fix.

The alternative is kept in the header as diffuse_coupling::half_vector, named and measured, exactly as Lesson 6.3 kept smith_g_separable. You will meet it in every engine you read next, and now you know precisely what it is approximating.

8Implementation

Everything above goes into gfx/microfacet.hpp, beside the D and G Lesson 6.3 left there. We build it in five steps, and the order is the order the derivation went in.

8.1Step one: the constant, and where it comes from

Start with the two functions that turn a physical measurement into a renderer parameter. They are three lines each and they are the reason the rest of the material is authorable.

The clamp in ior_from_f0 is not defensive tidying: at f0 = 1 the expression divides by zero, and f0 = 1 is a perfect mirror, which is a real thing a caller might ask about. Clamping just below returns a very large index of refraction, which is the right answer to the question "what material reflects everything?".

8.2Step two: Schlick, twice

The scalar version is for dielectrics, where F0 is grey. The linear_rgb version is what makes a metal a metal — and it is worth writing both rather than always paying for three channels, because the dielectric path is the common one.

m2 * m2 * m rather than std::pow(m, 5.0f): four multiplies against a library call that will compute exp(5 * log(m)) and be both slower and less accurate. The clamp matters for the same reason it mattered in Lesson 3.7 — two unit vectors' dot product is a cosine and cannot exceed 1, but it can round above it, and a negative m raised to the fifth is negative.

8.3Step three: the coupling, as a named choice

§7 measured two forms and found the difference large enough that picking one is a decision. That is exactly the situation Lesson 6.3 met with the two geometry terms, and it gets the same treatment: an enum, both implementations, and the measurement written into the doc comment so the choice cannot quietly become a default.

8.4Step four: the material, and a deletion

Now the part that touches forty-four call sites. The old surface description was 

struct specular
{
    linear_rgb colour{0.0f, 0.0f, 0.0f};
    float shininess = 32.0f;
};

and neither field survives. shininess was replaced in Lesson 6.3 by a roughness that measures something; colour is replaced here by an F0 that a physicist could look up. So specular is deleted, and microsurface takes its place — in microfacet.hpp, because these are the parameters of the microfacet model and that file is the model.

Why delete rather than re-document

colour = 0.85f and f0 = 0.04f are both perfectly good floats. There is no diagnostic anywhere in C++ for "same type, new meaning" — a struct that kept its name would have let every material in the engine keep compiling while rendering twenty-one times too bright.

This is the same bargain Lesson 6.2 struck when it renamed intensity to irradiance, and Lesson 3.7 struck when it gave shade() a to_eye parameter with no default. When the meaning changes, the type system is the only tool that reaches every caller.

Those last two functions are §6's two sentences, and nothing more.

8.5Step five: the assembly

The specular BRDF goes in microfacet.hpp beside its parts; the two-lobe assembly goes in light.hpp, because that is where the shading equation lives.

Note the four cosines arriving as parameters rather than the three vectors. That is not micro-optimisation: it makes this function pure arithmetic that a test can drive directly, which is what verify_64 §E does when it integrates the thing over a hemisphere. It also matches how the shader wants to work — compute the dots once, share them between the two halves.

8.6And in HLSL, in the same commit

shade() and scene.frag.hlsl have tracked each other since Lesson 4.8, and until now one has always followed the other by a lesson. Today they had to move together, and that is the first time in the course.

That is worth being nervous about, because a shader that drifts from the CPU does not fail loudly — it renders something plausible. The thing that makes it survivable is verify_48 §F, which renders the same 4096 fragments through both paths and compares them. Its model sweep gained a fourth row today: 

=== F. engine::shade() against scene.frag.hlsl ===
  none   4096 fragments: mean |CPU - GPU| = 0.000e+00, worst = 0.000e+00
  Phong  4096 fragments: mean |CPU - GPU| = 0.000e+00, worst = 0.000e+00
  Blinn  4096 fragments: mean |CPU - GPU| = 8.273e-08, worst = 1.788e-07
  Cook   4096 fragments: mean |CPU - GPU| = 7.831e-08, worst = 2.384e-07

2.4 × 10⁻⁷ — a couple of float ULP, from two independently written implementations of a dozen transcendental operations on two different processors. That is as close to "the same equation" as two machines get.

Getting there required copying one non-obvious thing across: Lesson 6.3's rearranged GGX denominator. The textbook form c²(α²−1)+1 is a catastrophic cancellation in float that loses 1.1% of the model's energy, and a GPU is no less IEEE-754 than a CPU. The shader carries the same (1−c)(1+c) + α²c², and the same comment explaining why.

One HLSL-specific trap, which cost a compile: cbuffer members share a single global namespace across every buffer in the shader. The Light buffer already had a pad0, so adding one to Material is a redefinition, not a local name — and the error message ("nameless block contains a member that already has a name at global scope") is not the first thing you would guess from.

9Re-authoring the Materials, and Breaking the Golden

§7.1 measured that the same surface is 1.96× brighter under the old model. Lesson 6.3 measured that the specular constant alone was 17× too small, and that specular::colour had been absorbing it. Those two facts together dictate the shape of this commit:

The constants and the materials move together, or not at all

Fix the constant and leave the materials, and every highlight in the engine blows out by seventeen times. Fix the materials and leave the constant, and everything goes black. They were wrong in compensating directions, so they have to be corrected in the same commit — which is why this lesson edits the engine, the shader, and every demo together, rather than doing the tidy thing and landing them separately.

10What This Lesson Does Not Do

Three honest gaps, each measured rather than waved at.

The model still loses light, and now we have two reasons. Lesson 6.3 measured the first: single-scattering microfacet theory lets a facet bounce light once and then forgets it, so a fully rough white metal returns 0.3000 of what arrives — the missing 69% hit a second facet on the way out. Today adds the second: the two-crossing coupling forgets the light that reflects back inside at the exit boundary, costing about 8.5% on a white dielectric at normal incidence. Both are the same kind of gap — a single-bounce model dropping the second bounce — and both have the same standard fix, multiple-scattering energy compensation (Kulla & Conty 2017), which is a lookup table and is out of scope here.

The ambient term still has no specular half. shade() ends in albedo * ambient, exactly as it has since Lesson 3.6, and nothing in this lesson touched it. So a mirror in a bright uniform room renders black except where the key light reaches it. Lesson 6.2 Exercise 13.5 predicted precisely this, and it is Lesson 6.12's subject: the missing term is the specular half of image-based lighting.

Fresnel is the dielectric form only. A conductor's index of refraction is complex — it has an absorption coefficient — and the correct Fresnel equations for one are a good deal messier than §5.1's. We do what every real-time renderer does: take the measured F0 and run it through Schlick, which is a fit to a curve the material does not actually have. It is close enough that no engine bothers, and it is worth knowing that the coloured F0 of a metal is a measurement, not something you can compute from an index of refraction the way §5.2 does for glass.

13Common Pitfalls & Debugging

The highlight is enormous and blown out

Symptom: every shiny object has a huge white blob on it.

Cause: you corrected the normalisation constant without re-authoring the materials. Lesson 6.3 measured the old constant as 17× too small, and the authored specular::colour values were 21× too large to compensate.

Fix: both, in the same commit. §9's table has the translations.

Fresnel evaluated at the wrong angle

Symptom: the rim lighting is wrong — either absent, or present everywhere. Objects look flat and slightly plastic-y in a way that is hard to name.

Cause: fresnel_schlick(n_dot_v, ...) in the specular term instead of fresnel_schlick(v_dot_h, ...). It is an easy substitution to make because both are "the angle you are looking at it from".

Fix: the specular's Fresnel is about the microfacet, and the microfacet's normal is h. It is the facet that is doing the mirroring, not the macroscopic surface. (The diffuse coupling is a different question and does use the macroscopic angles — see §7.)

Metals look like grey plastic

Symptom: you set metallic = 1 and got something dull and colourless rather than gold.

Cause: the albedo is dark. For a dielectric the albedo is the diffuse colour; for a metal it is F0, and metals have very bright F0 — gold is (1.00, 0.71, 0.29), not (0.4, 0.3, 0.1).

Fix: author metal albedos bright. A metal darker than about 0.5 in every channel is not a metal anyone has seen.

NaNs, or black pixels at silhouettes

Symptom: isolated black or garbage pixels at the edge of objects.

Cause: n_dot_v reaching zero in the denominator. At a silhouette the surface is edge-on to the eye and the interpolated normal can land exactly perpendicular — or, after interpolation, slightly past it.

Fix: cook_torrance_specular returns black when either cosine is non-positive, before it divides. That is not defensive coding: light below the horizon is a real case, and the true answer for it is zero. Note that smith_g goes to zero faster than the denominator does, so the quotient stays finite for small positive cosines without any epsilon.

The CPU and GPU disagree by a lot

Symptom: verify_48 §F reports a worst difference around 10⁻¹ or 10⁻² rather than 10⁻⁷.

Cause: almost always a uniform layout mismatch, not a maths error. The material buffer changed shape this lesson, and a shifted field renders something plausible rather than failing.

Fix: the static_asserts on every offset in gpu_uniform.hpp are what make a change this size safe. If they pass and the pixels still disagree, look for arithmetic that was rearranged on one side only — Lesson 6.3's GGX denominator is exactly such a case, and it is worth 1.1% on its own.

HLSL: "nameless block contains a member that already has a name at global scope"

Symptom: the shader will not compile after you add a field to a cbuffer.

Cause: cbuffer members live in one global namespace across the whole shader, so a second pad0 in a different buffer is a redefinition.

Fix: name padding per buffer — pad_m, not another pad0.

14Exercises

14.1 — Ship the glTF coupling and see it fail

Switch the default to diffuse_coupling::half_vector and re-run verify_64 §E. Then find a camera angle in the sandbox where the difference is visible on screen, not just in the integral.

Hint

The excess lives at grazing view angles on smooth surfaces. Set roughness to 0.05 with E and get the camera nearly edge-on to the floor.

Solution sketch

§E's worst case goes from 0.9255 to 1.3395. On screen it is much subtler than that sounds, because the excess needs light arriving from a wide range of directions to accumulate — with one directional light you mostly see a brighter rim. That is the real lesson: an energy error of 34% can be nearly invisible under a single light and becomes obvious the moment Lesson 6.12 adds an environment. Bugs that only appear when you add the next feature are the expensive kind.

14.2 — The furnace, as a picture

Lesson 6.2 Exercise 13.5 made a white Lambertian sphere vanish into a uniform background. Do it again with a specular sphere and explain what you see.

Solution sketch

It does not vanish — it renders as a dark disc. The ambient term has no specular counterpart, so the specular lobe contributes nothing at all (the key light is off), and the coupling now removes energy from the diffuse that nothing puts back. The sphere is darker than it was before this lesson. That is not a regression: it is the missing image-based specular term becoming visible, which is precisely what a furnace test is for.

14.3 — Is Schlick's error visible?

§5.3 measured Schlick at 23% relative error for glass and twice the absolute error for diamond. Implement the exact Fresnel equations in the shader, render both, and difference the frames. At what F0 does the error first reach one 8-bit code?

Hint

The harness already has fresnel_exact. Port it; it is eight lines. Remember the difference is in linear light and the display is sRGB-encoded, so a small linear difference in the darks is a larger code difference — Lesson 6.1's whole subject.

14.4 — Audit every material in the engine

Write a twenty-line program that walks the demo scenes and prints, for each material, the index of refraction its F0 implies. Flag anything above 3.

Solution sketch

After this lesson nothing should be flagged, because every dielectric is at the 0.04 default. Run it against the previous commit and all four demo materials fail. The point of the exercise is the technique: a parameter that round-trips into a physical quantity can be audited automatically, and one that cannot, cannot.

14.5 — Buy the energy back (engine challenge)

Implement multiple-scattering compensation. Precompute the directional albedo of the specular BRDF into a small table indexed by roughness and n·v, then add a second lobe carrying the energy the first one lost.

Hint

verify_64 §F already computes exactly the numbers the table needs — it is the white-furnace integral. A 32×32 table of floats is 4 KB. Kulla & Conty's 2017 course notes are the reference.

Solution sketch

The rough metal that returns 0.3000 should come back to within a percent of 1. Watch what it does to the look: rough metals stop being unaccountably dark, which is the single most visible artifact of a single-scattering renderer.

15Recap & Next

The engine has a physically-based BRDF. Not "a shinier highlight" — a surface model in which every photon is accounted for once, every parameter is a quantity somebody could measure, and the claim that it conserves energy is a number you can print.

\[ f_r \;=\; \frac{D\,G\,F}{4(n\!\cdot\!l)(n\!\cdot\!v)} \;+\; \bigl(1 - F(n\!\cdot\!l)\bigr)\bigl(1 - F(n\!\cdot\!v)\bigr)\frac{\text{albedo}}{\pi} \]

In prose: what bounced off the microfacets, plus what got past the interface, scattered, and got back out.

What the engine gained:

  • Fresnel, with F0 derived from an index of refraction rather than typed, and Schlick's fit measured (23% relative, 0.019 absolute) rather than praised.
  • The denominator, derived. The 4 is a Jacobian; the (v·h) cancelled; the (n·v) is radiance's definition; the (n·l) is the BRDF's.
  • The metallic workflow, arriving as a consequence of where a metal's colour can live rather than as a pipeline convention.
  • Energy conservation, measured. Worst hemispherical reflectance 0.9255 against 1.4300 before — and the discovery that the coupling nearly every engine ships reaches 1.3395.
  • A better characterization test, extended at the one moment it was free.

And one deletion: engine::specular, the struct Lesson 3.7 called "the first two fields of a material". Both fields turned out to be wrong — one measured nothing, and the other was absorbing a normalisation error. What replaced them, microsurface, is three numbers that all mean something.

Next: 6.5 — A Material System. Watch what just happened to this lesson's diff. A surface description now travels with an albedo, a texture, a cull mode and a blend mode, and changing it meant editing forty-four call sites across the engine, four demos and six test harnesses. Lesson 3.7 said "this is not called material because it is not one yet". It is now, and the pull that made this commit so wide is the design telling us what it wants to be.
