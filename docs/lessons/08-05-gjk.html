GJK: Convex Distance from a Support Function

8.4's Separating Axis Theorem answered are these two touching? and was honest, in its own doc comment, about the question it could not answer. Two unit cubes whose centres are (2, 2, 2) apart are √3 ≈ 1.732 m apart, corner to corner — and every one of the SAT's fifteen candidate axes reports 1.0, because every one of them is a coordinate axis and the gap along an axis is the offset's component rather than its length. The SAT is not wrong. It is 42.3% low, and on random box pairs it is more than 10% low 5.7% of the time. A lower bound answers a yes/no question and nothing else; a character controller wants to know how far it can step, and a continuous test wants the distance so it can bound the time of impact. Worse, the SAT's fifteen candidates were a fact about boxes. Hand it a cylinder and there is no list to write down. This lesson replaces it with an algorithm that has no list at all. The move is 8.4 §3.2's, finally cashed: two shapes become one set, the Minkowski difference A ⊖ B, and a question about a pair becomes a question about a point — does that set contain the origin? You cannot build the set, and you never need to, because its support function is two calls to theirs. Then GJK searches it with a simplex of at most four points, and the search is honest in a way few algorithms are: it maintains an upper bound (a real vector between two real surface points) and a lower bound (a supporting plane with the origin outside it), and when they meet the distance is not believed but proven — which is what lets this lesson's harness verify GJK without a second GJK. Then the things nobody warns you about, all of them measured. The textbook simplex solver, the one every implementation copies, returns a point that is not on the triangle when the triangle is a sliver — 1,048 failures in 200,000, worst case 14.5% wrong — and a sliver is not an edge case in GJK, it is the last two iterations of every query, so we compute four candidates instead of deciding between seven regions. |v| is monotone by construction, which makes a two-line check both a terminator and a corruption detector: it caught a search converging cleanly to 0.0233827 m and then jumping to 0.0674522. GJK terminates exactly on polytopes and only converges on curved shapes — box, hull and sphere are flat against tolerance while a capsule costs one more pass per decade. Two boxes standing on the same floor produce a difference set in which every vertex has y exactly zero, so the search is confined to a plane and can never build a tetrahedron: 0 of 100,000, against 77,326 once one box is tilted a single degree — which is a problem for 8.6, and named here. There is a resolution floor, and two separate things give out at two different gaps. And 8.4 §11's precision wall is fixed rather than merely repeated: taking every support point relative to its own shape's centre makes the error 96,270× smaller a thousand kilometres from the origin.

1. The problem: a lower bound is not a distance

Make two unit cubes. Put the first at the origin and the second at (2, 2, 2), axis aligned, no rotation at all. Ask 8.4's collide how far apart they are. 

const shape cube = box_shape(vec3{0.5f, 0.5f, 0.5f});
const obb A = world_obb(cube, vec3{0.0f, 0.0f, 0.0f}, quat::identity());
const obb B = world_obb(cube, vec3{2.0f, 2.0f, 2.0f}, quat::identity());

const separation s = collide(A, B);
// s.depth == -1.0f       "they are 1 metre apart"

They are not 1 metre apart. The nearest point of the first cube is its (+, +, +) corner at (0.5, 0.5, 0.5); the nearest point of the second is its (−, −, −) corner at (1.5, 1.5, 1.5); and the distance between those two points is

|(1, 1, 1)| = √(1² + 1² + 1²) = √3 = 1.7320508…

So the answer is out by 42.3%, and the interesting part is that nothing is broken. Run the SAT's whole candidate list rather than stopping at the first axis that separates, and the best of all fifteen is still exactly 1.0. That is not a bug in the implementation; it is what the algorithm measures.

Here is why. A separating axis test projects both boxes onto a direction and looks for a gap between the two intervals. The gap it finds is the component of the offset along that direction, minus the two projected radii. For these cubes the offset is (2, 2, 2), its component along x is 2, the two radii are 0.5 each, and 2 − 1 = 1. Perfectly correct — and it is a component, while the distance is a length, and a component is never longer than the length it came from.

The gap on an axis is a lower bound on the distance, always, and it is tight only when that axis happens to point along the true separation. For two convex polytopes the direction between the closest points is a face normal when the contact is vertex-to-face, and an edge cross product when it is edge-to-edge — both of which are in the SAT's list. But when the closest features are two vertices, the direction is whatever line joins those two corners, and no finite list built from faces and edges contains it. That is the case above, and it is not rare.

The measurement is §A.2 of this lesson's harness. Over 200,000 random oriented box pairs, 124,216 of them apart, comparing the best of all fifteen SAT axes against the true distance: 

  A.2  200000 random OBB pairs, 124216 of them apart
       SAT best-of-15 / true distance:
         mean  0.9817    worst  0.6901
         more than  1% low: 35671  (28.7%)
         more than 10% low: 7031  (5.7%)

The mean is reassuring and the tail is not. Nearly three in ten separated pairs are reported more than 1% too close together, one in eighteen more than 10%, and the worst case in the run was 31% low. If all you want is a boolean, none of this matters — a proof of separation is a proof of separation however loose the number beside it. The moment you want the number, it does.

1.1 And the axis list was a fact about boxes

There is a second, larger problem, and 8.4's own doc comments keep bumping into it. The fifteen candidates were derived from a specific fact: a box has three distinct face normals and three distinct edge directions. Every step of that derivation consulted the shape.

Hand the same theorem a cylinder and there is no list to write down, because the rim has infinitely many face normals. Hand it an artist's twenty-vertex convex collider and the list is twenty face normals from each shape plus several hundred edge pairs — no longer a test but a survey, and one whose cost grows as the product of the two vertex counts. The Separating Axis Theorem is still true for those shapes. It has simply stopped being an algorithm.

So this lesson does not improve the SAT. It replaces it with something that never asks what shape it is holding, and — this is the part worth sitting with — that turns out to be more accurate rather than less, because knowing less about the shape means having nothing to enumerate and therefore nothing to be wrong about.

2. Building intuition: two sets become one set

Put the boxes away for a moment and think about two people walking around a field. Person A wanders inside one fenced area, person B inside another. You want to know whether the two areas overlap.

Here is a strange way to find out. Instead of watching both people, watch the arrow from B to A: its tail on B, its head on A. As they wander, that arrow changes length and direction; collect every arrow that is possible, and translate all of them so their tails sit at one point. What you get is a cloud of arrowheads, and that cloud is a new region — one region, not two.

Now ask a question about that cloud. When does it contain the point where all the tails are? That happens exactly when some arrow has zero length — when A and B can be standing on the same spot, which is exactly when their two areas overlap. And when the cloud does not contain that point, the shortest arrow from the tail-point into the cloud is the shortest distance between the two areas, because it is one of the arrows, and every arrow runs from a point of B to a point of A.

Two regions have become one region, and a question about a pair has become a question about a point. That is the whole trick, and the rest of this lesson is arithmetic.

Hold on to why this is progress rather than bookkeeping. "Do these two sets intersect?" is a question about a relationship, and relationships are awkward to search: there is no obvious thing to move, no obvious direction to move it in. "Does this one set contain this one point?" is a question about a location, and a location is something you can walk toward. The 8.4 approach searched a space of directions and had to enumerate it; this one searches a space of points and can descend through it.

3. The Minkowski difference, and the two equivalences

The cloud of arrows has a name. For two sets A and B, the Minkowski difference is

A ⊖ B = { a − b : a ∈ A, b ∈ B }

— every point of A minus every point of B, which is exactly the set of arrows from B to A with their tails at the origin. 8.4 §3.2 introduced it, used it to explain where the fifteen candidate axes came from, and then walked away. This is where it earns its keep, and it does so through two statements that are almost too easy to prove:

Equivalence 1 — overlap becomes containment.

A and B overlap ⟺ A ⊖ B contains the origin

Left to right: if some point p is in both sets, then p − p = 0 is in the difference. Right to left: if 0 is in the difference, then 0 = a − b for some a ∈ A and b ∈ B, so a = b and that point is in both. Two lines, no convexity required, no approximation anywhere.

Equivalence 2 — distance becomes distance to the origin.

distance(A, B) = distance(origin, A ⊖ B)

Every point of the difference is a − b and its length from the origin is |a − b|, which is the distance between that particular pair of points. Minimise over the difference set and you have minimised over all pairs. Same two lines.

Figure 2 is the whole idea in three panels, and the two numbers in it are the same number. The left panel is a pentagon and a quadrilateral in the plane with the segment between their closest points; it measures 1.6530 m. The middle panel is their difference — a single set, drawn by taking all twenty pairwise differences and hulling them — and the arrow from the origin to its nearest point measures 1.6530 m. The right panel moves the quadrilateral until they overlap, and the origin is now inside the difference.

3.1 Worked, with real numbers

Take the smallest interesting case: two segments in one dimension. A = [3, 5] and B = [8, 11]. Their difference is every a − b, which runs from 3 − 11 = −8 to 5 − 8 = −3:

A ⊖ B = [−8, −3]

The origin is not in that interval, so the segments do not overlap — correct, one ends at 5 and the other starts at 8. The nearest point of the interval to the origin is −3, at distance 3 — and the gap between the segments is 8 − 5 = 3. ✓ Now slide B down to [4, 7]: the difference becomes [3 − 7, 5 − 4] = [−4, 1], which contains the origin, and the segments do overlap. ✓

Notice what happened to the arithmetic. Two intervals and a comparison became one interval and a containment test, and the containment test is about a fixed, known point. That is the shape of every use of this idea, in every dimension.

3.2 The catch, and why it is not fatal

The difference set is not free. Two convex polytopes with m and n vertices have a difference with up to m · n vertices — for two boxes that is 64 candidate points before hulling, and for two twenty-vertex colliders it is 400. Building it per pair per frame is out of the question, and building it once is impossible because it changes with every relative rotation.

So we will never build it. §5 shows that its support function — the only thing an algorithm actually needs — costs two calls to the two shapes' own, no matter how many vertices the difference would have had. First, though, we need to be clear about what a support function is and why one function is enough.

4. The support function is the shape

8.4 slipped one of these into shape.hpp a lesson early, with a note saying it would matter here: 

/// The farthest point of the box in direction `d`. `d` need not be unit length.
[[nodiscard]] vec3 support(const obb& box, vec3 d);

That is a support function: hand it a direction, get back the point of the shape that is farthest that way. For a box it is three sign tests — go to the + end of each axis when the direction leans that way and the − end when it leans the other. For a sphere it is centre + r·d̂. For a cloud of points it is a loop with a running maximum.

It looks like a small utility. It is not: a convex set is completely determined by its support function. Not described by, not approximated by — determined. Two convex sets with the same support function are the same set.

4.1 Why one point is enough

The argument is worth doing slowly, because it is the reason the rest of the lesson works.

Fix a direction d and let h = dot(support(X, d), d). By definition no point of X has a larger dot product with d, so every point satisfies dot(x, d) ≤ h. That inequality describes a half-space: everything on one side of a plane whose normal is d. So each direction gives you one half-space that contains the whole set, and the boundary of that half-space touches the set — it is not slack.

Now intersect all of them, over every direction. The result contains X, because each one does. Does it contain anything else? Suppose a point p is outside X. Because X is convex and closed, there is a plane that separates them — that is the separating hyperplane theorem, and it is the same fact 8.4's shadow test rests on, wearing different clothes. Take that plane's normal as d: the half-space it defines contains X and excludes p. So p is not in the intersection.

Every point outside X is excluded by some half-space, and every point of X is in all of them. The intersection is X, and support named the boundary of every one of those half-spaces. That is what "determines" means.

The convexity is load-bearing and it is the same convexity 8.4 needed. For a non-convex shape the separating hyperplane theorem fails — think of 8.4's ball resting in the mouth of a cup — and the intersection of all those half-spaces gives you the shape's convex hull rather than the shape. That is not a defect to be worked around; it is the reason collision geometry is convex in the first place, and the reason a barrel collides as a cylinder.

4.2 Worked: a box, by hand

Take the box from 8.4 §6: half extents (2, 1, 0.5), turned 30° about z, centred at the origin. Its axes are then

u₀ = (0.866025, 0.500000, 0), u₁ = (−0.500000, 0.866025, 0), u₂ = (0, 0, 1)

Ask for the support point in direction d = (1, 0, 0). A point of the box is Σ sᵢ hᵢ uᵢ with each sᵢ in [−1, 1], so

dot(p, d) = s₀ · 2 · 0.866025 + s₁ · 1 · (−0.500000) + s₂ · 0.5 · 0

The three terms are independent, so each sᵢ is chosen on its own to make its own term as large as possible: s₀ = +1 because its coefficient is positive, s₁ = −1 because its coefficient is negative, and s₂ is free because its coefficient is zero (the implementation picks +1, deterministically, so two runs agree). The support point is

p = +2·u₀ − 1·u₁ + 0.5·u₂ = (1.732051 + 0.500000, 1.000000 − 0.866025, 0.5) = (2.232051, 0.133975, 0.500000)

and dot(p, d) = 2.232051 — which is exactly the projected radius 8.4 computed for the same box and the same direction, as it must be, because the projected radius is the support value of a centred box. The two formulas were the same formula all along; 8.4 kept the number and threw the point away, and GJK needs the point.

4.3 And a capsule is a Minkowski sum, which makes its support two terms

A capsule is a segment inflated by a ball — formally segment ⊕ ball, a Minkowski sum this time. Support functions add over sums:

supportX⊕Y(d) = supportX(d) + supportY(d)

because a point of X ⊕ Y is x + y with x and y chosen independently, so maximising dot(x + y, d) maximises dot(x, d) and dot(y, d) separately — the same separability argument as the box's three axes, one level up. For a capsule that reads: 

vec3 support_local(const capsule& c, vec3 d)
{
    const vec3 spine = c.axis * ((dot(c.axis, d) >= 0.0f) ? c.half_height : -c.half_height);

    const float len2 = length_squared(d);
    if (len2 <= 0.0f) { return spine; }
    return spine + d * (c.radius / std::sqrt(len2));
}

Two terms, one per operand, and neither knows the other exists. Set half_height to zero and the first term vanishes and this is a sphere — exactly, not approximately: §B.4 of the harness ran 20,000 random directions against support(sphere) and got 20,000 bit-identical points. Set the radius to zero instead and it is a segment. One function, three shapes, and the reduction is the arithmetic rather than a special case in it.

5. The identity that makes the difference computable

Now the step that turns §3's idea into an algorithm. We need the support function of A ⊖ B without ever building A ⊖ B.

A point of the difference is a − b. Maximise its dot product with d:

dot(a − b, d) = dot(a, d) + dot(−b, d) = dot(a, d) − dot(b, d)

Again a and b are chosen independently, so the maximum of the sum is the sum of the maxima. The first term is maximised by the farthest point of A in direction d. The second is −dot(b, d) = dot(b, −d), maximised by the farthest point of B in direction −d. Therefore

supportA⊖B(d) = supportA(d) − supportB(−d)

Two calls. Not m · n, not proportional to anything about the difference set — two. A set with four hundred vertices answers a question about itself at the price of two questions to its parents, and it is this identity, more than the algorithm built on it, that makes GJK possible.

The minus sign on the second call is the one thing people get wrong, so it is worth a sanity check with the 1D segments from §3.1. A = [3, 5], B = [8, 11], d = +1. The farthest point of A in the + direction is 5; the farthest point of B in the − direction is 8; so the support of the difference is 5 − 8 = −3, which is indeed the largest element of [−8, −3]. ✓ Take d = −1 and you get 3 − 11 = −8, the smallest. ✓

§B.1 checks it on shapes rather than intervals, and checks it against the definition rather than against another implementation: two twelve-point sets, all 144 pairwise differences built explicitly, and the maximum over that built set compared against the identity, on 2,000 random trials. Worst disagreement: 0.0000e+00 m — not small, zero, because both sides select the same float and subtract the same pair.

5.1 Carrying the two halves along

There is a free bonus here, and taking it is what separates a useful GJK from a toy one. Computing a point of the difference requires computing a point of A and a point of B anyway. Keep them: 

struct gjk_vertex
{
    vec3 w{};    ///< `pa − pb − (b.origin − a.origin)`. A point of `A ⊖ B`.
    vec3 pa{};   ///< The supporting point on A, relative to `a.origin`.
    vec3 pb{};   ///< The supporting point on B, relative to `b.origin`.
};

Six extra floats per simplex vertex, carried through a loop that runs three or four times. In §6 they turn the abstract answer — "the nearest point of the difference set is here" — into two actual points on two actual surfaces, which is what a character controller, a contact generator and a debug draw all actually want.

6. The algorithm, in four lines — and worked

We have one set, and we want the point of it nearest the origin. The set can have hundreds of vertices and we can only ask it questions of the form "which way is farthest that way?". GJK is the descent that answers anyway.

Keep a simplex: between one and four points of A ⊖ B — a point, a segment, a triangle, a tetrahedron. Four, because four is the most a simplex in three dimensions can have, and because a tetrahedron is the smallest thing that can enclose a point. Let v be the point of that simplex nearest the origin. Then:

  1. Ask the difference set for its farthest point in direction −v. Call it w.
  2. If the two bounds below have met, stop — the distance is proven.
  3. Add w to the simplex, then reduce the simplex to the smallest face that still contains its own nearest point.
  4. v is that nearest point. Repeat.

6.1 The two bounds, which are the whole design

Step 2 is where the algorithm stops being a plausible descent and becomes something you can trust, so it deserves its own derivation.

The upper bound is |v|. The simplex is made of points of A ⊖ B; the set is convex; so any convex combination of them is also in the set, and v is one. It is therefore an actual point of the set, at distance |v| from the origin, so the true distance cannot be larger than that. You get this bound for free, and it only ever improves.

The lower bound is dot(v, w) / |v|. This one is the interesting half. w was chosen to maximise dot(x, −v) over the whole set, which is the same as minimising dot(x, v). So every point x of A ⊖ B satisfies

dot(x, v) ≥ dot(w, v)

Divide by |v| and the left side is the signed distance of x from the plane through the origin with normal v̂. So the entire set lies at least dot(w, v)/|v| beyond that plane, and the origin is on the plane. Nothing in the set can be nearer the origin than that. Note what this gives you when it is positive: a complete proof that the shapes are apart, from one dot product.

The gap between the bounds is |v| − dot(v, w)/|v| = (|v|² − dot(v, w)) / |v|. Stop when it is small compared with |v|, which after multiplying through is 

const float vw = dot(v, w.w);
if (v2 - vw <= tol * v2)
{
    break;
}

Dividing by |v| a second time is what makes the test scale free: multiply the whole scene by a thousand and both sides scale by a million, and the test says exactly the same thing. §11 measures what an absolute tolerance would have cost instead.

6.3 Getting the witness points back

v is a convex combination of the reduced simplex's vertices: there are weights λᵢ ≥ 0 summing to 1 with v = Σ λᵢ wᵢ. Those are barycentric coordinates — 2.3's construction, back for a third time in this course after deciding which pixels are inside a triangle and how to interpolate across one.

Each wᵢ was built as paᵢ − pbᵢ − delta. Apply the same weights to the two halves separately:

pA = Σ λᵢ paᵢ ∈ A, pB = Σ λᵢ pbᵢ ∈ B

Both are convex combinations of points of their own shapes, so both are in those shapes — that is what convexity means. And because the weights sum to one,

pA − pB = Σ λᵢ (paᵢ − pbᵢ) − delta·Σλᵢ = Σ λᵢ wᵢ = v

so |pA − pB| = |v|. The two witness points are a real pair of surface points whose separation is the reported distance, and they cost one weighted sum each. §E.4 of the harness checks that they are genuinely on their shapes by comparing them against a fresh support call along the reported direction: worst overshoot 4.67e−07 m over 20,000 pairs, which is float noise at metre scale.

6.4 The seed matters, and its sign is not arbitrary

The loop has to start from some point of the difference set, and any will do for correctness. Not any will do for speed. The difference set is centred near −delta, where delta = b.origin − a.origin, so the direction from the set toward the origin is +delta, and the support point that way is already close to the answer. Seeding with −delta instead picks the point of the set farthest from the origin — perfectly legal, and it costs a whole iteration on every query for ever.

That is not a hypothetical. It is what this lesson's first draft did, and the measurement is what caught it: mean iterations over 117,413 separated box pairs went from 4.52 to 3.52, and sphere-against-sphere went from two iterations to one, because for two spheres the second support point is the answer. A sign on a seed, worth 22% of the whole algorithm.

6.5 And warm starting, which is worth less than you would think

If the seed matters, last frame's answer should be a better seed than the centre line — two bodies that have barely moved have barely moved their nearest features either. §G measures it over 234,197 queries, 2,000 pairs drifting past each other for 120 frames each: 

  G.1  234197 queries, 2000 pairs drifting over 120 frames
       mean iterations
         cold  (centre to centre)   2.219
         warm  (last frame's axis)  2.152   1.03x
         CONTROL (random axis)      3.414   0.65x

Read the control first. A random seed costs 54% more passes than the centre line, so the seed really does matter a great deal — and then warm starting buys only 3% on top of the free heuristic, because two bodies that have not moved much have not moved their centre line much either. Reporting the 3% without the 54% would suggest the seed is unimportant; reporting the 54% without the 3% would suggest warm starting is a major win. Neither is true on its own.

Warm starting stays in the API anyway, and 8.7 will want it for a reason that has nothing to do with iteration counts: keeping the same contact feature from frame to frame is what makes a persistent manifold persistent, and that is about stability rather than speed.

7. The simplex solver: compute, don't decide

Step 3 of the four-line sketch — "reduce the simplex to the smallest face that still contains its own nearest point" — is where GJK's correctness lives, where its bugs live, and where most write-ups get vague. It is the only part of this lesson that needed rewriting after it was measured, so it gets the space.

The job: given up to four points, find the one nearest the origin and discard the vertices that do not contribute. Discarding matters because the simplex has only four slots, and a vertex that contributes nothing to v still occupies one and makes the next simplex thinner than it needs to be.

7.1 The triangle case, four candidates

Start with the triangle, because it is the case that decides everything. The nearest point of a triangle to the origin is either in its interior or on one of its three edges. A clamped edge — one where the parameter is held in [0, 1] — already includes its own two endpoints, so three clamped edges cover all six boundary regions (three edges, three vertices). Add the interior projection and you have four candidates that cover everything:

  • the interior: project the origin onto the triangle's plane; if its barycentric coordinates are all non-negative, that point is in the triangle and is a candidate;
  • three clamped edges: for each pair of vertices, project the origin onto the line, clamp the parameter into [0, 1], take the point.

Evaluate all four, take the smallest. There is no case analysis because there is nothing to decide.

The interior candidate's barycentric coordinates come from signed areas, which is 2.3's construction exactly. With the origin as the query point the three cross products collapse pleasantly: for a triangle abc with normal n = (b − a) × (c − a),

λa = dot(b × c, n) / n·n, λb = dot(c × a, n) / n·n, λc = dot(a × b, n) / n·n

and those three numerators sum to n·n exactly, which is why one reciprocal does all three divisions. If any of them comes out negative the origin's projection is outside the triangle and the interior candidate is simply skipped — which covers both "the origin projects outside" and "this triangle is too thin to have an inside", with no separate test for the second.

7.2 The textbook version, and why we do not ship it

There is a faster way, and it is the one every implementation copies — Ericson's ClosestPtPointTriangle, which this lesson's first draft shipped. It identifies which of the triangle's seven Voronoi regions the query point lies in, using six dot products and three 2×2 determinants, and then evaluates only that one region. Fewer square roots, fewer divisions, one branch instead of four comparisons. In exact arithmetic it is correct.

The three determinants are signed areas: differences of nearly equal products. On a thin triangle they are noise, so the region the test selects can be the wrong one — and when it is, the point it returns is the foot of the perpendicular on the triangle's plane, which can be arbitrarily nearer the origin than the triangle itself is. The right-hand panel of figure 6 shows the same failure one dimension down, where it can be drawn: the nearest point of a segment is 1.252 m away, the foot of the perpendicular on its infinite line is 0.350 m away, and a solver that reports the second has not made a small error — it has reported a point that is not on the simplex at all.

Slivers are not an edge case in GJK. They are where the algorithm spends its time. The search's entire job is to drive the simplex onto the closest feature, so by the last two iterations of every query the triangle is as thin as the problem allows and the origin is a fraction of a millimetre from it. The failure mode lives exactly where the algorithm converges, which is why it survives any test suite written against uniform random data.

§C.5 measures both populations, 200,000 triangles each, counting how often the textbook version claims a point closer than ours — which on a triangle is only possible if its point is not on the triangle: 

  C.5  FAT triangles, origin anywhere, 200000 of them:
       textbook answer claims to beat ours  3
       worst relative               4.1796e-03

  C.5  SLIVERS with the origin 10um-10mm away, 200000 of them:
       textbook answer claims to beat ours  1048
       worst relative               1.4540e-01

Three failures on fat triangles and 1,048 on slivers, with a worst case 14.5% wrong. And §C.6 prices the alternative: computing all four candidates costs 21.7 ns per triangle, against roughly 115 ns for a whole box-against-box query of which the solver runs two or three times. Refusing to decide costs a few percent of a collision test and buys an answer that cannot be wrong.

7.3 The tetrahedron, and the one thing that must be decided

A tetrahedron is four triangles plus one question the triangles cannot answer: is the origin inside? If it is, the distance is zero and the search is over. If it is not, the nearest point is on one of the four faces and §7.1 finds it.

Containment is decided by asking, for each face, whether the origin is on the same side as the fourth vertex. That is a sign comparison of two dot products — and it has exactly the conditioning problem §7.2 just described, because GJK's tetrahedra are as flat as its triangles are thin. §C.3 makes it concrete with a tetrahedron whose four vertices lie on the plane y = 1 and an origin a metre below it: the reference vertex is on every face's plane, so sp · sd is exactly zero, 0 < 0 is false, and all four faces report "not outside". Without a guard, two things a metre apart are reported as touching.

So outside_plane carries a relative degeneracy test, and it is relative for 8.4 §11's reason — a threshold with units must be scaled by something with the same units, or it means a different thing on every scene: 

const float scale = length_squared(n) * length_squared(d - a);
if (sd * sd <= 1e-12f * scale) { return true; }

Reporting "outside" for a degenerate face is both the conservative answer and the correct one: a flat tetrahedron has no interior, so nothing can be inside it, and the caller goes on to find a nearest point on the face instead of claiming contact.

7.4 The invariant that catches what the guards miss

That guard is not enough on its own, and the thing that saves the algorithm is not another guard but a theorem you can check.

After a reduction, the simplex is the minimal face containing its own nearest point, so the reduced simplex and the full one have the same v. The next iteration adds w to that reduced simplex, and the new simplex therefore contains the old one. The nearest point of a superset cannot be farther away than the nearest point of a subset. So:

|v| is monotonically non-increasing for the whole search, by construction. A step that fails to decrease it cannot be the algorithm converging slowly. It is float arithmetic having run out of room, and one more pass will produce the same non-decrease for ever.

Two lines test it, and they catch two completely different failures.

The first is the loop cycling, and it happens on ordinary input rather than adversarial. Two 3 m boxes 2 mm apart reached the 64-iteration cap with the distance already correct to six digits, because the termination test's threshold falls as the gap squared while its rounding error falls only as the gap — §11 measures that floor exactly.

The second is the simplex solver going backwards, which is how the sliver problem was found in the first place. A sphere against a turned box: eighteen iterations converging cleanly toward 0.0233827 m against a true 0.0233828, and then one reduction returning 0.0674522 — three times too far, from a triangle two of whose vertices agreed to six digits. The monotonicity check noticed, restored the last good state, and reported the answer it already had.

An invariant you can check is worth more than one you believe, and this one costs a comparison and a 160-byte copy against two support calls.

7.5 And a proof outranks a decision

There is one more place the same idea applies, and it is the reason 8.4's certificate habit was worth forming. §6.1 noted that a single positive dot(v, w) is a complete proof that the shapes are apart: the supporting plane with normal v̂ has the whole difference set strictly beyond it, and the origin is not.

Now consider what happens when a later, flatter tetrahedron's sign test reports "the origin is inside". Those two claims cannot both be true, and they are not equally trustworthy: one is a theorem about a maximum, the other is the sign of a signed volume computed on a degenerate shape. So the loop banks the best lower bound it has proven and refuses any containment that contradicts it: 

if (reduce_simplex(s, v, weights))
{
    if (lower2 > tol * tol * scale2)
    {
        s = previous;
        v = previous_v;
        for (int i = 0; i < 4; ++i) { weights[i] = previous_weights[i]; }
        r.stalled = true;
        break;
    }

    r.status = gjk_status::intersecting;
    r.terminal = s;
    return r;
}

Measured on the case that motivated it: a sphere and a turned box 55 mm apart, fourteen clean iterations converging to 0.0553297 m, and then a tetrahedron whose four face tests all reported containment — turning a 55 mm gap into a contact. The proof was already in hand, and a proof does not become false because a later determinant changed sign.

8. Implementation

Three new files and a handful of edits. The order matters: the interface first, because it is the piece that decides what the algorithm is allowed to know.

8.1 convex.hpp: a shape, reduced to one question

GJK needs to ask two shapes for support points and nothing else. The C++ question is how to hand it two different shape types without teaching it what they are. Four answers were available and three of them are wrong for this engine:

  • A virtual base class. Ruled out by 5.2: no RTTI and no virtual dispatch in the engine core.
  • A template. GJK would live in a header and be instantiated once per pair of shape types — ten instantiations for four shapes, growing as the square, and a hundred-line algorithm recompiled into every translation unit that touches physics.
  • A switch on shape_kind, like 8.4's. Workable, and it means every new shape edits the algorithm. A hull is not a shape_kind (§8.2 explains why), so this one cannot reach it at all.
  • A function pointer plus a context pointer. Which is what 6.17's frame graph chose, and chose with a number.
struct convex
{
    using support_fn = vec3 (*)(const void* data, vec3 d);

    support_fn support = nullptr;
    const void* data = nullptr;
    vec3 origin{};

    [[nodiscard]] vec3 world_support(vec3 d) const { return origin + support(data, d); }
};

Twenty-four bytes, no allocation, no vtable, and one indirect call per support query. §I.3 prices it: 1.908 ns direct against 1.906 ns through the pointer, with both arms producing bit-identical floats. It is a tie, and the reason is structural rather than lucky — within one query the target never changes, so the branch predictor learns it on the first call and is right for the rest.

The adapters use a C++ feature worth naming the first time it appears: a captureless lambda converts to a plain function pointer. A lambda with no captures has no state, so the language lets it decay to vec3(*)(const void*, vec3), and the leading + forces that conversion: 

[[nodiscard]] inline convex as_convex(const obb& box)
{
    return convex{
        +[](const void* data, vec3 d) { return support_local(*static_cast<const obb*>(data), d); },
        &box,
        box.centre,
    };
}

That is how a modern C++ codebase writes a C-style thunk without writing one. And because convex holds a pointer it does not own, it is a view, with the same contract std::span has: it must not outlive what it views. The failure mode is a temporary, and it is worth turning into a compile error rather than a Tuesday afternoon: 

convex as_convex(obb&&) = delete;
convex as_convex(engine::sphere&&) = delete;
convex as_convex(capsule&&) = delete;
convex as_convex(hull&&) = delete;

An rvalue argument prefers the && overload, which is deleted, so as_convex(world_obb(s, p, q)) does not compile. An lvalue cannot bind to && at all, so the ordinary case is untouched. Four deleted functions, no runtime cost, and a class of dangling-pointer bug that now names its own line number.

8.2 support_local, and why the centre is kept out of it

Notice that convex::support returns a point relative to origin rather than a world point. That split is numerical, and it is 8.4 §11's finding turned into an interface. §12 measures what it buys; the short version is that a box's support point is centre + axes·s — three additions — and doing them in world space rounds each one to half an ulp of the position. GJK does two of those per iteration and then subtracts them, so it would pay the cancellation once per step rather than once per query.

So shape.cpp grows a support_local for each primitive that never forms the centre at all, and the old support becomes a one-line wrapper: 

vec3 support_local(const obb& box, vec3 d)
{
    const float sx = (dot(box.axes.c0, d) >= 0.0f) ? box.half_extents.x : -box.half_extents.x;
    const float sy = (dot(box.axes.c1, d) >= 0.0f) ? box.half_extents.y : -box.half_extents.y;
    const float sz = (dot(box.axes.c2, d) >= 0.0f) ? box.half_extents.z : -box.half_extents.z;
    return box.axes.c0 * sx + box.axes.c1 * sy + box.axes.c2 * sz;
}

vec3 support(const obb& box, vec3 d)
{
    return box.centre + support_local(box, d);
}

And gjk.cpp forms the one large subtraction exactly once, outside the loop: 

const vec3 delta = b.origin - a.origin;

8.3 Two new shapes, to prove the point

An interface that claims to work on any convex shape should be handed a shape the previous lesson could not test. shape.hpp gains two.

A capsule joins shape_kind, because it is fixed-size data — a radius and a half-height — and because 8.3 already shipped inertia_capsule, so it arrives able to simulate as well as collide. The half-height is half the segment, not half the capsule, for the same reason half_extents are halves: it is what appears in the support function.

A hull does not join shape_kind, and the reason is worth stating because it is a physical-design decision rather than an oversight: 

struct hull
{
    vec3 centre{};                       ///< World space.
    mat3 axes = mat3::identity();        ///< Columns are the unit body axes in world space.
    std::span<const vec3> points{};      ///< Body axes, relative to `centre`.
};

A shape is a 24-byte value that Module 9 will write to a save file, and a pointer is not a value. A hull's geometry is an asset: loaded once, shared by every instance, outliving any particular placement, exactly like a mesh. There is a second reason and it is sharper — inertia_of(shape) would have to answer for a hull, and it cannot: the tensor of a point set needs faces, a point set has none, and a function that returns a plausible wrong answer is worse than one that does not exist. §14 says so out loud.

Its support function is a brute-force scan, and that is a decision rather than a placeholder: 

vec3 support_local(const hull& h, vec3 d)
{
    if (h.points.empty()) { return vec3{}; }

    const vec3 dl = transpose(h.axes) * d;

    const vec3* best = &h.points[0];
    float best_dot = dot(*best, dl);
    for (const vec3& p : h.points)
    {
        const float v = dot(p, dl);
        if (v > best_dot) { best_dot = v; best = &p; }
    }
    return h.axes * *best;
}

One change of basis, not one per vertex: dot(axes·p, d) == dot(p, axesᵀ·d) because the axes are orthonormal and a rotation's inverse is its transpose — 2.5's identity, doing real work. So the loop is n dot products rather than n matrix-vector products, and the single winner is carried back out.

8.4 The loop, and the four ways it can stop

With all of that in place the search itself is short. The exits are the interesting part, and there are four:

  1. The bounds met — the answer is proven to the tolerance. The normal finish.
  2. The origin is enclosed — reduce_simplex reports containment and no lower bound contradicts it. The shapes overlap, and 8.6 takes over from the terminal simplex.
  3. No progress — either the support function returned a vertex the simplex already has, or |v| failed to decrease. On a polytope the first is how the search finishes exactly; on a curved shape both mean float has run out.
  4. The iteration cap — measured at zero firings in 200,000 capsule pairs even at tolerance 1e−7, and still the only reason this function returns at all on a curved shape.

The duplicate test in (3) deserves one note, because its threshold is not the user's tolerance and the first draft made it so: 

constexpr float k_duplicate_rel2 = 1e-12f;

A polytope's support function returns the same vertex when there is nothing better, so an exact-repeat test is all that is needed. A capsule's moves continuously with the direction, so two consecutive iterations produce points a fraction of a millimetre apart — "duplicates" by a 1e−4 relative measure — and the loop exited before the bounds had converged. Measured: capsule gaps reported with a certificate slack of 2.4% where the tolerance promised 0.01%, on results that were not flagged as stalled and therefore looked trustworthy. The threshold belongs at float's own resolution, where an exact repeat still trips it and a genuine small step does not.

8.5 The façade

collide.hpp gains one overload, because a caller wants one function: 

[[nodiscard]] separation collide(const convex& a, const convex& b);

It returns the same separation as every other test in that file, so a caller can swap the specialised test for the general one without touching anything downstream. Three fields are filled differently and the header states the contract: axis_index is −3 so source_of reports witness; axes_tested is the GJK iteration count rather than a candidate count — same meaning, different unit; and depth is exact when negative and a placeholder when not. A gap is the true distance. An overlap reports +0, because GJK cannot measure penetration: the origin is inside A ⊖ B and depth is a distance to its boundary, which the search never looks at.

That is the honest state of the engine after this lesson, and naming it is the point. The general test answers the question the specialised one could not — how far apart, exactly — and cannot yet answer the one it could.

One small thing the compiler could not have caught. The first draft gave GJK axis_index = −2, which read naturally next to −1 for radial. But collide.cpp already had constexpr int k_no_axis = −2, and source_of mapped it to none by falling off the end of the function rather than through a named case. A sentinel only reachable by fallthrough is a sentinel waiting to be reused by mistake. GJK is −3, and both values are named cases now.

9. What 8.6 will actually be handed

Every description of the Expanding Polytope Algorithm — 8.6's subject — begins the same way: start from the tetrahedron GJK terminated with. Before writing a lesson on that premise it seemed worth checking that GJK produces one.

§C.4 takes the arrangement 8.4 §F already identified as the commonest thing a game ever produces: two objects standing on the same floor, so their up axes are parallel whatever their yaw. A hundred thousand such pairs, unit cubes, centres within 1.2 m of each other, so most of them overlap.

Not one tetrahedron. Zero, out of a hundred thousand — including all 65,633 pairs overlapping by more than 10 cm. Tilt one box by a single degree and 77,326 appear.

The cause is not subtle once you look for it. Both boxes have the same up axis, so their y half-extents multiply the same world direction, and support_local chooses the same sign on that axis for both — the tie-break is >= 0, so a zero component picks + deterministically for either box. Every vertex of the difference set therefore comes out with

w.y = pa.y − pb.y − delta.y = (+h) − (+h) − 0 = 0

exactly. All 100,000 simplices lay entirely in the plane y = 0, and a search confined to a plane cannot build a tetrahedron. The difference set is perfectly three-dimensional; it is the search that never leaves its equator, because every direction it asks about is itself in that plane. Preset 3 of the demo shows it happening, one keystroke at a time.

This is 8.4 §F's finding from the other side. There, a shared axis made one of the nine candidate cross products degenerate and the folklore about guarding it turned out to be about a different formulation. Here, the same shared axis collapses the entire search by a dimension. A shared axis is not a coincidence in a game — it is what "standing on the floor" means — and every algorithm in this module has to survive it.

So 8.6 will have to build its own starting polytope rather than inherit one. That is what real EPA implementations do, and it is usually presented as defensive programming for a rare case. It is not rare. It is the default.

10. Polytopes terminate, curves converge

GJK is usually described as an iterative algorithm that converges to the distance. That is half true, and the half that is false explains where the cost goes.

For a polytope — a shape with finitely many vertices — GJK does not converge. It finishes. The simplex can only ever improve, there are finitely many vertices to improve onto, so after a bounded number of steps the support function returns a vertex the simplex already has and there is nothing further out to find. The tolerance is never what stopped it.

For a shape with curvature there is no last vertex, and the search genuinely converges: every decade of tolerance costs more work, for ever.

A sphere is the most curved shape there is and it costs one iteration. The reason is a small piece of geometry: the nearest point of a ball to an exterior point lies on the line joining that point to the centre, so the very first support call — taken in direction delta, which is that line — lands exactly on the answer. The difference of two spheres is another ball, so the same argument covers the pair. GJK does not iterate on a sphere because there is nothing to iterate toward.

Only the capsule genuinely converges, at almost exactly one extra pass per decade of tolerance — 2.06 → 7.43 over five decades. And that fits the shape: the difference of two capsules is (segment ⊖ segment) ⊕ ball, a parallelogram rounded by a ball, so the nearest point can sit on the curved part where neither operand decides the answer alone. A sphere's curvature is trivial because it has no other feature; a capsule's is not.

The error column tells the same story from the other direction. §F.3 measures GJK against a closed form and the numbers go flat at 2.2e−07 relative — float's noise floor — rather than at the tolerance. The only row where the tolerance is visible is 1e−2 on the boxes, at 1.8e−4, which is exactly what a 1e−2 relative tolerance permits. For everything tighter than that, the tolerance is not what you are buying.

Why an engine keeps specialised tests anyway. Sphere-against-sphere is one GJK iteration and two support calls, or it is one subtraction and one comparison in 8.4's collide. Generality is worth paying for where it buys something; where a closed form exists it buys nothing. 8.8's broadphase will reach for the cheap test first whatever pair of shapes it is holding.

11. The resolution floor, and the margin it implies

§10 said the error goes flat at float's noise floor. That is true when the gap is comfortable. When the gap gets small relative to the shapes, two separate things give out — at two different gaps — and keeping them apart is the whole of this section.

11.1 The proof goes first

The termination test asks whether |v|² − dot(v, w) ≤ tol · |v|². Look at how the two sides behave as the gap shrinks. The right-hand side is proportional to |v|² — the gap squared. The left-hand side is a difference of two products of |v| with |w|, so its rounding error is of order ε·|v|·|w| — the gap times the shape. One falls quadratically and the other linearly, so they cross:

|v| ≈ ε · |w| / tol

For ε = 1.19e−07, a difference set about 4 m across and the default tol = 1e−4, that is 4.8e−03 m — and the stall column of §F.5 turns over right there. Below it the comparison is noise against noise, and the loop stops because |v| stopped decreasing rather than because the bounds met.

What is lost is the certificate, not the answer. A stalled result still reports |pA − pB| for two real surface points, so it is still a valid upper bound — the error has a direction, and it is the safe one. §A.5 checks exactly that over 36,000 sphere-against-box pairs: worst shortfall below the true distance, 8.5e−06 m, which is 8.4's own float noise on the reference side.

11.2 The answer goes second, and that one is deliberate

Further down, GJK stops calling the shapes apart at all. That is a different test: 

if (v2 <= tol * tol * scale2 && lower2 <= tol * tol * scale2)
{
    r.status = gjk_status::intersecting;

which is |v| ≤ tol · |w| — a contact margin, and a deliberate one. §F.6 measures where it sits by bisection, on cubes spanning four decades of size: 

  F.6  side S    margin (m)   margin / (tol * S)
           0.1   2.1115e-05   2.111
           1.0   2.1116e-04   2.112
          10.0   2.1120e-03   2.112
         100.0   2.1102e-02   2.110
        1000.0   2.1102e-01   2.110

A fixed multiple of tolerance times size, across four decades. That last column is the finding: tolerance is not a distance and must not be set as one. It is a relative quantity, which is exactly what makes one default usable on a marble and on a cathedral — and what makes "set the tolerance to a millimetre" a category error. If you want a margin in metres, divide by the size of the shapes you are testing.

Every physics engine has a margin, and this is where ours comes from. A contact detected only at exactly zero distance is a contact detected one frame too late, and 8.7's manifolds will deliberately keep contacts that are within a margin rather than touching, so the solver has something to work with before interpenetration happens. The margin arriving out of the tolerance rather than out of a separate constant is a small piece of luck; the important part is knowing it is there and knowing its units.

12. Where the origin is

8.4 §11 ended on a wall: two boxes a millimetre apart, measured a hundred kilometres from the world origin, reported as touching — because b.centre − a.centre subtracts two world-sized floats to produce a crate-sized one, and the information was gone before collide was called. The section named the disease and said every quantity in a collision test should be a difference taken as early as possible.

GJK would inherit that intact and make it worse, because it forms support_A(d) − support_B(−d) on every iteration. So this lesson ships the cure, and then measures it — which is the only way to find out whether a cure is a cure.

The experiment is one pair of boxes at a fixed diagonal offset, walked away from the origin along (1,1,1)/√3. The distance between them is the same at every stop by construction, so any change in the reported number is arithmetic rather than geometry — and because the boxes are axis aligned there is an exact reference: three one-dimensional gaps and a square root, evaluated in double from the boxes as float actually stores them.

Two arms, and they are not two implementations. gjk.cpp is the same code in both columns; only the convex view differs. The relative arm hands it support points measured from each shape's own centre and one delta formed once; the naive arm hands it world support points and a zero origin, so every iteration subtracts two world-sized numbers. Making the bad version an adapter rather than a fork is what makes the comparison mean anything — 8.4 §3's lesson about harnesses that construct their own configuration, applied on purpose this time.

A centimetre of error at a thousand kilometres, against a tenth of a micron: 96,270 times better. And §H.2 shows it is not luck — the naive column's error tracks ulp(position) at a fixed fraction all the way up, while the relative column's tracks nothing at all and stays at the shape's own resolution wherever the shapes happen to be.

The mechanism is countable. A box's support point is centre + axes·s, three additions. In world space each rounds to half an ulp of the position, and there are two support points per iteration — six world-sized additions per pass. Taken relative they round to half an ulp of the shape, and the one world-sized subtraction is b.origin − a.origin, which is exact whenever the two are within a factor of two of each other. That is Sterbenz's lemma, and two things about to collide always are.

Note carefully what the reference is, and is not. It is the distance between the boxes float holds, not the distance between the boxes the program meant. Those diverge too, and no arrangement of the arithmetic can fix it: at a thousand kilometres a float position cannot name a centimetre, so the shapes are not where you put them before any algorithm runs. What the relative form buys is that GJK adds nothing of its own to a degradation the input already has — which is everything an algorithm can buy, and exactly the point 8.4 §11 reached from the other side. The real fix for a large world is to stop storing positions as single-precision absolutes, and that is a Module 9 conversation.

       collide  (SAT, MTV)       49.477
       gjk_intersects (bool)     73.223   8.68x
       gjk_distance (metres)    114.744   2.32x

GJK is slower on boxes, and it should be. The SAT knows the shape; GJK searches for it. Fifteen dot-product tests with an early exit beat a loop that calls out through a function pointer six times and runs a four-candidate simplex solver two or three times. Nothing about that is surprising and nothing about it is a problem — what GJK buys is the metres and the generality, not the speed, which is precisely why an engine keeps both tests rather than replacing one with the other.

The right panel prices generality where it actually costs something. A hull's support function is linear in its vertex count, as a brute-force scan must be: 3.9 ns at 8 vertices, 51.5 ns at 128, and gjk_distance is that line multiplied by roughly twice the iteration count. Hill climbing over an adjacency list would bend that toward √n; §14 says why we have not built it.

And the abstraction itself is free. §I.3 times the same support call directly and through convex::support: 1.908 ns against 1.906 ns, with the two arms producing bit-identical floats — a check worth printing, because an arm that is faster because it did less has not won a comparison.

14. What this does not do

Five things, named rather than discovered later.

No penetration depth. When the shapes overlap, GJK stops with the origin inside the difference set and has nothing more to say. Depth is a distance to the boundary of that set, and the search only ever looks inward. That is 8.6, and gjk_result carries the terminal simplex rather than discarding it precisely so that 8.6 has somewhere to start — though §9 is the warning that what it gets handed is often a triangle.

No contact manifold. Two witness points are one contact. A box resting on a plane needs four, and needs them to be the same four next frame or it will wobble. That is 8.7.

No hull builder. hull takes a point set and computes the support function of its convex hull for free, so passing a cloud works — but nothing here computes the hull itself, which would be a quickhull implementation and an asset-pipeline conversation rather than a physics one. The consequence is that a hull has no inertia tensor: the divergence-theorem sum needs faces, and a point set has none. A hull in this engine can collide and cannot yet be simulated, which is why 8.12's ragdolls will be built from capsules.

No hill climbing. A support query over an adjacency list starts from the previous answer and walks downhill, turning O(n) into roughly O(√n). §13 measured the crossover to be beyond where hand-authored colliders live, and the data structure it needs does not exist in this engine. If a hull ever arrives with hundreds of vertices, this is the first thing to build.

No swept or continuous test. GJK answers a question about two shapes at one instant. A fast-moving object still passes through a wall between frames — 8.4 §9's tunnelling case, unfixed. The distance this lesson computes is one of the two ingredients of a conservative-advancement time-of-impact test, and the other is a bound on closing speed; the combination is out of scope here and is named on 8.13's list, where a character controller needs it most.

17. Common pitfalls & debugging

The distance is right but the direction is backwards

Symptom: objects are pushed together instead of apart, which looks like a solver instability rather than a sign error.
Cause: v runs from B's witness to A's, because v = pA − pB. The axis pointing from a toward b — conventions.html §9e, unchanged since 8.4 — is therefore −v̂.
Fix: the negation in gjk_distance's last lines is deliberate. Check it by translating b by +direction · distance and re-testing: the shapes should touch, not separate further.

The loop never terminates on one particular pair

Symptom: a hang, or an iteration counter pinned at the cap, always on the same two objects.
Cause: almost always the gap being tiny compared with the shapes — §11.1's floor — or a simplex solver returning a point that is not on the simplex.
Fix: the monotonicity check (§7.4). Print |v| per iteration before doing anything else; if it goes up, the solver is the problem and no amount of tolerance tuning will help. If it merely stops going down, you are at the floor and the answer you already have is correct.

Two shapes a centimetre apart are reported as touching

Symptom: phantom contacts, worse on large objects.
Cause: the contact margin is tolerance × |w|, so it scales with the shapes. On a 100 m object the default margin is about 2 cm.
Fix: this is working as designed (§11.2). If you need a tighter margin on large shapes, tighten gjk_config::tolerance — and read §10 first, because on a curved shape that costs iterations, and on a polytope it costs nothing because the tolerance was never what stopped the search.

A convex gives garbage after a few frames

Symptom: distances that are usually right and occasionally absurd.
Cause: the view outlived what it viewed — the classic being a std::vector of hull points that reallocated while a hull held a span into it.
Fix: the deleted rvalue overloads catch the temporary case at compile time. They cannot catch a reallocation, so a hull's points belong in storage that outlives every query against them; the demo keeps them in a member vector that is only rebuilt on reset().

The answer is fine near the origin and wrong far from it

Symptom: a level that collides correctly near its centre and produces jittery, imprecise contacts at its edges.
Cause: §12. If you wrote your own convex adapter and returned world support points with a zero origin, you have built the naive arm.
Fix: return the support point relative to a point inside the shape, and put that point in origin. Test it by translating both shapes by a kilometre and checking the answer does not move.

EPA has nothing to expand

Symptom: (a preview of 8.6) a penetration solver that works on tumbling debris and fails on a crate resting on a floor.
Cause: §9 — a shared up axis confines the search to a plane, so terminal.count is 3 rather than 4 on precisely the arrangement that matters.
Fix: build the starting polytope rather than assuming one. It is on 8.6's critical path, not its error path.

18. Exercises

  1. Run the algorithm by hand. Take A = [3, 5] × [1, 2] and B = [8, 11] × [4, 6] — two axis-aligned rectangles in the plane. Write down the four vertices of each, compute the sixteen pairwise differences, and hull them by inspection. Then run GJK on that set with a pencil, starting from the support point in direction delta, and record |v| and dot(v, w)/|v| at each step until they meet.

    Hint: the difference of two axis-aligned rectangles is another axis-aligned rectangle, which makes the hull step trivial and the answer checkable against the two-interval formula. The true distance is √(3² + 2²).

  2. Add a cylinder. A cylinder is disc ⊕ segment, so its support function is two terms, like the capsule's — but the disc's term is a projection of d onto the plane perpendicular to the axis, normalised and scaled by the radius. Write support_local(const cylinder&, vec3), add an as_convex overload with its deleted rvalue partner, and check it against a dense sampling of the surface the way §B.2 checks the capsule.

    Hint: the degenerate case is a direction exactly along the axis, where the perpendicular component is zero and there is no preferred point on the rim. Return any rim point — the value is a tie, so only reproducibility is at stake. Then ask yourself why 8.4 could not have added a cylinder at all.

  3. Measure the seed. §6.4 claims the initial direction is worth 22% of the iteration count. Verify it: add a gjk_config whose initial_direction is −delta rather than +delta, run it over the same population as §F.1, and compare the histograms. Then explain, in one sentence, why the sphere column in §F.2 moves from 1.00 to 2.00 under that change and the box column moves by only about one.

    Hint: for a ball the first support point in the right direction is the answer; for a box it is a vertex that may or may not be the nearest feature. One seed wastes exactly one iteration; the other wastes one and then still has work to do.

  4. Break the simplex solver on purpose. Replace reduce_triangle with the textbook Voronoi-region version reproduced in §C.5 of the harness, run the full verify, and record which sections fail and which do not. Then turn off the monotonicity check in gjk_distance and run it again.

    Hint: expect §C.2 and §E to complain and §A.3 to stay green, because an AABB pair rarely produces a sliver. The pair of runs is the point: the second one shows how much of the damage the invariant was absorbing silently, which is the argument for having invariants you can check.

  5. Engine challenge: conservative advancement. GJK gives a distance; a time-of-impact test needs to know when that distance reaches zero. Implement the simplest form: given two bodies with constant linear velocities over a step, repeatedly compute the distance d and the closing speed along the reported direction, advance time by d / closing_speed, and iterate until the distance is within the contact margin or the step is exhausted. Test it on a bullet fired at a wall thin enough for 8.4 §9's tunnelling case.

    Hint: the step is conservative because nothing can close faster than the maximum closing speed, so advancing by d / vmax can never step past the impact. Rotation makes vmax harder — you need a bound involving the angular velocity and the shape's bounding radius — so start with translation only and say so in the doc comment. Then notice which of this lesson's guarantees you are relying on: the distance must be an upper bound for the advance to stay conservative, which §11.1 measured and §A.5 checked.

19. Recap & next

The engine can now measure the distance between any two convex shapes and say where. Not a bound on it — the distance, with two surface points that realise it and two bounds that prove it. Box, sphere, capsule, arbitrary point set, in any of the ten pairings, through one function that names none of them.

The pieces, in the order they were needed:

  • The Minkowski difference turns a question about a pair into a question about a point, through two equivalences that each take two lines to prove.
  • The support function determines a convex set, so one function pointer is a complete shape interface — and the difference set's support function is two calls to its parents', which is what makes the whole thing computable.
  • GJK descends with a simplex, carrying an upper bound that only falls and a lower bound that only rises, so its answer arrives with a proof attached.
  • The simplex solver computes four candidates rather than choosing one, because the shortcut everyone copies fails exactly where the algorithm spends its time — 1,048 failures in 200,000 slivers, worst case 14.5% wrong.
  • A monotone invariant does double duty, terminating the loop and catching a solver that goes backwards; and a proven lower bound outranks a containment test computed on a degenerate tetrahedron.

And five measurements worth carrying forward. GJK finishes on polytopes and only converges on curves, so a capsule costs one pass per decade of tolerance and a box costs none. The tolerance is a relative quantity that implies a contact margin of 2.11 × tol × size across four decades, which means it is not a distance and must not be set as one. The certificate gives out before the answer does, at ε·|w|/tol, and a stalled answer is still an upper bound. Taking support points relative to each shape's own centre makes the error 96,270× smaller a thousand kilometres from the origin. And two boxes on a floor produce a search trapped in a plane, so the tetrahedron everyone assumes GJK leaves behind is absent from 100,000 consecutive queries.

Next: 8.6 asks the question this lesson refused. When the origin is inside the difference set, how far inside, and which way out? That is a distance to the set's boundary rather than to its interior, so the search has to run the other way — outward, expanding a polytope until it reaches the surface. The Expanding Polytope Algorithm starts from the simplex GJK left behind, and §9 is the reason its first job is to build one.
