Module 6 — Advanced Rendering · Lesson 6.12

HDR and Tonemapping

This engine does not have an HDR bug. It has been avoiding the question by construction, which is a different thing and worth seeing clearly before anything is built. Two choices have been holding the lid on: Lesson 6.2 set k_reference_irradiance to π precisely so a white surface renders at exactly 1.0, and the demo's roughness is 0.49 — which peaks at 0.8676, just under. Change the second number and the lid comes off at once: the same shading equation returns 11.59 at roughness 0.20 and 55,917 for a polished metal, and to_encoded stores every one of those as code 255. Not "a bit too bright" — the same code for all of them, with the highlight's entire shape replaced by a constant. Fixing it needs a buffer that can hold the values, an exposure that decides what maps to white, a curve that brings the range down, and — the part that is architecture rather than arithmetic — a second render pass.

1The problem: a lid, and what has been holding it on

Two lessons in this module left a note saying this one would arrive. It is worth reading them before reading any code, because between them they say exactly what is wrong and exactly why nobody has noticed.

The first is in light.hpp, beside the constant that sets a light's brightness:

…so it renders at exactly 1.0 when E_perp is pi. That is the physical meaning of "correctly exposed" for this engine, and it is the same convention a photographer's white card encodes. It is a choice, not a law — Lesson 6.12 replaces it with a real exposure control and a tonemapper, at which point lights get authored in lux and this constant becomes the default rather than the rule.

The second is forty lines further down, beside shade_encoded:

to_encoded clamps, which is where an over-bright result gets clipped to white… A specular highlight is the first thing in this engine that routinely exceeds 1.0, so the clamp stops being theoretical here: a bright highlight arrives at the screen as a flat white blob with its shape clipped off, which is exactly the information tonemapping exists to keep.

So why has nothing looked wrong?

Because of the first note. k_reference_irradiance = π was chosen so that a white Lambertian surface lit square-on renders at exactly 1.0 — and then the demo's materials came in at roughness 0.49, whose specular peak at the mirror angle is 0.8676.

The engine has been living just under the lid, on purpose, for six modules.

That is not a criticism of either decision. It is the observation that makes the rest of the lesson measurable: change one number and the situation changes completely, and we can say by how much.

Worked example. One material, five roughnesses, evaluated at the light's actual mirror direction (verify_612 §A).

Fifteen and a half stops of range, stored as one number. And note what the last column is not saying: this is not a rounding error at the margin. Above 1.0 the clamp is total — 1.0, 4.0 and 55,917 are the same pixel.

The same problem wearing a suit: the units debt

Lesson 6.2 renamed intensity to irradiance and made a point of why: "intensity meant nothing: it was a number you turned up until the picture looked right. irradiance is a measurable physical quantity." It was right, and it was incomplete — because having made the field measurable, the engine could not then let anybody author a measurement into it.

Full daylight is about 120,000 lux. Put that number into directional_light::irradiance and the polished surface above returns 8,333,875, which is code 255, as is every other pixel in the scene.

You cannot author lights in physical units until something downstream decides what maps to white. That something is an exposure control, and its absence is why k_reference_irradiance had to exist.

2Building intuition: a camera, not a clamp

The real world has an enormous range of luminance in it — starlight to direct sun is something like twenty stops — and every device that has ever produced an image of it has had a much smaller range to work with. Film, paper, a phone screen: all of them narrow. So the problem is not new, and the solution is not new either.

A camera does exactly two things about it, and they are separate things:

  1. An exposure decides which part of the range you are interested in. Shutter speed, aperture, ISO — three dials that combine into one number, and what that number says is "this much light shall be white". Point a camera at a dim room and at a beach and you change the exposure, not the film.
  2. A response curve decides what happens to the light that is still too bright. Film does not clip; it rolls off, compressing the highlights into a shoulder so that a bright sky retains some structure rather than becoming a white hole.

Those are the two halves of this lesson, and keeping them separate is more than tidiness — they answer different questions, they are set by different people (an exposure is a shot decision, a curve is a look decision), and §3.5 shows that performing them in the wrong order destroys the ability of the first to do its job.

What "clamping" actually is

It is worth naming the status quo in the same vocabulary, because it makes the gap obvious: min(x, 1) is a response curve. A very bad one — it is the curve that maps everything above white to white — but it is a member of the same family, and treating it as one is how the lesson keeps it honest. That is why tonemap::clamp is a named operator in the code rather than the absence of one.

And one thing this is not about

"HDR" means two different things, and this lesson is only one of them

HDR rendering — this lesson — means computing and storing light values without an artificial ceiling, then mapping them down for a display. The display is an ordinary one; the pipeline is what changed.

HDR display — the label on a television — means the panel itself accepts a wider range and a different transfer function (PQ or HLG rather than sRGB), so the mapping down is to a bigger target. SDL exposes this: gpu_device's report carries an SDL_GPUSwapchainComposition, and Lesson 6.1 logged it.

The first is a prerequisite for the second, and only the first changes how you render. This lesson does the first and leaves the second as an exercise, because the interesting part of it — which is colour volume and metadata, not range — is a different subject.

3The theory

3.1   Exposure, in units somebody can talk about

An exposure is a multiplier applied before the curve. You could expose a scene by typing multipliers — 0.5, 0.001, 3 — and a great many engines have. The reason not to is that nobody can reason about them, and there is a scale that a century of photography has already agreed on.

EV is a logarithmic scale of light, and one EV — one stop — is a factor of two.

The conversion from an exposure value to a render-time multiplier is standardised rather than derived, and it is worth being precise about which part is which:

\[ L_{\max} = 1.2 \cdot 2^{\text{EV}_{100}} \qquad \text{exposure} = \frac{1}{L_{\max}} \]
max_luminance = 1.2 × 2^EV100 exposure = 1 / max_luminance

The 2EV is the definition of a stop and is not negotiable. The 1.2 is not physics: it is 78 / (q × 100) with q = 0.65 from ISO 12232's saturation-based speed definition — a calibration of real camera hardware. This course takes it as given and says so; Lagarde and de Rousiers' Moving Frostbite to PBR §5.1 works it through properly. Quoting a constant and saying that you are quoting it is a different act from presenting it as a derivation.

Worked example, and it is the one that matters. What EV is the engine already using?

k_reference_irradiance = π was chosen so a white surface renders at exactly 1.0. "1.0 maps to white" is precisely "L_max = 1", so:

1 = 1.2 × 2^EV → EV = log2(1 / 1.2) = −0.2630

The harness confirms that exposure_from_ev100(k_reference_ev100) returns 1.0000000. And that is the sentence worth taking away:

Lesson 6.2's choice was an exposure setting. It was simply the only one available.

Which turns this lesson from a replacement into a generalisation — the old behaviour is a point on the new scale, not a special case beside it. Three real settings for calibration: a dim room is EV 3 (white at 9.6), an overcast day is EV 12 (white at 4,915), full daylight is EV 15 (white at 39,322).

3.2   What makes a curve legal

Now the second half. We need a function from [0, ∞) to [0, 1], and before choosing one it is worth writing down what a candidate has to satisfy — because these three properties are what separate an operator from a preference, and every one of them has a visible failure.

  • Monotonic. Brighter in, brighter out. Fail this and a highlight can come out darker than its own edge, which no amount of artistic intent will make read as an image of anything.
  • Close to the identity near zero. Fail this and the dark end of every image is wrong — and the dark end is where an sRGB encode spends most of its codes, so the error lands exactly where it is most visible.
  • Bounded by 1. Fail this and it has not done its job; something downstream will clamp, and you will have a curve and a clamp.

Everything after this is taste. Here are four.

Reinhard, derived in one line

We want to divide x by something that is about 1 for small x (so the dark end is untouched) and about x for large x (so the bright end flattens). The simplest function that is both is 1 + x:

\[ f(x) = \frac{x}{1 + x} \]
f(x) = x / (1 + x)

That is the whole derivation. Check the three properties: the derivative is 1/(1+x)², positive everywhere, so monotonic; f'(0) = 1, so it is the identity to first order at the origin; and f(x) < 1 for every finite x, so it is bounded.

That last property is also its famous defect

f(x) < 1 strictly. So pure white is unreachable — and it is worth computing how unreachable, because the number is much smaller than people expect and the arithmetic is a trap this whole module keeps setting.

Code 255 needs an encoded value of at least 254.5/255 = 0.998039. Inverting the sRGB transform, that is a linear 0.995545. So:

x / (1 + x) = 0.995545 → x = 223.5

An input of 224. So an image tonemapped this way has no pure white in it at any brightness you would plausibly render, and looks washed out for exactly that reason.

This paragraph first said 768, from applying the 254.5/255 threshold in the wrong space — the same confusion between a code and a quantity of light that Lessons 1.6, 2.4, 3.9, 6.10 and 6.11 each found in a different place, committed here by the person writing the module about it. verify_612 §C measures it rather than repeating it, which is how it was caught.

The white point, also derived

So give the artist the dial the plain operator lacks: pick the value W you want to render as pure white, and require f(W) = 1 while keeping the shape. The answer is:

\[ f(x) = \frac{x\left(1 + \dfrac{x}{W^2}\right)}{1 + x} \]
f(x) = x(1 + x/W²) / (1 + x)

And the check is two lines of algebra rather than an assertion:

f(W) = W(1 + W/W²)/(1+W) = W(1 + 1/W)/(1+W) = (W+1)/(1+W) = 1

The harness drives it at W = 2, 4 and 11 and gets exactly 1.000000 each time. Note also that as W → ∞ the extra factor vanishes and it becomes plain Reinhard, which is the right behaviour for a generalisation to have.

ACES, which is a fit and not a model

The curve the industry actually ships is an approximation to the ACES reference rendering transform — five constants published by Krzysztof Narkowicz in 2015: 

return clamp((x * (2.51f * x + 0.03f)) / (x * (2.43f * x + 0.59f) + 0.14f), 0.0f, 1.0f);

There is no derivation to give, and pretending otherwise would be exactly the hand-waving this course refuses. What it has that the Reinhard family does not is a toe and a shoulder: it darkens the very bottom and rolls off the very top, which mimics film and reads as "photographic" rather than "mathematically correct".

The toe has a price, and the harness prints it: a dark value of 0.02 — code 39 — comes out at code 26. Thirteen codes darker, in the region where the eye is most sensitive. That is a real artistic choice being made on your behalf, and it is worth knowing that you are accepting it rather than discovering it later as "the shadows look crushed".

3.3   Per-channel, or on the luminance?

A curve takes one number. A colour is three. There are two defensible ways to reconcile that and they give visibly different pictures — this is a real choice, like blend_space in 2.4, not a tuning knob.

Worked example. A bright saturated orange — the colour a fire actually is — at (8.0, 2.0, 0.4), through Reinhard both ways.

per channel: (0.8889, 0.6667, 0.2857) red:blue = 3.11
luminance: (1.9230, 0.4808, 0.0962) red:blue = 20.00

The input's red:blue ratio is 20. Per-channel drops it to 3.11, because a saturating curve compresses the big channel more than the small one — so the colour moves toward white as it brightens. That is what film does, it is what people expect a highlight to look like, and it is why it is the default.

Luminance-only holds it at exactly 20, because scaling a colour by a scalar is a move along the ray from black through it, which preserves hue and saturation by construction.

And luminance-only does not solve the problem it appears to solve

Look at its output again: r = 1.9230. The curve was applied to the luminance and the luminance landed in range, but an individual channel did not — and the encode will clamp it, shifting the hue anyway, at a threshold nobody chose.

Blue is the worst case, because its luminance weight is only 0.0722. A pure blue of (0, 0, 8) has a luminance of 0.5776 — comfortably mid-range — sails through the curve, and arrives with b = 5.0710 for to_encoded to clip.

So: per-channel desaturates predictably and stays in range by construction; luminance-only preserves hue right up to the point where it does not. Neither is correct, both are defensible, and the engine exposes the choice with the first as the default. Real pipelines add a third answer — tonemap the luminance and then apply a controlled desaturation — which is a knob this course names and does not build.

3.4   The resolve, and why its three steps cannot be reordered

Each wrong ordering has its own signature, and the harness measures both.

Worked example 1: exposure after the curve.

Take (6.0, 3.0, 1.5) at exposure 0.25 through Reinhard. Done correctly — multiply, then curve — the red channel lands at code 203. Done backwards, code 128.

And the second number is not merely darker. Everything above about 4 had already been squeezed into the same place by the curve before the multiply arrived, so the exposure no longer has a range to select from — which is the whole reason it exists. An exposure applied after a compression is a brightness slider wearing an exposure's name.

Worked example 2: the curve after the encode.

Mid grey, 0.5 of the light. Curved as light: 0.5/(1.5) = 0.3333. Encode it first — code 188, which is 0.7373 as a fraction — and curve that: 0.7373/1.7373 = 0.4244.

A curve applied to codes compresses exactly what the encode had carefully spread out, and because the sRGB curve is steepest at the dark end, it crushes the shadows first. This is Lesson 1.6's rule arriving for the sixth time in this module and the last time in this course: arithmetic on light happens in linear space; the encode is the last step and happens once.

Done in the right order, the payoff is the thing the whole lesson is for. Seven stops of input, through a resolve, into eight bits, with nothing clipped: 

 0.125->94   0.250->124   0.500->156   1.000->188   2.000->213   4.000->231   8.000->242   16.000->248

3.5   What an HDR target does to Lesson 6.11's rule

Lesson 6.11 spent a section establishing that compositing must decode both operands, blend in linear light, and re-encode — and priced the round trip at 3.14× a naive byte lerp. That machinery was correct. It is also, on a float target, entirely absent.

The destination is already linear light; so is the source; so over applies directly. The harness measures one composite at 9.25 ns on a float target against 75.12 ns on 8 bits — 8.1× cheaper, and exactly correct rather than correct-after-conversion.

An HDR buffer is not only about range. It is the buffer that holds the quantity the arithmetic is defined on — and every conversion 6.11 had to perform was a symptom of storing something else.

There is a second, sharper consequence. Compositing a value of 40 at quarter coverage over a dim background gives 10.19, and the float buffer keeps it. The 8-bit path cannot reach that answer at all: 40 was already code 255 before the blend, so it would have composited 1.0 and lost a factor of forty before the operator even ran. Some pictures are not merely less accurate in 8 bits; they are unreachable.

And 6.11's _SRGB-versus-UNORM argument does not transfer

6.11 established that hardware blending on a UNORM target with a shader that encodes its own output lerps sRGB codes, at a cost of 42.2% of the light. It is worth saying plainly that this does not carry over to a float target: there is no transfer function in the ROP to be right or wrong about, because nothing in a float pipeline stores codes.

Which also settles what happens to encode_output. That flag has always meant "must this shader apply the transfer function?", and with the scene rendering to a float target the answer there is permanently no. The question has not gone away — it has moved to the pass that actually writes the display, which is the only place it was ever answerable. Same number, third reason.

3.6   Measuring a frame: why metering is done in log space

One more piece of arithmetic, because it is the foundation of auto-exposure and because the reason is not obvious. To choose an exposure automatically you need a number that says how bright the frame is. The obvious candidate — the mean luminance — is useless.

Worked example. A 100×100 frame at a uniform 0.05, with one specular pixel at 5000.

arithmetic mean = (9999 × 0.05 + 5000) / 10000 = 0.5500

That single pixel — one ten-thousandth of the frame — contributes 0.5 of the 0.55, which is ten times everything else put together. An auto-exposure driven by this number stops down by three stops because one highlight came into view.

The geometric mean — the exponential of the mean of the logs — gives:

log-average = 0.05016

It barely notices, because the log of a large number is a small number. The two disagree by 11× on the same image, and that gap is the difference between an exposure that tracks the scene and one that flickers whenever something shiny turns toward the camera.

measure() returns both, for exactly this reason — a number you can compare against a wrong one is worth more than a number on its own. It also counts pixels over the lid, which is the diagnostic that tells you whether you have an HDR problem at all: run the demo's polished scene and it reports 126 of 129,600 pixels — 0.10%. A tenth of one percent of the frame, and all of the information being destroyed is in it.

4Implementation

4.1   Two passes, and why the tonemap is not in the scene shader

The tempting shortcut is to tonemap at the end of scene.frag.hlsl, where the colour already is. It costs nothing extra. It is also wrong in three ways, each of which becomes a real limitation within two lessons.

  • An exposure derived from the frame cannot be known while the frame is being drawn. Auto-exposure reads the finished image's log-average, and that does not exist until the last triangle has landed.
  • Bloom comes before the curve (Lesson 6.13) and operates on the whole image. A per-fragment tonemap has already compressed the values bloom needs to find.
  • Blending would composite tonemapped values. over is linear in light and a curve is not linear, so f(a) over f(b) is not f(a over b). Lesson 6.11's operator would quietly stop meaning what it means.

So: the scene renders into a float target, and a second pass maps it down. That second pass is the first thing in this engine whose cost scales with pixels rather than with geometry, and it is the seed of 6.13's stack.

4.2   A full-screen triangle, from nothing

A post pass has no geometry, so binding a quad mesh would be inventing a vertex buffer for a vertex shader to ignore. Instead the corners come from SV_VertexID: 

Output main(uint vertex_id : SV_VertexID)
{
    Output o;
    o.uv = float2((vertex_id << 1) & 2, vertex_id & 2);
    o.position = float4(o.uv.x * 2.0f - 1.0f, 1.0f - o.uv.y * 2.0f, 0.0f, 1.0f);
    return o;
}

That expression gives uv (0,0), (2,0), (0,2) for ids 0, 1, 2. Mapping uv to clip space with the standard flip — x = uv.x·2 − 1, y = 1 − uv.y·2, which is conventions §6, since NDC's +y is up and a texture's v runs down — puts the corners at (−1,1), (3,1) and (−1,−3). The screen is the square from (−1,−1) to (1,1), strictly inside that triangle; everything outside is clipped by hardware at no cost.

One triangle and not two, and Lesson 4.1 is why

The obvious shape for "cover the screen" is two triangles meeting along the diagonal. It works, and it is measurably worse, for a reason this course already built the vocabulary for: fragments are shaded in aligned 2×2 quads (4.1), so along the shared diagonal every quad straddles both triangles and is issued twice, with half of each being helper lanes.

One oversized triangle has no internal edge, so nothing is shaded twice. It is a small saving and an excellent illustration of why 4.1's quad traversal was worth building: the reason to prefer one shape over another here is invisible unless you know how a rasterizer issues work.

The draw call is the whole of the geometry submission:

SDL_DrawGPUPrimitives(pass, 3, 1, 0, 0);   // three vertices, one instance, nothing bound

4.3   The pipeline, and everything it does not have

gpu_tonemap_pass::create is short, and the absences are the interesting part — each one is a sentence about what a post pass is:

  • No vertex input. gpu_mesh::describe is never called, so num_vertex_buffers and num_vertex_attributes stay zero.
  • No depth. A triangle covering the screen has nothing to be occluded by, so the pass is begun with a null depth target.
  • No blending. The resolve replaces the swapchain's contents.
  • No culling. A full-screen triangle's winding is an accident of how SV_VertexID was turned into positions, not a fact about a surface.

4.4   The target, and the flag that is easy to forget

ti.usage = SDL_GPU_TEXTUREUSAGE_COLOR_TARGET
         | (sampled ? SDL_GPU_TEXTUREUSAGE_SAMPLER : 0u);

Both usages. A scene buffer is written by one pass and read by the next, and omitting SAMPLER produces a texture that renders perfectly and reads as undefined — with no error on a release device, since Lesson 6.10 established that SDL's GPU validation lives inside if (device->debug_mode). This is the same pair create_depth(..., sampled) documents for a shadow map, arriving on the colour side.

The format is R16G16B16A16_FLOAT, and the choice is worth three sentences:

  • It holds the range. A half float reaches 65,504, and this engine's own measured peak is 55,917 — inside it, but only just, which is a useful thing to know about your content rather than a comfort.
  • Its precision is relative. Ten mantissa bits, so about three decimal digits — at every magnitude. The dark end, where the eye is most sensitive and where an sRGB encode spends most of its codes, gets the same relative accuracy as the bright end. This is the answer to "why not 16-bit fixed point": fixed point spreads its steps evenly over a range that is not perceived evenly.
  • It costs 2×, not 3×. Eight bytes a pixel against four. The CPU's hdr_buffer stores three floats and pays 3× — 6.22 MB against 2.07 MB at 960×540 — and that difference is entirely the half float.

4.5   The software path: one hoist and one branch

The CPU rasterizer needs the shading equation's answer un-encoded. The whole change is a code motion: everything in fragment's lit branch moves into its own lambda, and the encode stays behind at the boundary. 

const auto shade_lit = [&](float f0, float f1, float f2, float w_recip,
                           float& out_alpha) -> linear_rgb { /* …the hundred lines… */ };

// …and the 8-bit path becomes one line:
else if (lit)
{
    return to_encoded(shade_lit(f0, f1, f2, w_recip, out_alpha), style.encode);
}

Note what is not in shade_lit: to_encoded. That function is the one thing in this engine that routinely exceeds 1.0, and until this lesson its answer was clamped one line later, every time, with no way to ask for the real number.

The HDR fragment is then four lines, and deliberately not a second implementation: 

const auto fragment_linear = [&](float f0, float f1, float f2, float& out_alpha) -> linear_rgb {
    if (lit)
    {
        const float w_recip = 1.0f / (f0 * iw0 + f1 * iw1 + f2 * iw2);
        out_alpha = style.opacity;
        return shade_lit(f0, f1, f2, w_recip, out_alpha);
    }
    return to_linear(fragment(f0, f1, f2, out_alpha));
};

That decode in the last line is exact enough, and it is worth saying why rather than hoping: the other shading modes cannot exceed 1.0 by construction — a uv checker is two constants, a texture sample is a reflectance, an interpolated vertex colour is a weighted average of values in [0,1]. So the round trip loses at most the encode's own rounding, on values that had nowhere else to go, and it buys one implementation of four shading modes instead of two. lit is the only mode with a range to preserve, and it is the one that skips the trip.

4.6   The fill's sixth nullable pointer

fill_style::hdr is a hdr_buffer*, and null is the whole of "this fill is LDR" — the sixth field on that struct to make the bargain, after lights, albedo, normal_map, shadows and cascades. Every fill written in the previous sixty-six lessons passes null and behaves identically.

The framebuffer is still required, and not as a formality: it supplies the dimensions the bounding box is clipped against. That is exactly the situation depth_only documented in 6.8 — "the colour target still has to EXIST, because fill_triangle clips its bounding box against the framebuffer's dimensions and there is nowhere else for a pass to learn how big it is" — arriving a second time, which is a good sign that it is a real constraint rather than an accident.

In the write path it is one branch on a per-triangle constant:

if (depth_only) { /* nothing to store */ }
else if (hdr_row != nullptr) { hdr_row[x] = fragment_linear(f0, f1, f2, ignored); }
else                         { row[x]     = fragment(f0, f1, f2, ignored); }

And in the blend path, the payoff §3.5 described — no decode, no re-encode, just the operator: 

if (hdr_row != nullptr)
{
    hdr_row[x] = (style.src_storage == alpha_storage::premultiplied)
               ? over_premultiplied(lin, a, hdr_row[x])
               : over(lin, a, hdr_row[x]);
    return;
}

Note in passing that 6.11's blend_encoded failure has nowhere to live on this path. There are no stored codes to lerp. A knob that cannot express its mistake here is not being silently ignored — the mistake genuinely does not exist, which is a stronger statement than the engine has usually been able to make about one of its deliberate wrong answers.

6Build & run

# Linux / macOS
cmake -S . -B build -DCMAKE_BUILD_TYPE=RelWithDebInfo
cmake --build build -j

# Windows (MSVC)
cmake -S . -B build
cmake --build build --config RelWithDebInfo

First, take the lid off — and notice that this is the only change to the scene:

./build/demos/gltf_view --hdr --polish 0.15 --pose 1.3

--polish forces every material to a roughness the file did not ask for, and adds a polished metal torus. The torus is not decoration: at roughness 0.15 the specular lobe is a few degrees wide, so a scene of flat faces presents the mirror angle at no pixel at all and the HDR buffer comes back with a peak of 0.22 — which is how a renderer with a real clipping problem can look completely fine. A torus is curved in both directions, so some point on it satisfies the mirror condition from every viewpoint.

Then the four curves, and the exposure:

./build/demos/gltf_view --hdr --polish 0.15 --pose 1.3 --tonemap reinhard
./build/demos/gltf_view --hdr --polish 0.15 --pose 1.3 --tonemap aces
./build/demos/gltf_view --hdr --polish 0.15 --pose 1.3 --tonemap white --white 8
./build/demos/gltf_view --hdr --polish 0.15 --pose 1.3 --tonemap aces --ev 2
./build/demos/gltf_view --hdr --polish 0.15 --pose 1.3 --tonemap reinhard --luma

And the harness:

sh scratch/build_verify_612.sh

Expected result

On the console, the diagnostic that says whether you have an HDR problem at all:

  polish        : every material forced to roughness 0.15, plus a metal torus to catch the lobe
  hdr           : max channel 411.8161 (8.7 stops), max luminance 398.9317
  over the lid  : 126 of 129600 pixels (0.10%) have a channel above 1 — every one of them would be code 255 under a clamp
  exposure      : EV100 -0.263 -> x1.0000   curve clamp   white 4.00
  log-average   : 0.00685  (arithmetic mean 0.04280 — the gap is what one specular pixel does to a mean)

0.10% of the frame. A tenth of one percent — and all of the information being destroyed is in it. Note also the last line: the arithmetic mean is 6.2× the log-average on this frame, which is §3.6's argument arriving in the demo rather than in a test.

On screen, the torus carries a long specular highlight. Under --tonemap clamp its core is a flat white region with a hard edge: 126 pixels of identical code 255, with the lobe's shape gone. Switch to reinhard and the core becomes a gradient — the same pixels, now carrying the shape they always had. Only 3 pixels remain at 255 rather than 126.

aces is visibly darker overall (its toe, costing 13 codes at the dark end) with the tightest roll-off; white sits between them and reaches true white where Reinhard cannot. And --ev 2 darkens everything by a little over two stops, which is what an exposure does and what a curve does not.

The harness ends:

== I  CPU vs GPU — THE SAME CURVE, COMPUTED TWICE ==
  a flat quad of known linear value, through BOTH resolves:
    value   curve            GPU   CPU
       4.0  reinhard          231   231
       4.0  aces              252   252
      16.0  reinhard          248   248
       0.5  reinhard          156   156
      40.0  clamp             255   255

45 checks, 0 failures

Five of five, exactly — not within a code, identical. And the fourth row is the one to notice: a linear value of 16, four stops above white, resolving to code 248 on the GPU through a float target that one pass wrote and another read. Before this lesson that value did not survive the fragment shader.

7Common pitfalls & debugging

The HDR buffer's peak is 0.22 and you were expecting thousands

Symptom. Everything is wired up, the scene is full of polished metal, and measure() reports nothing above 1.

Cause. A sharp specular lobe is a few degrees wide, and a scene of flat faces may present the mirror angle at no pixel at all. The highlight is not being clipped; it is not being hit.

Fix. Put a doubly-curved surface in the scene — this demo adds a torus — so that some point satisfies the mirror condition from every viewpoint. This is worth recognising because it is how a renderer with a real clipping problem can look completely fine right up until the day somebody adds a sphere.

Sharper surfaces report LOWER peaks than rough ones

Symptom. A probe sweeps the eye through a range of angles and finds that roughness 0.05 peaks lower than roughness 0.35, which is backwards.

Cause. The sweep misses the lobe. A narrow lobe needs the mirror direction exactly, and a sweep through a plane that does not contain it reports a peak that is an artefact of where you looked.

Fix. Evaluate at reflect(−to_light, n) rather than sweeping. And keep the symptom in mind as a diagnostic: a monotonic relationship coming out backwards usually means the measurement is missing the thing it is measuring, not that the physics is surprising. The first version of probe_612.cpp made exactly this mistake and the reversal is what gave it away.

The tonemapped image is washed out and never reaches white

Symptom. Everything looks slightly grey; nothing in the frame is code 255, even where the input was enormous.

Cause. Plain Reinhard. x/(1+x) is strictly below 1, and reaching code 255 needs an input of 224.

Fix. reinhard_white with a white point you choose, or ACES. It is worth knowing this is a property of the operator rather than a bug in your exposure — turning the exposure up to chase white just clips everything else.

The scene renders black, or the resolve samples garbage

Symptom. The HDR pass looks right in a capture and the resolve produces noise, black, or last frame's contents.

Cause. The HDR texture was created with COLOR_TARGET and not SAMPLER. It is a legal texture that cannot legally be read.

Fix. Both usage flags. And note there is no error to look for: SDL's GPU validation of this lives inside if (device->debug_mode) (Lesson 6.10), so on a release device the result is simply undefined.

Everything is far too bright, or far too dark, by exactly the gamma curve

Symptom. The resolve produces a picture that is recognisable but wrong in the familiar way — washed out or muddy across the whole frame.

Cause. Two encodes, or none. The scene shader is still encoding (encode_output = 1) while writing a float target, or the resolve is not encoding into a UNORM swapchain.

Fix. Exactly one encode, at the end. A float target never carries codes, so the scene pass writes linear; the resolve encodes if and only if its own target is not _SRGB. If you are unsure which you have, gpu_device's report prints the swapchain format and output_encodes_in_hardware.

The auto-exposure flickers whenever something shiny comes into view

Symptom. The whole image dims for a frame or two as the camera turns.

Cause. An exposure derived from the arithmetic mean luminance. One pixel in ten thousand at 5000 contributes ten times the rest of the frame put together.

Fix. The log-average, which is what hdr_stats reports beside the arithmetic mean precisely so the two can be compared. The gap on a frame with one bright highlight is 11×.

A saturated colour still clips after tonemapping

Symptom. Blues and reds go flat in bright areas even though the tonemapper is working.

Cause. Luminance-only tonemapping. The curve was applied to the luminance and landed in range; an individual channel did not. Blue is worst, at a luminance weight of 0.0722 — a pure blue of (0, 0, 8) arrives at b = 5.07.

Fix. Per-channel, which is in range by construction — or keep luminance-only and add a desaturation step, which is what production pipelines do and what this course names without building.

8Exercises

8.1 — Find your own lid guided

Run the demo at four roughnesses and record max channel and the over-the-lid count at each. Predict, before running, roughly where the count starts climbing — then check it against §A's table of peaks.

Hint

The peak scales roughly as 1/α² at the mirror angle, where α = roughness² (6.3's Disney remap) — so halving the roughness should multiply the peak by about sixteen. The table goes 1.74, 11.59, 177.03, 2824: ratios of 6.7, 15.3, 16.0. The agreement gets better as the approximation's assumptions get truer, which is itself worth noticing.

8.2 — Reach white with Reinhard guided

Compute by hand the input that plain Reinhard needs to produce code 254 rather than 255, and check it. Then work out what white point reinhard_white needs so that the demo's measured peak of 411.82 maps to exactly 1, and run it.

Hint

For the first: code 254 needs an encoded 253.5/255, invert the sRGB transform, then solve x/(1+x) = L for x. For the second, the answer is --white 411.82 — and the picture it produces is worth looking at, because mapping the brightest pixel in the frame to exactly white is almost never what you want.

8.3 — Auto-exposure engine

Use hdr_stats::log_mean_luminance to choose the exposure each frame instead of taking it from the command line: ev100_from_luminance(log_mean * K) for a K you pick. Then make it adapt — move the EV toward its target by a fixed rate per second rather than jumping.

Solution sketch

The standard K is 12.5/100 giving EV = log2(L · 100/12.5). The adaptation is one line — ev += (target − ev) · (1 − exp(−rate · dt)) — and the exp form rather than a plain lerp is what makes the rate frame-rate independent, which is Lesson 1.4's fixed-timestep argument arriving somewhere unexpected. Real implementations adapt up and down at different rates, because eyes do.

8.4 — Prove the ordering claim on a picture guided

Add a --wrong-order flag that applies the curve before the exposure, render the polished scene at EV 2 both ways, and diff. Describe what is lost in words before looking at the numbers.

Hint

The prediction: the wrong order will be darker and will have less variation in the highlight, because the curve has already mapped everything above about 4 into a narrow band and the exposure then scales that band. Look specifically at the range of codes inside the highlight, not at the mean.

8.5 — A real HDR display engine

gpu_device's report carries an SDL_GPUSwapchainComposition. Find out which compositions your machine claims to support, and write the branch that would use one — without implementing the PQ transfer function, just the structure and the honest list of what else would have to change.

Hint

SDL_WindowSupportsGPUSwapchainComposition is the query. The interesting part of the answer is that the tonemap curve's target changes: you are no longer mapping to [0,1] for an sRGB display but to an absolute luminance in nits, which makes the white point a display property rather than an artistic one. Most of the rest of this lesson survives unchanged, which is the point of having separated exposure from curve from encode.

9Recap & next

The engine stopped avoiding a question it had been avoiding for six modules.

  • A float render target in both renderers — hdr_buffer on the CPU at 3× the memory, R16G16B16A16_FLOAT on the GPU at 2×, and the difference between those multipliers is entirely the half float.
  • An exposure in real photographic units, paying a debt light.hpp recorded by name — and locating the engine's previous behaviour at EV −0.263 rather than treating it as a special case.
  • Four curves, two of them derived rather than copied, with the three properties that make one legal stated and checked.
  • A full-screen resolve pass — the first thing in this engine that draws no geometry and whose cost scales with pixels — with no vertex buffer, no depth and no blending.

Three numbers to carry forward. The demo's polished scene has 0.10% of its pixels over the lid, and all of the destroyed information is in them. The arithmetic mean of a frame's luminance is 11× its log-average when one specular pixel is present, which is why metering happens in log space. And compositing on a float target is 8.1× cheaper than on 8 bits, because Lesson 6.11's decode-and-re-encode round trip is not a cost of blending — it was a cost of storing something other than light.

It is worth stating what did not change, too. The reference render is byte-identical for the twenty-first lesson, and for a structural reason rather than a flag: the fixture renders into an 8-bit framebuffer through the path it has always used, and everything here operates on a different buffer type. This is not "the tonemapper is optional" — it is "the tonemapper is a stage over a target the fixture does not have".

Next: 6.13 — Bloom and the Post-Processing Stack. This lesson built one post pass and deliberately did not build a stack, because a stack is an architecture question and one user is not enough evidence to answer it. Bloom supplies the second user, and it also supplies the reason the ordering matters: a bloom operates on the pre-tonemap image, because it is looking for exactly the values above 1 that this lesson finally lets the engine keep. Which means the first thing 6.13 has to decide is who owns the intermediate targets — and that is the same question a frame graph (6.17) answers at a larger scale.

10Further reading

  • Reinhard, E., Stark, M., Shirley, P. and Ferwerda, J., "Photographic Tone Reproduction for Digital Images", SIGGRAPH 2002. The source of the operator and of the log-average, and much more interesting than its reputation: the whole paper is an argument by analogy with the Zone System, which is to say with how photographers actually decide an exposure.
  • Lagarde, S. and de Rousiers, C., "Moving Frostbite to Physically Based Rendering" (2014), §5. Where the 1.2 comes from, worked properly, along with the whole photometric-units argument this lesson's exposure control rests on. The reference to read if you want to author lights in lux and lumens rather than in numbers.
  • Narkowicz, K., "ACES Filmic Tone Mapping Curve" (2015). Two pages, five constants, and an honest account of how the fit was made. Worth reading precisely because it does not claim to be more than it is.
  • Hable, J., "Filmic Worlds" — the Uncharted 2 tonemapper and its successors. The other curve everyone uses, and Hable's later posts revisiting it are an unusually candid record of somebody changing their mind about their own widely-copied work.
  • Real-Time Rendering, 4th ed., §8 "Light and Color". Radiometry, photometry, and the tonemapping chain in one place, with the display side this lesson leaves out.
  • SDL3 wiki: SDL_SetGPUSwapchainParameters and SDL_GPUSwapchainComposition. What Exercise 8.5 needs, and the honest scope of what SDL gives you for a real HDR display.
