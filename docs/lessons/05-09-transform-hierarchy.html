Module 5 — Becoming an Engine · Lesson 5.9

Transform Hierarchy and the Camera System

A turret sits on a tank. That sentence needs one component and one line of matrix algebra, and both were ready before this lesson started — parent_from_local has been called that since Lesson 2.8 precisely so this day would change nothing. What is not ready is the order: a parent must be resolved before its children, and Lesson 5.7 established that a component pool hands entities back in insertion order, which is not that. This lesson measures three ways to reconcile them, ships the one the numbers chose, and then spends the payoff on making the camera an ordinary entity.

1The Problem

Lesson 5.8's world is flat. Every transform is a placement in world space, which was exactly right for a swarm of objects orbiting an origin and is useless the moment one thing has to move with another. A turret on a tank; a lamp on a table; a camera in a car; a hand on an arm on a body. Every one of those is the same sentence — my placement is relative to something else's — and a flat world cannot say it.

The fix is one component and one line of algebra:

The composition rule

world_from_local(child) = world_from_local(parent) × parent_from_local(child)

That is the whole of a scene graph. And the striking thing about it, in this course, is how little it costs to adopt: math/transform.hpp has been written for it since Lesson 2.8, which is why the function that turns a transform into a matrix is called parent_from_local and not world_from_local. That header's comment said so at the time:

“Why ‘parent’ and not ‘world’: in Module 2 every object stands on its own, so its parent IS the world… Module 5 adds a transform HIERARCHY, where an object's transform is relative to another object rather than to the world. When that happens this function does not change by a single character — only the meaning of ‘parent’ widens. Naming it world_from_local today would have been a lie we had to go back and correct.”

It did not change by a single character. Neither did transform. So if the maths is free and the naming was paid for seven lessons ago, what is this lesson about?

The order. Look at the lower half of Figure 1. The composition rule reads a parent's world matrix, which means that matrix has to exist already — the parent must be finished before any of its children start. That is an ordering constraint on the graph.

And Lesson 5.7 established, in as many words, that a component pool's dense order is nobody's business but the pool's: it is insertion order, disturbed by every swap-and-pop, and the whole reason a sparse set can be fast is that it was never promised to anyone. Those two facts are in direct tension, and this lesson is what it costs to reconcile them.

It is worth seeing that this is a real problem rather than a theoretical one. §C of the verification harness builds a four-level tree of forty-eight entities, churns it the way a running game churns — create some things, destroy some things — and then asks how many entities sit in the transform pool ahead of their own parent. The answer is twelve of forty-eight. A walk in dense order would compose each of those against a matrix left over from the previous frame: a picture that is wrong, subtly, in a way that nothing reports and that looks like a physics glitch.

A test that passed for the wrong reason — twice

Producing those twelve took three attempts, and the failures are more instructive than the success. The first version destroyed a scattering of leaves and found zero violations, because swap-and-pop fills a hole with the pool's last element, and in a tree built level by level the last elements are themselves leaves — a leaf replaced a leaf and the order was undisturbed. The second version destroyed filler entities created at the end of the pool, which had the same problem for the same reason.

What works is destroying something early while deep entities sit at the end. Twice in a row the test passed because the situation it described had never arisen — which is the failure mode a test of an ordering property is most prone to, and the reason its assertion is written as “this count is greater than zero” rather than “the answer is right”.

2Building Intuition

2.1 The rule, and why nothing had to change

Start with the arithmetic, on real numbers, because the rule is easier to trust once you have pushed something through it.

Worked example — a moon, two composes deep

A sun at the origin, a planet at (10, 0, 0) in the sun's space, a moon at (0, 2, 0) in the planet's space.

world(sun) = parent_from_local(sun) = T(0,0,0)

world(planet) = world(sun) × T(10, 0, 0) = T(10, 0, 0)

world(moon) = world(planet) × T(0, 2, 0) = T(10, 2, 0)

Nobody computed (10, 2, 0). It is not stored anywhere and no system produced it; it is what the two multiplications happen to say.

Now move the sun to (100, 0, 0) and re-run only the composition. The planet is at (110, 0, 0) and the moon at (110, 2, 0), and again — no code said so. “Following” is not a feature anybody implemented. It is what relative to my parent means, and the second half of the sentence is the interesting half:

Rotation and scale compose too, and scale is the one that surprises people. Halve the sun's scale and the planet's world distance halves with it — 10 becomes 5 — because the parent's matrix scales the child's whole coordinate frame, its offset included. In the demo, a moon authored at scale 0.8 under a planet of scale 0.16 comes out 0.128 in world units, and its local orbit radius of 3.6 becomes 0.58. That is not a bug in the composition. It is why a “one-metre” prop dropped under a scaled parent turns out to be twenty centimetres, and it catches everybody once.

2.2 The order, and why everything did

So the maths is free. Here is what is not.

To compute a child you need its parent's world matrix. To compute the parent you need its parent's. The dependency runs up the tree, so the computation has to run down it, and any visit order that ever reaches a child before its parent produces silent nonsense. There is no test you can put in the loop that fixes this cheaply — you could check “has my parent been resolved this frame?”, but then you need a per-entity flag, a way to clear it, and a plan for what to do when the answer is no.

The alternative is to arrange for the question never to arise: visit in an order that guarantees the answer. That is what the rest of this lesson is about, and the reason it is worth measuring rather than deciding is that there are three such orders, they are all correct, and they do not cost the same.

2.3 Depth is a topological sort

One idea makes the whole thing simple, and it is worth stating on its own because it is doing more work than it looks like it is.

The observation the design rests on

A parent's depth is always exactly one less than its child's. So sorting entities by depth puts every parent before every child — which is precisely the definition of a topological order. And a counting sort by a small integer key is O(n).

That gets you a valid order for the price of one pass. But it gives you something else as well, and this is the part that pays off in Module 8: within a level, the order does not matter at all. Everything at depth k depends only on things at depth k−1, and nothing at depth k depends on anything else at depth k. A level is therefore a batch of completely independent work — a parallel_for waiting to happen — and nobody had to design that. It arrived with the choice of order.

A depth-first recursion has the opposite property. It also produces a valid order, but its order is a sequence: node 3 must follow node 1 which must follow node 0, and there is no batch anywhere in it that can be handed to another thread without a dependency analysis.

3Three Candidate Orders

3.1 What the three actually differ in

The three candidate resolve orders and what each one costs.
the orderwhat it is good atwhat it pays
A — recurse from roots depth first the best temporal locality available: a parent's matrix was written one call ago and is certainly in L1 stack traffic, and a write pattern that jumps
B — level order, via an index breadth first, through an order[] array flat loops, no recursion, and a level is independent work both reads and writes are scattered through arrays that were never moved
C — level order, rows packed breadth first, with the rows physically rearranged reads and writes go straight down the arrays a permutation, every time the shape changes

B and C are worth looking at twice, because they visit in the same order and differ only in where the rows live. C is Lesson 5.7's group idea, generalised: 5.7 measured that two pools sorted to agree on index let a query skip the sparse lookup entirely, and noted that this was available precisely because dense order had never been promised to anyone. Here the same freedom buys an ordering constraint instead of an agreement between two pools — and it is worth noticing that the second use arrived two lessons after the first, without the design needing to be revisited.

3.2 Measuring an order without an ECS in the way

The three arms differ in a visit order over three parallel arrays, and that is all they differ in. So the probe (scratch/hier_probe.hpp) is three parallel arrays — a local transform, a parent row, an output matrix — plus two indexes built on top of them. No registry, no pools, no entities.

That is the same method Lesson 5.7 used to choose between an archetype and a sparse set before either was built, and it is worth naming the reason again: building the experiment on the ECS would tie four timings to one container's incidental behaviour, and then a change to the container would silently invalidate a design decision. What is being compared here is the order. The container's own cost is measured separately, in §4.4, and it is not small.

3.3 Being fair to all three

Three decisions in the probe are there to stop an arm winning for the wrong reason.

The recursive arm gets a CSR children list, not a vector of vectors. The obvious spelling for “each node's children” is std::vector<std::vector<uint32_t>>, which would hand arm A n separate heap allocations to chase and would make it lose to anything. Compressed sparse row — one children array and one child_start array — is what a competent implementation uses, so it is what the competitor gets.

Arm C's permutation is not timed inside the resolve. It is a cost the world pays when its shape changes, and hiding it in the per-frame number would be measuring a world that never spawns anything. §4.3 prices it separately and it turns out to matter.

The row order is an axis, not an assumption. In a world built all at once, row order is creation order, so parents naturally sit near their children and every arm walks nearly forwards. In a world that has been running for ten minutes the orders have decayed. Both are real; quoting only the first is not an argument. The tables below report both, and the difference between them turns out to be the entire case for arm C.

A measurement that reported the timer instead of the code

The first run of this benchmark reported 10.417 ns/entity for two different arms at n = 100. That number is 25 ticks of SDL's performance counter exactly — 41.67 ns each on this machine — and a pass over a hundred entities takes about a microsecond, which is below the course's noise floor of 100 ticks. The timer was talking, not the code.

The fix is the standard one from Lesson 4.9: scale the workload until it clears the floor and divide. Small worlds are now walked repeatedly — enough repetitions to reach 200,000 composes — and the total is divided by the total items. Every number below the thousand-entity mark in the tables that follow would otherwise be quantisation.

Read the first row first. At depth 1 there is no hierarchy at all — every entity is a root, both arms do exactly the same work, and the ratio is 1.01×. That is the control, and it is the row that says the harness is measuring the tree rather than measuring itself. If it had come out at 0.6× or 1.4× nothing below it would be worth reading.

Everything after it is the tree. Recursion goes from 3.34 to 13.55 ns/entity — a factor of four — while level order goes from 3.39 to 4.94, a factor of 1.5. The entity count never moves; only the shape does. So:

The finding

Recursion is depth-dependent. Level order is very nearly not.

And it saturates: doubling the depth from 16 to 32 costs recursion almost nothing more, because by then every entity that is going to be a non-root already is.

The mechanism is the write pattern. A depth-first walk writes to whichever row the next child happens to occupy, and in a churned world those rows are scattered; a level-order walk writes forwards. The reads have the opposite bias — recursion's parent matrix is guaranteed hot, level order's may have been evicted since its level finished — and the measurement says the writes win. That is a claim about this machine's cache, which is why the next subsection varies the working set.

Two things to take from that table, and the second is the one that decides §5's design.

Below a thousand entities, nothing matters. Every arm is within 20% of every other, which is the same shape Lesson 5.6 found for scene layouts and Lesson 5.7 found for ECS storage. This engine's demo has 154 entities. The correct order is not an optimisation here; it is a correctness requirement that happens to be free.

Packing only pays once the row order has decayed. In creation order B and C are indistinguishable — the rows are already nearly in level order, so physically moving them achieves nothing. Scramble the rows and B loses ground (5.00 → 7.63 ns at n = 100,000, +52%) while C barely moves (4.81 → 4.91, +2%). The permutation's entire value is undoing a decay that a freshly built world does not have.

4.3 What the order costs to maintain

Neither B nor C is free to keep. Both need the level index rebuilt when the shape changes, and C needs the rows moved as well.

So rebuilding the index costs roughly one resolve, and permuting the rows costs roughly 1.6. Both are paid only when the shape changes, and a game changes shape far less often than it moves things — which is the observation the implementation is built around.

It also settles B against C. C buys about 30% at a hundred thousand entities in a decayed world, and charges 1.6 resolves for every topology change to get it. Re-parent anything and the saving is gone for that frame and the next. At the sizes this engine has, C buys nothing at all and charges the same. So:

The decision

Ship B. Level order through an index array, no permutation. It captures all of the depth-independence, it needs no row movement, and — the part that matters most — it is the same shape as C, so a world that outgrows it can adopt the permutation later without changing the resolver's loop.

When B becomes the wrong answer, in one sentence: a world past ~10⁴ entities whose rows have decayed and whose shape is stable, which is a streaming open world or a crowd. Even then the change is to add a permutation step to rebuild(), not to rewrite anything.

4.4 …and what the container adds on top

Everything above measured a visit order over plain arrays. The shipped resolver walks the same order through registry and pool<T>, so it pays the ECS's own indirection: three sparse lookups per entity instead of three array reads. Quoting the probe's numbers for shipped code would be quoting a measurement of something else.

The ECS costs 22–40% on this pass, and that is a number worth having rather than hiding: it is what being able to ask “which entities have a transform?” is priced at, on a loop that does very little arithmetic per element. It is also the number that tells you the lookup-caching exercise in §11.4 is worth attempting.

And the last column is the one the implementation is designed around: a rebuild costs about one resolve. Rebuild every frame and you have doubled the price of the hierarchy for nothing; rebuild only when the shape changes and it disappears into the noise.

Why every arm reported agree, when Lesson 5.6 said it would not

These arms visit the same rows in different orders, so on 5.6's evidence their running checksums should differ in the last bits — floating-point addition is not associative. They do not. The reason is the widening: each addend is a float (24-bit mantissa) accumulated into a double (53), and a running sum of 10⁵ values of magnitude ~1 never exceeds 2¹⁷, so every partial sum needs at most 17 + 24 = 41 significant bits and nothing is ever rounded. An exact sum is order-independent.

That is an explanation, so it needs a knob. Change the accumulator to float and the agreement vanishes — 49928.1484 forwards against 49928.2734 backwards. §F of the harness runs both and asserts both outcomes, because “an effect that survives the removal of its explanation had a different explanation”.

5The Implementation

5.1 Two components, and what is not in them

Four bytes and sixty-four. What is absent from parent is the interesting part: there is no child list. Children are derivable from parents — the rebuild does exactly that — and a stored child list would be a second source of truth that has to be kept in step with the first on every re-parent, every destroy, every load. That is the same argument registry::destroy made in Lesson 5.8 for not keeping a per-entity component mask, and it costs the same thing: a scan where an index would have been O(1). You can see the bill in destroy_subtree, which sorts the world's links once rather than maintaining an index forever.

An entity with no parent is a root, so Lesson 5.8's flat world is the special case rather than a thing that had to be migrated. Nothing in the demo's waypoints or sparks changed to accommodate a hierarchy; they simply acquired one.

Without it, a sixteen-deep chain of 100,000 entities walks 1.6 million links instead of 100,000, and the difference shows up at exactly the sizes where anyone would care. 5.3 resolve() — the dullest loop in the engine

The dullness is the finding. No recursion, no stack, no visited set, and — the one worth pointing at — no test for whether the parent has been resolved yet. The order already guarantees it. Every one of those absent mechanisms is a thing that would have had to be written, tested and paid for per entity if the visit order had been chosen carelessly.

Note also that three of the four “is this a root?” cases collapse into one line: no parent component, a parent that is not alive, and a parent with no world_transform of its own all produce above == nullptr and therefore “already in world space”. That is not a coincidence — it is what makes the orphan policy in §5.4 cheap enough to be defensible.

The split between rebuild() and resolve() is the design's whole economy, and §4.4 priced it: a rebuild is about one resolve, so calling it every frame doubles the hierarchy's cost. The caller says when the shape changed:

The order of those six lines is the lesson. Everything above the resolve writes local data and knows nothing about parents. The resolve turns local into world, once, in an order that guarantees a parent is finished before its children start. Nothing below it has to think about hierarchy at all.

A flag, not a signal — and the honest cost of that

mark_topology_changed() is something the caller remembers to call. The registry notifies nobody about anything (Lesson 5.8 ships no signals), so this is where a human takes responsibility. Forget it and a debug build asserts on the next resolve() — if the entity count moved. The assertion compares the order's length against the transform pool's, and that is the strongest check available without a version counter. It cannot see an add and a remove between two resolves: the count matches and the order is stale.

Signals would close the hole and add a mechanism nobody has asked for. Module 8 revisits it with the editor, which is the first caller that genuinely needs one.

5.4 Orphans, cycles, and the two policies

Two things can go wrong with a parent link, and they are not the same kind of wrong.

An orphan — a child whose parent has died — becomes a root. Its local transform is used unchanged, so it stops following and stays where it last was, and hierarchy_report::orphans counts it. The alternative is to destroy the subtree, and that is a policy a game may want and a transform system must not impose: silently deleting entities during a resolve pass is a surprise nobody asked for. So the engine offers destroy_subtree() — an explicit function whose name says what it does — and the demo's X key uses it, while the same key with a plain destroy() would leave the moons orbiting the world origin with a number on the HUD explaining why.

A cycle is a different class of failure entirely. A wrong picture is recoverable; a hang is not, and a naive resolver walking a loop hangs. So there are two defences, deliberately:

The two defences against a parent cycle.
wherewhat it doeswhy both are needed
set_parent() walks up from the proposed parent and refuses if the child is on that path stops a cycle being created through the intended API — including grandchildren, which a one-link check would miss
rebuild() marks nodes k_in_progress, breaks the chain at the top, counts it, logs it parent is a public component and nothing stops a caller writing one directly — or a scene file loading one

§E of the harness writes a three-cycle straight into the components, resolves, and checks four things: that it did not hang (the only one that really matters), that the cycle was counted rather than absorbed, that all four entities were still resolved, and that the entity outside the cycle was unaffected. One bad link must not cost the frame.

6The Optimisation We Did Not Ship

There is an obvious fourth arm, and it is the one everybody suggests: only recompute what moved. Mark an entity dirty when its local transform changes, propagate the mark to its descendants, and skip everything else. It reduces work rather than improving locality, which is a genuinely different axis, and in level order the propagation is free — mark a child as you pass its parent, and the parent has always been visited first.

So it was measured, at 100,000 entities and depth 8, against the fraction of the world that moved.

A dirty-subtree pass compared with a full pass, against the fraction of entities whose local transform changed.

The second column is the one nobody quotes. Moving 10% of a depth-8 tree dirties 36% of it, because every descendant of a moved entity has to move too. At 25% moved the pass is reaching two thirds of the world, and the saving has evaporated.

So the crossover is at roughly a quarter of the world moving, and past it the dirty pass is slower — 1.29× when everything moves, which is precisely what an animated scene does every frame. The demo in this lesson moves 100% of its entities on every step: an orbit system writing every placement, a spin system writing every rotation. Shipping a dirty flag would have made it 29% slower and added a per-entity flag, a propagation rule and a way to clear it.

The decision, and it is a no

Not shipped. It wins below about 20% moved and loses above 25%, and the engine's own demo sits at 100%. A game whose scene is mostly static — an architectural walkthrough, a strategy game with a fixed map, a level whose props never move — would take the other decision on the same evidence, and now has the number to make it with.

Lesson 5.6 said a measurement lesson has to be willing to conclude no. This one concludes no twice: no permutation (§4.3) and no dirty flags (here). Both holes are named, both have a crossover attached, and Exercise 11.2 builds the dirty version for a reader who wants to see it.

7The Camera Becomes an Entity

Through Module 4 the camera was demo::orbit_camera: a struct with a target, a radius and two angles, living in the demo, reachable only by the one program that owned it. That worked because there was exactly one camera and exactly one program, and it stopped working the moment either of those became two.

With an ECS a camera is not a kind of thing at all. It is an entity that happens to have four components, and three consequences fall out that nobody had to design:

  1. A camera can be parented. Attach it to a car and it rides in the car, resolved by the same pass as everything else. In a world of camera structs this is a special case somebody writes by hand.
  2. “Which camera is active” is a component, not a pointer. Switching moves a tag; there is no dangling camera pointer to invalidate when the thing it aimed at is destroyed, because a stale entity simply stops resolving (Lesson 5.4).
  3. A camera is findable. view<camera, active_camera>() is the query, and it is the same query shape as everything else in the engine.

active_camera is an empty struct, and that is the whole feature. A component with no data is a tag: its presence is the information. Switching cameras is two structural changes, each of which Lesson 5.7 measured at about four nanoseconds.

7.1 The maths, which Lesson 2.9 already did

A camera entity has a placement. Lesson 2.9 established that the view matrix is the inverse of the camera's model matrix, and that for a rigid placement that inverse is written down rather than computed. Until now there was no camera model matrix to invert, so look_at took an eye and a target and did both steps at once. Now the placement exists, so the two steps separate:

For M = affine(R, t) with R orthonormal, the inverse is affine(Rᵀ, −Rᵀ·t) — nine multiplies for a transpose that is really just a relabelling, and nine more for the offset. There is still no general 4×4 inverse in this engine, for the reason mat4.hpp gave in Module 2: it would answer a question we never ask.

And the identity that says all of this is the same maths, checked bit for bit in §D:

The identity — verify_59 §D, worst element difference 0.000e+00

rigid_inverse(parent_from_local(look_along(e, t, u))) == look_at(e, t, u)

look_along answers “where does the camera have to be?”; look_at answers “what matrix takes the world into its view?”. They are inverses, and now both are spelled out.

One field is deliberately missing from camera, and it is the one people expect: aspect ratio. A camera's aspect is not a property of the camera — it is a property of the surface it is drawn to, which is the window, which the user can resize. Storing it would mean every camera in a scene file carries a number that was true on the machine that saved it. projection_of(c, aspect) takes it from whoever owns the framebuffer.

A precondition asserted rather than checked

rigid_inverse is only an inverse for a rigid matrix. A scaled camera is not a camera with a bug — it is a category mistake, since scaling a camera would change its field of view by changing its units. By Lesson 5.3's test that makes it a programmer error rather than something the world can do to a correct program, so view_from_camera asserts is_rigid() in a debug build and pays nothing in a release one.

is_rigid deliberately accepts a reflection (determinant −1), because a mirrored camera is a legitimate thing and the inverse is still correct for it. The check exists to catch scale.

10Common Pitfalls & Debugging

One object lags a frame behind its parent

Symptom. A turret trails its tank by exactly one frame — worse when the tank is fast, invisible when it is stopped.

Cause. The child was resolved before the parent, so it composed against last frame's matrix. Either the resolve is walking dense order, or the level index is stale.

Fix. Call rebuild() whenever the shape changes. To find it, walk tree.level(i) and assert depth_of(parent) + 1 == i for every entity — verify_59 §C is exactly that loop, and it fails immediately on a stale order.

A re-parented object teleports

Symptom. Dropping an object onto a new parent in the editor moves it.

Cause. Not a bug — set_parent keeps the local transform, so a new parent means a new world position. §B of the harness asserts this explicitly: an entity at world 3 under a chain of ones lands at world 2 when re-parented one level up.

Fix. Decide which behaviour you want and be explicit. “Keep world position” means computing a new local transform — local' = rigid_inverse(world(new_parent)) * world(child) — which needs the resolve to have run first. Editors offer both; this engine offers the simple one and Exercise 11.3 adds the other.

The program hangs after loading a scene

Symptom. No crash, no error, no frame. A profiler shows one thread at 100% inside the transform update.

Cause. A parent cycle. set_parent refuses to create one, but parent is a public component and a scene file, a serializer bug or a direct add<parent> can write one anyway.

Fix. This engine cannot hang on it — rebuild() marks nodes k_in_progress, breaks the chain, counts it in hierarchy_report::cycles and logs a warning naming the entity. Put that count on your HUD. It is zero forever in a healthy program, and the frame it stops being zero is the frame you want to know.

A child keeps following a parent that is dead

Symptom. Or the opposite — a child stops following and you expected it to be deleted.

Cause. The orphan policy, working as designed: a child whose parent died becomes a root and keeps its local transform, so it stays where it was.

Fix. If you wanted the subtree gone, say so with destroy_subtree(). If you wanted it kept but re-parented to the grandparent, that is a third policy and about six lines — but decide it in your code rather than expecting the transform system to guess. hierarchy_report::orphans is how you notice you have not decided.

A “1-metre” object is not 1 metre

Symptom. A prop authored at unit scale is 20 cm in game, and scaling it up by 5 “fixes” it until somebody re-parents it.

Cause. Scale composes down the chain like everything else. A child under a parent of scale 0.16 is 0.16 of its authored size, and under two such parents it is 0.0256.

Fix. Keep scale on the leaves and out of the intermediate nodes wherever you can — a common convention is that only the geometry-carrying node scales. This is worth knowing rather than fixing: the demo's moons are deliberately built this way, and the comment beside them does the arithmetic.

Everything vanishes after destroying the camera

Symptom. A black frame, no error.

Cause. find_active_camera returned null_entity, which is a state a scene can legitimately be in — before the camera is spawned, or after it dies.

Fix. Branch on it, as the demo does. Note what did not happen: nothing dangled and nothing crashed, because the tag went with the entity. That is Lesson 5.4's argument reaching the renderer.

11Exercises

  1. 11.1 — Find your own crossover. §4.3 says the permutation (arm C) costs about 1.6 resolves and buys about 30% at 100,000 scrambled entities. Solve for the number of frames a world must go without a topology change before packing pays for itself, at 10,000 and at 100,000 entities, using your machine's numbers. Then decide whether any game you have played would clear it.

    Hint

    Packing costs P once and saves (B − C) per frame. Break-even is P / (B − C) frames. Use the scrambled rows — in creation order the saving is zero and the answer is “never”.

    Solution sketch

    With this machine's numbers at 100,000 scrambled, depth 16: B = 7.63, C = 4.91, so the saving is 2.72 ns/entity/frame; the permutation is 7.96. Break-even is ≈ 3 frames — which sounds like packing always wins, until you notice that a spawn is a topology change and that a game spawning anything at all resets the clock every frame. At 10,000 entities the saving is 6.15 − 4.79 = 1.36 and break-even is ≈ 5.8 frames, but the absolute total is 60 µs a frame either way. The honest conclusion is that the crossover is not the interesting number — the absolute one is.

  2. 11.2 — Ship the dirty flag, and find where it stops paying on your scene. Add a dirty flag to the resolver: a per-entity byte, set when a local transform is written, propagated to children during the level walk, cleared at the end. Then measure it on the demo (which moves 100% of its entities) and on a modified demo where only the sparks move. Report both.

    Hint

    The propagation is free in level order — mark a child as you pass its parent, because the parent has always been visited first. The hard part is not the resolver, it is setting the flag: every write to a transform has to go through something that knows to mark it, which is an argument for an accessor and against handing out transform&.

    Solution sketch

    Expect §6's table shape: a big win below ~10% moved, a crossover around 25%, and a loss above it. The interesting part is the second measurement — a scene where only sparks move should land in the 0.08–0.15× band, which is where the optimisation is genuinely worth its complexity. Note also what the flag costs when you get it wrong: a missed mark is an object that silently stops following, which is strictly worse than a slow frame.

  3. 11.3 — Re-parent while keeping world position. Add set_parent_keep_world(registry&, hierarchy&, entity child, entity new_parent), which computes a new local transform so the object does not move. Then use it in the demo so H detaches the outer ring without the objects jumping.

    Hint

    local' = inverse(world(new_parent)) × world(child). The parent's world matrix may have scale, so rigid_inverse is not enough — this is the first place in the course that genuinely needs a general affine inverse, which is most of the exercise.

    Solution sketch

    An affine inverse is affine(inverse(L), −inverse(L)·t) where L is the 3×3 linear part — and mat3.hpp already has inverse(mat3), so it is four lines rather than a 4×4 cofactor expansion. Then decompose the resulting matrix back into a transform, which is where it gets interesting: the general case needs a polar or QR decomposition, and a hierarchy with shear has no exact transform representation at all. That is worth discovering — it is the reason production engines store a matrix rather than a TRS on the resolved side.

  4. 11.4 — Cache the dense rows and measure the container's tax away. §4.4 measured the shipped resolver at 1.22–1.40× the probe, and the gap is three sparse lookups per entity. Since rebuild() already runs whenever the pools change shape, it could record each entity's dense position in the transform and world-transform pools, letting resolve() index arrays directly. Implement it, measure it, and then write down the invariant it depends on and one way to violate it.

    Hint

    The invariant is that no pool's dense order changes between rebuild() and resolve(). Adding any component to any entity is safe; adding or removing a transform is not.

    Solution sketch

    Expect most of the 1.22–1.40× back, since the remaining work is two array reads and a matrix multiply. The violation to write down is the dangerous one: a cached row is a stale index, and a stale index does not fail — it silently reads a different entity's transform. This is Lesson 5.4's whole argument arriving inside the engine's own code, and the honest answer is that the cache needs the same generation check the pools already have, at which point you have re-invented the lookup you were avoiding. Deciding not to ship it is a legitimate outcome of this exercise.

  5. 11.5 — A second camera, and a cut between them. Add a second camera entity parented to the sun, and a key that switches which one is tagged active_camera. Then add a third that is parented to a moon, and explain — before running it — what the view will do and why.

    Solution sketch

    The switch is set_active_camera and nothing else: no pointer, no re-initialisation, no renderer change. The moon camera is the interesting one — it inherits the moon's scale as well as its placement, so unless the chain's scale is 1 the camera's placement is not rigid and view_from_camera's assertion fires. That is the assertion doing its job, and the fix is to give the camera a local scale that cancels its parents' — or, better, to attach cameras only to unscaled nodes, which is why real engines keep a scale-free “socket” convention.

12Recap & Next

The engine can now express “this is attached to that”, and the camera stopped being a special case. What it gained:

  • A hierarchy, in two components. parent is four bytes and world_transform is the composed result. No child list, deliberately — children are derivable, and a stored list is a second source of truth.
  • A resolve order that is flat in depth. Level order, chosen by measurement over depth-first recursion, which ran 3.34 → 13.55 ns/entity from depth 1 to 32 while level order ran 3.39 → 4.94. And a level is independent work, so Module 8's job system has its unit of parallelism already.
  • A split between shape and motion. rebuild() costs about one resolve and runs when the shape changes; resolve() runs every frame. That split is worth more than any choice of visit order.
  • Two policies, both stated. An orphan becomes a root and is counted; a cycle is broken, counted and logged, because a hang is not a recoverable failure. destroy_subtree() is the explicit tool for callers who want the other behaviour.
  • A camera that is an entity. Parentable, switchable by moving a tag, findable by a query — and its view matrix is rigid_inverse of its placement, which is bit-for-bit Lesson 2.9's look_at.

And two things it deliberately did not gain, each with a number attached: no packed rows (1.6 resolves per topology change to buy 30% at a scale this engine does not have) and no dirty flags (1.29× slower on a scene that animates everything, which is this one). Both holes are named in the source.

The reference shot from Lesson 5.1 is byte-identical for the ninth lesson. 5.9 added two headers and two functions to mat4.hpp and touched no line the reference scene executes — and the two things that made the whole lesson cheap were decided long before it: parent_from_local was named for this in Lesson 2.8, and Lesson 2.9 derived the rigid inverse that rigid_inverse is a copy of.
