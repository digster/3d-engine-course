Sequential Impulses: Warm Starting, Islands, and Sleeping

Lesson 8.9 ended on a confession. One pass of impulses over a four-point manifold does not hold a crate up: it leaves 2.6 × 10⁻² m/s of residual approach velocity, the crate sinks 275 mm in fifteen seconds, and it never stops. That was one of two failures, and the honest half of the confession was that they are different problems needing different machinery. This lesson builds both, and then the two things that make the result affordable. Iteration is the first: sweep every contact again, against the velocities the other contacts have since produced, and the residual falls by a measured factor of 0.53 per sweep — geometric, constant over four decades, until it hits the float floor. Warm starting is what makes a handful of sweeps enough instead of hundreds, and it is already built: 8.7 gave every contact a feature id, 8.9's write_back puts the impulse in the manifold, and this lesson closes the loop by reading it back. A settled five-crate tower reaches a residual of 10⁻⁴ in 89 iterations warm and has not reached it in 400 cold; at the eight iterations this engine ships, the cold tower collapses — 2,020 mm of sink — where the warm one holds at 40. Closing that loop also exposed a bug three lessons old, and it is the best kind: the two cached friction impulses are coordinates in a basis, the basis was never stored with them, and tangent_basis re-chooses it every frame by comparing two components of the normal that are both around 10⁻⁵. They cross constantly. Measured: 0.96% of manifold-frames rotate by more than thirty degrees, worst case 134.6, and last frame's friction is applied sideways. Then the second failure, which no number of velocity iterations can touch, because a converged solve says the overlap stops growing and nothing at all about the overlap that is already there. §6 sets out to prove that arrival depth is exactly one step of travel and the measurement refuses it: it is a ceiling, and the depth is uniform on [0, v·h] with a mean of 0.49. Two corrections fix it and one of them adds energy — a crate spawned 200 mm inside the floor is thrown 99 mm clear of it by Baumgarte and settles quietly at the slop under split impulse, for the same time constant and 46% of a velocity iteration. Then islands, whose whole content is one rule: a body that cannot move is not a bridge. Break that rule and a yard of twenty separate towers becomes one island, which is not a slow simulation — it is no sleeping at all. And sleeping, which takes a settled yard's solve from 131 µs to 3, with two findings that are not in the textbooks: the sleep test cannot run before the solve, because every resting body in the scene is travelling at g·h then (0 of 100 quiet, against 96 of 100 after), and sleeping has to be decided per island, because a body slept on its own hangs in the air when the crate under it is knocked away. Along the way body_world::step is split in two so that a contact solve has somewhere to go — checked bit-for-bit against the function it replaced, 400 bodies over 120 steps — and the lesson ends where it must: eight sweeps hold a five-crate tower and do not hold a ten-crate one, which is the honest capability of a Gauss–Seidel solver and the reason Lesson 8.11 writes a constraint as a Jacobian.

1. The problem: one pass, and two different failures

Here is where Lesson 8.9 left the engine. A crate falls onto a floor. The broadphase reports the pair, GJK and EPA say how deep and which way out, the manifold gives four contact points with ids that survive across frames, and solve_contacts applies an impulse at each of them that leaves the relative normal velocity exactly on target. Every stage works. The crate lands — and then it sinks, 275 mm in fifteen seconds, a quarter of its own height, and it never stops.

8.9 §12 diagnosed that carefully enough to hand this lesson two separate jobs, and the most useful thing you can do before writing a line of code is to keep them separate:

  1. One pass is not a converged solve. The four impulses are applied one after another, and each one disturbs the velocity the previous one just set. What is left over is 2.6 × 10⁻² m/s of approach speed — small, but it is a speed, so it accumulates into a distance without limit.
  2. The overlap the contact arrived with does not go away. Iterate the same solve thirty-two times and the residual vanishes — 9.9 × 10⁻⁹ — and the crate stops sinking. It stops at 7.2 mm inside the floor, which is where it was on the frame the contact was first detected, and no number of velocity iterations moves it by a micron.

Those are not two symptoms of one bug. They are a velocity error and a position error, and every equation in solver.hpp so far has been about velocity. §3 and §4 are about the first; §6, §7 and §8 are about the second. Conflating them is how people end up tuning an iteration count to fix a penetration, which does not work and cannot work, and §6 measures exactly how much it does not work.

And then there is a third thing, which is not a failure of the physics but of the shape of the program. body_world::step integrates velocities and positions in one call, so there is nowhere for a contact solve to go except after the body has already moved. §11 measures what that costs and the answer is a closed form: g·h² per resting step, forever, which at 60 Hz is 2.725 mm and puts a crate through a half-metre floor in four seconds.

2. Building intuition: a stack is a system

Start with the smallest interesting case, which is not two crates but one. A crate resting flat on a floor has a four-point manifold, and 8.9's solve walks those four points in turn. Point 1's impulse is computed to make the relative normal velocity at point 1 exactly zero, and it does. Then point 2's impulse is computed the same way — and it changes the crate's velocity, and its angular velocity, and therefore the velocity at point 1, which is no longer zero.

That is not an implementation mistake and there is no clever ordering that avoids it. The four impulses are coupled: any one of them moves the velocity at all four points, because they all act on one rigid body. What the four constraints ask for together is a system, and solving each one in isolation is not solving the system.

Worked, with real numbers. Take a 10 kg cube, half-extent 0.25 m, resting flat. Its inertia tensor about each axis is m(w² + h²)/12 = 10 × (0.25 + 0.25)/12 = 0.4167 kg·m². A contact at a corner has a lever arm of r = (±0.25, −0.25, ±0.25), so w = r × n with n = (0, 1, 0) is (−0.25, 0, 0.25) at one corner: length 0.3536. The angular term in the effective mass is w·(I⁻¹w) = 0.125 / 0.4167 = 0.300, against a linear term of 1/m = 0.1. So three quarters of the effect of an impulse at a corner is rotation — and rotation is exactly what is shared between the four points. An impulse at one corner lifts the opposite corner. That is the coupling, in one number.

Gauss–Seidel, and why sweeping again converges

The fix is the oldest idea in numerical linear algebra. Solve each constraint in turn, using the current velocities — which already include everything the earlier constraints in this sweep have done — and then sweep the whole set again. Each sweep leaves each constraint satisfied at the moment it was visited and slightly violated afterwards, but less violated than the sweep before, because the corrections compose rather than compete.

That is Gauss–Seidel, and the reason it converges here is worth stating precisely, because it is also the reason it converges slowly on a tall stack. Write the whole system as A j = b, where j is the vector of contact impulses, b is the vector of velocity errors to remove, and A is the matrix whose entry A[i][k] is "how much does a unit impulse at contact k change the relative velocity at contact i". The diagonal of A is the effective mass 8.9 derived — that is the same quantity, seen as one entry of a matrix. A Gauss–Seidel sweep is one pass of

\[ j_i \;\leftarrow\; j_i + \frac{1}{A_{ii}}\Bigl(b_i - \sum_k A_{ik}\,j_k\Bigr) \] j_i += (b_i - sum over k of A[i][k]*j_k) / A[i][i]

and the error after a sweep is the error before it multiplied by a matrix that depends only on A. So the error falls geometrically, by a constant factor per sweep, and that factor is the one number you actually want to know about a solver. §3 measures it.

We never build A, and that is the whole trick. The sum Σ A[i][k] j_k is "the relative velocity at contact i right now", which is three dot products on state the bodies already carry — so a sweep costs O(contacts) and needs no matrix, no allocation, and no knowledge of how the contacts are connected. That is why this shape scales to thousands of contacts where a direct solve would not, and it is also why it inherits Gauss–Seidel's weakness: information travels one contact per sweep.

There is one honest complication, and it is what makes this a solver rather than a linear solve. A contact may push and may not pull, so the true problem is not A j = b but A j ≥ b with j ≥ 0 and complementarity between them — a linear complementarity problem. Sequential impulses handle it with the max(0, …) 8.9 already wrote: each sweep solves each constraint as an equality and then projects the accumulated impulse back onto the admissible set. That is projected Gauss–Seidel, it is what every game physics engine of the last twenty years does, and it converges for the same reason the unconstrained version does. It is also why 8.9's insistence on clamping the accumulated impulse rather than the increment was not pedantry: the projection is onto the total, and at one pass the two happen to agree.

3. What a second sweep buys

One crate, four points, warm starting off and the position correction off so that iteration is the only variable. Ask the solver for n sweeps from an identical settled state and read off the residual — the worst |u_n − target| over the four points after the last sweep. 

   iters    residual m/s     ratio to previous
       1      2.0296e-02            -
       2      9.9972e-03       0.4926 per iteration
       4      3.0210e-03       0.5497 per iteration
       8      2.3918e-04       0.5305 per iteration
      16      1.6755e-06       0.5379 per iteration
      32      1.1418e-08       0.7321 per iteration   (at the float floor)
      64      1.1420e-08       1.0000 per iteration   (at the float floor)
   mean contraction over the 4 rows above the float floor: 0.5276

Each row doubles the iteration count, so the per-iteration factor is the n-th root of the row-to-row one. It comes out at 0.53 and stays there over four decades of residual, which is precisely the claim §2 derived: the error is multiplied by a constant matrix each sweep, so it decays geometrically. The last two rows are excluded from the mean and the reason is not tidiness — at 1.14 × 10⁻⁸ the residual has stopped being a measurement of the solver and started being a measurement of float. Including them would report a contraction of 0.64 where the solver's is 0.53.

Worked: choosing an iteration count from an accuracy target. You want the residual under 10⁻⁴ m/s, which is a tenth of a millimetre per second — a body that would drift six millimetres in a minute. Starting from 2.03 × 10⁻², you need to shrink by a factor of 203, and each sweep buys 1/0.53 = 1.89. So n = ln(203)/ln(1.89) = 5.30/0.636 = 8.3 sweeps. The table says eight gets you to 2.39 × 10⁻⁴ and sixteen to 1.68 × 10⁻⁶, which brackets the prediction exactly where it should.

And the residual is not an abstraction. Let the same crate run for fifteen seconds with no position correction, so that all that holds it up is the velocity solve, and the residual becomes a distance: 

   iters    sink mm     final residual m/s
       1     113.720       2.3728e-02
       2      27.360       1.4022e-02
       4       7.250       3.2872e-03
       8       1.121       2.4168e-04
      16       1.171       1.6315e-06
      32       2.155       6.5957e-09

Read the first four rows and the last two separately, because they are measuring different things. From one pass to eight the sink falls by a hundred-fold and tracks the residual: that is the creep being solved. From eight to thirty-two it stops falling and wobbles around a millimetre or two — which is not the solver getting worse. It is the arrival depth, which iteration has never had anything to do with, arriving early. §6 is about that number and this is the first place it shows itself.

The control

If iteration were helping for some reason other than the one claimed — a warm-up effect, an accumulator settling, something numerical — then a contact with nothing to iterate against would improve too. A sphere on a floor is one point of one manifold. There is no second impulse to disturb the first, so the very first pass should be exact and every later one should have nothing to do: 

  CONTROL, one sphere = one point = nothing to iterate against:
     1 pass 2.5247e-13   16 passes 2.5247e-13   improvement 1.0000x

2.5 × 10⁻¹³ is zero as far as a float is concerned, and sixteen passes reproduce it to the last bit. A four-point manifold is already a system — you do not need two bodies to need a solver, and that is worth internalising before the stacks arrive, because it means the thing you are debugging when a single crate misbehaves is the same thing you will be debugging when a pyramid does.

4. Warm starting, and the bug it was hiding

Eight sweeps of a four-point manifold is nothing. Eight sweeps of a five-crate tower's twenty points, every frame, is still nothing. The trouble is that a tower needs far more than eight from a cold start, and the reason is §2's last observation: a Gauss–Seidel sweep carries information across one contact, so a chain of five contacts needs roughly five sweeps before the bottom of it has heard about the top, and then several more before the answer settles.

But we are not starting cold. On the previous frame the solver worked out exactly how hard each point had to push, and on this frame — for a stack that has not moved — the answer is almost the same. That answer is already in the manifold. It has been since 8.7, and the machinery to get it there took four lessons:

The loop closes in prepare_contacts: with solver_config::warm_start set, each constraint's accumulator begins at the impulse the matched point inherited, and warm_start_contacts applies those impulses to the bodies once before the sweeps begin. The solve therefore starts from the velocity the scene would have if last frame's answer were still right — and on a settled stack it very nearly is. 

  A FIVE-CRATE TOWER, settled, asked to reach a residual of 1e-04:
     cold (every accumulator starts at zero):  400 iterations   (the limit; it did not get there)
     warm (each starts from last frame's):      89 iterations

  AT THE EIGHT ITERATIONS THIS ENGINE SHIPS, over 20 s, no position
  correction, so all that holds the tower up is the velocity solve:
     warm    top crate sink mm    deepest mm    final residual
     no             2019.78       199.919      2.7324e-04
     yes              40.14         9.165      4.6848e-07

  THE MATCH RATE on a settled tower, 600 frames: 11708 of 11994 points
  inherited an impulse (97.62%).

Two metres of sink is not a tower that settles badly; it is a tower that has fallen over and into the floor. The warm one holds within four centimetres of where it was built, at the same eight iterations and the same cost — because the impulses are already there. Warm starting is free in time and costs one bool.

What it is, exactly, and what it is not

A warm start is an initial guess. It does not make the solve more accurate; it starts the iteration closer to the answer. That distinction is not philosophy, because it predicts something testable: a guess that is wrong should be worse than no guess at all. Scale every inherited impulse by a known factor before the solve and watch: 

     scale    iters to 1e-04    residual at 8    top crate m/s after 1 s
       0.0               400       2.3484e-02                    0.5975
       0.5               400       1.3542e-02                    0.3209
       1.0                89       1.3309e-04                    0.0048
       2.0               400       3.8701e-02                    0.6080
       5.0                52       2.7670e-01                    1.5402

Doubling the inherited impulse is worse than discarding it: 2.0 fails to converge in 400 sweeps where 0.0 also fails, but with a larger residual and six times the leftover motion. That is the prediction confirmed, and it is also the argument for the accumulator clamp — a warm start hands the solver a number it has no reason to trust, and the projection onto j ≥ 0 is what stops an untrustworthy number becoming an unbounded one.

And then read the ×5 row, because it is the warning that goes with every convergence number in this lesson. Fifty-two iterations looks like the second-best result in the table. The last column says the tower is moving at 1.54 m/s: the solver has thrown the stack into the air, and a solver that has launched everything has nothing left to disagree with itself about. The residual measures consistency, not correctness. Every time this lesson quotes one, there is a second measurement beside it saying where the bodies actually are.

The knob 8.7 left at zero

manifold_config::keep_slop keeps clipped points that are slightly separated rather than only ones that penetrate, and 8.7 left it at zero so that every number that lesson printed was a real overlap. Its doc comment says a solver wants a small positive value, because a point a tenth of a millimetre clear this frame is a contact next frame, and having it already in the manifold with its impulse warm is what stops a stack breathing. That claim is now testable: 

     keep_slop    mean points/frame    inherited %
         0.000               19.990         97.62
         0.002               19.985         98.09
         0.010               20.000         98.17

Half a percent. It is real, it is in the direction the doc comment predicted, and it is much smaller than that comment implies — twenty points a frame out of a possible twenty means the manifolds were essentially complete already, because the position correction is holding the tower at a depth where all four corners of every contact are genuinely overlapping. The knob earns its default of zero. Turn it up when your contacts are marginal; do not turn it up hoping to fix a stack.

And the bug warm starting was hiding

Here is the thing that came out of building this, and it is three lessons old.

contact_point::tangent_impulse is two floats. 8.9's doc comment calls them "the accumulated tangential impulses in the batch's tangent basis", and that is exactly what they are: coordinates. A coordinate is meaningless without the frame it was measured in, and the frame was never stored anywhere. Each frame, prepare_contacts calls tangent_basis(normal, t1, t2) and gets a fresh basis — and tangent_basis, reasonably enough, seeds its cross product from whichever axis the normal is least aligned with, by comparing the magnitudes of the normal's components.

On a crate resting on a floor the normal is very nearly (0, 1, 0). Which means the choice of basis comes down to a comparison between |n.x| and |n.z|, two numbers that are both about 10⁻⁵ and wander around each other from frame to frame. When they cross, the basis rotates by ninety degrees — and last frame's friction, applied as though nothing had happened, acts sideways. 

     manifold-frames whose basis rotated by more than 30 deg:
     113 of 11830 (0.96%), worst rotation 134.6 deg

     basis stored    sideways drift mm    top crate y    standing
     yes                        155.43         4.7030         yes
     no (pre-8.10)              379.54         4.6923         yes

The fix is two dot products and it is exact: store the basis on the manifold alongside the impulses, rebuild the world-space friction impulse in the frame it was measured in, and re-measure it in this frame's. contact_manifold gains a vec3 tangent[2], write_back fills it, carry_impulses carries it, and prepare_contacts rotates. Twenty-four bytes per manifold.

The bug class is worth more than the bug. Nothing in the type system was ever going to catch this: both versions are two floats called tangent_impulse, both compile, both look right in a debugger, and the symptom — a tower that leans — names no subsystem at all. What found it was a probe: having decided the drift was suspicious, compute the basis independently in the harness and count how often it moves. Whenever you cache a quantity that lives in a frame, cache the frame. 8.9 learned the same lesson in the opposite direction, when the restitution artifact turned out to be about when a velocity was read rather than what it was.

And be honest about the size of it: at 24 iterations the fix takes the tower's sideways drift from 380 mm to 155, which is a factor of 2.4 and not a factor of ten. At eight iterations the ten-crate tower falls over either way, for the reason §14 measures. This is a correctness fix — the old code was applying a cached vector in the wrong frame, which is simply wrong — and the measurement is what keeps the claim proportionate.

5. The order within a sweep

A Gauss–Seidel sweep uses each impulse as soon as it is computed, which means it carries information in the direction it walks. A tower is held up from the bottom, so solving it top-down means each contact is solved against a support that has not been established yet; solving it bottom-up means each contact already knows what is under it. Every write-up of sequential impulses says so, usually with some confidence.

This engine does not sort. The order is whatever the caller added the manifolds in, grouped by island — which is cell-bucket order out of 8.8's grid, and therefore arbitrary. Before defending that, it is worth finding out what a sort would have been worth. 

     order        n=1         n=4         n=16        n=64        n=256
     as added     3.798e-02   2.152e-02   1.090e-02   3.357e-03   3.623e-04
     bottom-up    3.798e-02   2.152e-02   1.090e-02   3.357e-03   3.623e-04
     top-down     2.122e-02   2.485e-02   1.156e-02   3.621e-03   3.676e-04
     bottom-up against top-down: 1.06x at n=16, 1.08x at n=64

Eight per cent. At §3's measured contraction of 0.53 per iteration, a factor of 1.08 is worth ln(1.08)/ln(1/0.53) = 0.12 of an iteration — which is to say, sorting the whole scene every frame buys you about an eighth of one sweep. The folklore makes more of solve order than this fixture supports.

Two things in that table are worth more than the headline. The first is that the "as added" row is identical to "bottom-up" — the broadphase's cell order happens to run up the tower, which means the default is not a coin toss on this scene and would not be on most. The second is the control: 

     bottom-up 0.247181252 m   top-down 0.247181252 m   difference 0.000e+00

With no chain of contacts there is no direction for information to travel in, and the two orders do exactly the same arithmetic in a different sequence — bit for bit, over two seconds of simulation. That is what says the 8% above is the chain and not something else.

And the sort is not free of design, even where it is free of cost. "Bottom-up" means "by height", which is a statement about gravity pointing down. An engine whose levels have ceilings to hang from, ropes, and rotating platforms has no such axis. The general version is a topological order of the contact graph, per island, which is a sort per frame rather than a comparison — and it would need the graph, which §9 builds for a different reason. This engine ships the broadphase's order and names it, which is more honest than implying that an accident is a decision.

6. The frozen overlap

Now the other failure, and it starts with a claim that turned out to be the wrong shape.

A converged velocity solve leaves the relative normal velocity at zero. That means the overlap stops growing. It says nothing whatever about the overlap that is already there — and there is always some, because collide_manifold reports nothing at all until the shapes actually overlap. The first frame on which a contact exists is the frame after the body crossed the surface. So the first thing a solver ever sees is a contact that is already too deep, and the obvious claim is that it is one step of travel deep: v·h.

That claim is wrong by up to 81%, and the measurement is more interesting than the claim was. 

     drop m    arrival m/s    v*h mm     min     mean     max
       0.10         2.1255     35.425   0.005    0.486    0.919
       0.50         3.5970     59.950   0.004    0.489    0.950
       2.00         6.5400    109.000   0.002    0.505    0.972
     largest fraction seen anywhere: 0.9725;  means are within 0.014 of 0.5

v·h is a ceiling, not an equality, and once the measurement says so the reason is obvious: the body crosses the surface at some instant inside a step, and the next sample is taken a whole step later. How much of that step was left over depends on where the body happened to be when the step began, which is to say it is arbitrary. So the arrival depth is uniform on [0, v·h], its mean is half the ceiling, and only the ceiling is predictable.

Worked, with real numbers. A crate dropped 0.5 m arrives at √(2gh) = √(2 × 9.81 × 0.5) = 3.13 m/s in continuous time; the measurement reads 3.597, which is larger because a 60 Hz semi-implicit step overshoots the fall slightly and because the drop is measured to the crate's face. One step at that speed is 3.597/60 = 59.95 mm, which is the ceiling in the table. The deepest arrival over 200 finely spaced drops was 0.950 of it — 56.9 mm — and the mean was 0.489, or 29.3 mm. Half a crate-depth of overlap, waiting for the solver on the very first frame.

And no amount of iteration touches it: 

     iters    depth mm       residual m/s
         1     17.6959         2.1986e-09
         2     18.1701         1.9977e-09
         4     18.2592         1.5694e-09
         8     17.7703         2.3629e-09
        16     17.7503         3.0436e-09
        32     17.7501         5.4689e-09
        64     17.7501         5.4689e-09
     over a 64x range of iteration count the depth moves 0.5633 mm (3.09%)
     and the residual moves by orders of magnitude.

Three per cent, over a sixty-four-fold range of the knob people reach for. That is as close to a controlled experiment as this lesson gets: the residual falls by whatever you ask of it and the depth does not move, because they are two different problems and iteration is the answer to one of them.

The control is the other end of the same statement. If the frozen depth really is bounded by one step of approach travel, then a body arriving arbitrarily slowly must arrive arbitrarily shallow. Lower a crate onto the floor at 1 cm/s with gravity switched off: the ceiling is 0.01/60 = 0.1667 mm and the measurement is 0.1630. Which is also the bound on how good position_correction::none can be — a scene in which nothing ever arrives faster than a few centimetres per second does not need a position correction at all, and knowing that is worth more than the correction is.

7. Baumgarte, and the energy it adds

A position error needs a position fix, and the oldest one is to smuggle it into the velocity solve. If the contact is d metres too deep, ask the solver not merely to stop the approach but to produce a small separating velocity — enough to remove a fraction of d over the coming step. The bodies then push apart as well as refusing to approach, and the overlap shrinks.

The arithmetic is one line. To remove β·d metres of overlap in h seconds you need a relative separating velocity of

\[ b \;=\; \frac{\beta\,\max(0,\; d - s)}{h} \] bias = beta * max(0, depth - slop) / h

where s is the penetration slop §8 is about. Add b to the target velocity in solve_one_normal and you are done. This is Baumgarte stabilisation, from a 1972 paper on constrained dynamics by way of every rigid-body write-up since, and it is what every first implementation does.

The time constant, in closed form

Each step removes the fraction β of whatever excess remains, so after n steps the excess is (1 − β)ⁿ of what it started at. That is an exponential wearing a discrete hat:

\[ (1-\beta)^n \;=\; e^{\,n\ln(1-\beta)} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \tau \;=\; \frac{-h}{\ln(1-\beta)} \] (1 - beta)^n = exp(n ln(1 - beta)), so tau = -h / ln(1 - beta)

Worked. At β = 0.2 and h = 1/60: ln(0.8) = −0.22314, so τ = (1/60)/0.22314 = 0.0747 s — 74.7 ms. Four and a half frames to remove 63% of an overlap, twenty frames to remove 99%. Notice what is not in that expression: the mass, the depth, the number of contacts. A Baumgarte correction settles at the same rate for a pebble and a piano. 

     beta    predicted tau ms    measured tau ms    ratio
     0.05              324.93              333.33    1.026
     0.10              158.19              166.67    1.054
     0.20               74.69               83.33    1.116
     0.40               32.63               33.33    1.022
     worst deviation: 11.57%, and one 16.67 ms step is 51.1% of the
     shortest row — the measurement cannot be finer than a frame.

Every measured value is the predicted one rounded up to a whole frame, which is the finest a quantity sampled once per step can be. That is agreement, and stating the resolution is what makes it agreement rather than a 12% error.

The first draft of that table had gravity switched off, and it came out 33% too fast at β = 0.05 and dead on at β = 0.40. An error that grows as a parameter shrinks is a second effect, not a wrong constant — 8.9 §8's rule, arriving again. The second effect is this section's whole subject: an unheld crate keeps the correction velocity, so it coasts out at the largest bias it ever saw instead of decaying with the overlap. Geometric decay is what a correction does to a body that is being pushed back in, which is every body in a real stack. The fixture had to have gravity in it.

And then the bill

The bias is a velocity the contact was never asked for, and the solver cannot tell it from a real one. When the overlap runs out, the body is still carrying it. Put a crate 100 mm inside the floor with gravity switched off, so that a correction which added nothing would leave it exactly at rest on the surface:

         50.0           0.5400    1.4582e+00        14.9 mm

Six and a half joules, from nowhere, on a 10 kg crate. The control is what makes that the correction's doing rather than something else's: switch the correction off and the crate sits where it was put, motionless, forever.

With gravity on, this is mostly hidden, which is exactly why it survives in so many engines. The push has to climb, and below a certain starting depth it never gets out:

At 200 mm it is thrown 99 mm clear of the floor, and at 300 it reaches 215. The −5.000 in most rows is the penetration slop: a crate resting correctly.

The clamp is the difference between a push and a launch, and it is one min:

         100.0                3.5400                  368.523

Worked: why the clamp is not optional. A body spawned a whole metre inside a wall — every project does this, usually by dragging an object in an editor — is told to leave at β·d/h = 0.2 × 1.0 × 60 = 12 m/s. Under Baumgarte that is a real velocity it keeps. Twelve metres per second is a body that crosses a room in a quarter of a second and tunnels through everything on the way, from a placement mistake of one metre.

8. Split impulse, and the slop

The energy is not an accident of Baumgarte's arithmetic. It is structural: the correction is expressed as a velocity, and a velocity is a thing a body keeps. So do the same arithmetic against a velocity the body does not keep.

Give each body a second, shadow velocity — a pseudo_velocity, twenty-four bytes, zeroed at the start of every step. Run the same solve against it, with the same lever arms, the same effective masses and the same non-negativity clamp, targeting the same bias. Then, at the end of the step, move the bodies by pseudo * h as well as by velocity * h, and throw the pseudo velocity away.

That is Erin Catto's split impulse, and the accounting is exact: the position moves by the same amount Baumgarte would have moved it, and the momentum the body carries into the next step is untouched. Nothing survives. 

     start mm    correction       departure m/s    final depth mm
         10.0    Baumgarte              0.06001            0.0000
         10.0    split impulse          0.00000            5.0000
         50.0    Baumgarte              0.54004            0.0000
         50.0    split impulse          0.00000            5.0000
        100.0    Baumgarte              1.14006            0.0000
        100.0    split impulse          0.00000            5.0000

  AND IT IS NOT SLOWER. Time to remove 1/e of a 50 mm overlap on a held
  crate, against the closed form's 74.69 ms:
     Baumgarte  83.33 ms     split impulse  83.33 ms     difference 0.00 ms

Zero departure at every depth, at the same time constant to the frame. The last two columns of the first table are worth reading together: Baumgarte reaches zero final depth because it does not stop at the surface, and split impulse reaches 5.000 mm because it stops at the slop, which is where a resting contact belongs.

It costs a second solve. §13 prices one position iteration at 46% of a velocity one, which is about what you would guess from what it leaves out: no friction to clip, no restitution, one target per point instead of three. Three position iterations against eight velocity ones is the default, and §13's table is why.

The position pass runs entirely after the velocity pass, not interleaved with it. The separations it reads are ones the velocity pass is about to make stale, so interleaving spends corrections on a geometry that is still moving. It is also the reason contact_constraint needs a second accumulator, pseudo_impulse: the position problem is its own sequence of inequalities and needs its own projection onto j ≥ 0, for exactly the reason 8.9 gave for the first one.

The slop, measured rather than assumed

Every engine has a penetration slop and every explanation of it is the same: a correction that drives the overlap to exactly zero has no rest state, because at zero depth the contact separates, next frame's gravity puts it back, and the cycle has no fixed point. The stack breathes at 60 Hz. The slop gives it somewhere to sit.

That is a testable claim, so this section set out to test it, and the measurement refused it. 

     slop mm   1 crate split   1 crate Baum.   5 tower split   resting depth mm
        0.00            0.00            0.00         1801.25              1.204
        0.50            0.00            0.00         9085.89              3.109
        2.00            0.00            0.00            0.00              1.999
        5.00            0.00            0.00            0.00              4.999
       20.00            0.00            0.00            0.00              9.165
     (micrometres. A crate is half a metre.)

A single resting crate is bit-for-bit motionless at a slop of zero, under either correction. It has to be: the correction is geometric, so it removes 20% of what is left each step and never reaches zero, which means the contact never separates and there is nothing to oscillate. The folklore describes a correction that removes the whole overlap in one step, which is β = 1, which nobody ships.

What actually needs the slop is contacts that compete. Five crates at zero slop move 1.80 mm peak to peak; at half a millimetre, 9.09 mm; and at two millimetres and above, nothing at all. Each contact in a stack asks for its own separation, they cannot all have it simultaneously, and the slop is the band inside which they stop asking.

And the cost of the knob is in the last column, exactly: the resting depth is the slop. Five millimetres buys complete stillness and is invisible under a half-metre crate. It is also the one place this engine's "one unit is one metre" convention bites, because five millimetres is enormous under a marble — scale it with your content, the way time_scale_for_length_scale from 8.2 says to scale everything else.

9. Islands

Everything so far solves all the contacts, every step. On a level with ten thousand crates in twenty separate rooms, that is a great deal of arithmetic establishing that nothing in room seventeen has anything to say to room three.

Two bodies are in the same island when a chain of contacts joins them. Everything in an island has to be solved together, because an impulse anywhere in it propagates everywhere in it; and nothing in one island can influence another this step, by construction. Three things fall out of that, and only the first is what people usually build islands for:

  1. Sleeping becomes decidable. "Is this body quiet?" is not a question you can usefully answer about one body — the crate on top of a pile is perfectly quiet right up until the crate at the bottom is nudged. "Is this whole island quiet?" is answerable, and §10 is why it is the only version of the question worth asking.
  2. The solve is parallelisable. Two islands share no body, so two threads can solve them with no synchronisation at all — no locks, no atomics, no false sharing beyond whatever the allocator did. Module 9's job system is where that is collected; the partition is here, and it is the hard part.
  3. Convergence is per-island. A residual of 10⁻⁶ in one tower is not improved by sweeping the tower on the other side of the level, so an early-out can be taken island by island. This engine does not take one, because a joint changes what "converged" means and that is 8.11's problem.

The one rule

Building the partition is a textbook union–find over the contact graph. The rule that makes it useful is one sentence, and getting it wrong is the difference between a feature and nothing at all.

A body that cannot move is not a bridge. The floor touches everything. Let a contact through a fixed body join its two neighbours and every object standing on the ground is in one island — so a single rolling marble anywhere in the level keeps ten thousand crates awake forever. That is not a slow simulation; it is no sleeping at all, in exactly the scene sleeping exists for. 

     islands reported by the solver:            20
     manifolds:                                 100

  CONTROL, the same contacts through a union-find that lets a FIXED body
  join its neighbours:
     fixed bodies are not bridges (this engine):   20 islands
     fixed bodies ARE bridges (the usual bug):      1 islands

  THE PARTITION: 20 islands, smallest 5 bodies, largest 5,
  100 bodies placed of 100 dynamic (the floor is in none).

  COST: 101 bodies, 100 contacts, union-find and labelling:
     0.250 us  (2.48 ns per body)

Twenty against one, on the same contacts, with the rule as the only difference. And the rule is safe rather than merely convenient: an impulse applied to a fixed body changes nothing that any other contact can read, because its velocity is never written. It propagates nothing, so it joins nothing. A kinematic body is the same — gameplay owns its velocity and the solver cannot change it — so a lift does not weld its passengers to the floor beneath it.

A body with no contacts at all gets its own island of one. That is not a special case in the implementation and it is the right answer: a crate in flight is quiet nowhere, so its island never sleeps.

The output array is the scratch. A union–find wants a parent array of n ints and the answer is a label array of n ints, and they are never both live — the parents are finished with the moment the labels begin. So island_of is the parent array for the first half of build_islands and the answer for the second, and the whole thing allocates nothing on a scene whose size has not changed. It costs one trick, which is that a root's label has to be encoded so it cannot be mistaken for a parent index — and the obvious encoding, −label − 1, sends label 0 to −1, which is already the sentinel for "no island". §12 is the hour that cost.

10. Sleeping

An island whose every body has been quiet for long enough stops being simulated. Nothing integrates it and none of its contacts are solved, which on a settled scene is nearly all of the cost: 

  A YARD OF TWENTY TOWERS falls asleep at frame 168 (2.80 s):
     sleeping bodies 100 of 100, sleeping islands 20 of 20
     manifolds handed in 100, manifolds SOLVED 0
     solve cost asleep 3.041 us   awake 130.750 us   43.0x

Forty-three times, and the honest half of it in the next two lines: the broadphase and the narrow phase are unchanged. Sleeping saves the solve and the integration, not the detection — a sleeping body still has its bounding box gridded and its pairs tested every frame, because that is how it finds out something has arrived.

A caller can take most of the rest back, because two bodies that are both asleep cannot have moved relative to each other, so their pair does not need testing: 51.5 µs → 7.1, with 80 of 100 pairs skipped. It costs one frame of wake-up lag on the second body of a sleeping pair, and it is a caller-side rule rather than an engine one, because only the caller knows whether its narrow phase has side effects.

The test cannot run before the solve

This is the finding that is not in the textbooks, and it is 8.9's restitution artifact wearing a different hat.

A semi-implicit step applies gravity to every dynamic body before the solver looks. So a crate that has been motionless on a floor for a minute is, at that instant, travelling downward at g·h — 0.1635 m/s at 60 Hz, which is more than three times any sleep threshold a designer would accept. A sleep test placed there never fires, at any setting, and the symptom is indistinguishable from thresholds that are merely too tight: 

     read BEFORE the solve:    0 of 100 quiet, fastest 0.1836 m/s
     read AFTER the solve:    96 of 100 quiet, fastest 0.0629 m/s
     g*h at 60 Hz is 0.1635 m/s, which is 3.3x the threshold.

So the sleep test runs at the very end of contact_solver::solve, after the write-back. Waking, though, has to run at the beginning — an island that has just acquired a moving neighbour must be solved on the frame it acquires it, not the frame after — and those are two different things that read like one. wake_islands and update_sleep are separate functions for that reason and no other.

Waking, which is the half that has to be right

Falling asleep is an optimisation. Waking is correctness: a body that should have woken and did not is a crate hanging in the air, a door that will not open, a lift that ignores the player standing on it. The rule that does almost all of it is one sentence, and it needs no event, no callback and no dirty list:

An island is asleep only when every one of its bodies is; otherwise all of them are woken. A moving body that collides with a sleeping pile is joined to it by build_islands, so the pile's island now contains something awake, so the pile wakes. The graph does the work. 

     first frame anything else woke: 11 (0.183 s after the throw)
     bodies awake on that frame: 6 of 21
     still awake 3 s later: 4

Six of twenty-one on the frame of contact: the thrown crate and all five of the tower it hit. The other three towers never notice.

Why per island, and not per body

A body asleep is, to everything else in the simulation, an immovable body: the solver still sees its contacts, and nothing integrates it. So sleeping the top crate of a tower on its own turns it into a fixed body, and fixed bodies do not fall. That is emulated exactly by setting kind = body_kind::fixed, which is not an approximation — it is the same statement made in the type system: 

     rule          bottom crate moved m    top crate fell m    top awake
     per island                 70.5702              0.5000           no
     per body                   63.2080              0.0000          n/a

The island rule knocks the bottom crate seventy metres down the floor and the tower comes down half a metre onto it. The per-body rule knocks the bottom crate sixty-three metres away and the two above it stay exactly where they were, hanging in the air with nothing underneath them, indefinitely. That is the whole argument for the island being the unit, in one number that is exactly zero.

And note which experiment this is. The obvious one — teleport the support away — is unfair, because a support removed by teleport is invisible to both rules: nothing collided, so nothing in the contact graph changed. That is what wake is for, and it is why its doc comment says to call it after anything that writes to a body from outside the step. The experiment that separates the two rules has to involve a collision.

The thresholds, and the clock

Two thresholds, because there are two quantities: a linear speed in m/s and an angular one in rad/s. They are not derivable from each other — a half-metre crate turning at 0.1 rad/s has corner speeds of 3.5 cm/s, so one number in metres per second cannot express both without knowing the body's size.

And the linear threshold has a floor that the solver sets, not the designer. A settled stack does not reach zero velocity; it reaches whatever residual the iteration count leaves, and a threshold below that residual never fires: 

     threshold m/s    bodies asleep    time to sleep s
            0.0005               60              never
            0.0020               70              never
            0.0100               75              never
            0.0200              100              19.15
            0.0500              100              2.800
            0.1000              100              0.950
            0.2000              100              0.566

The default of 0.05 is a factor of 2.5 above the floor, not the comfortable margin it looks like, and one notch below it a quarter of the crates never qualify however long you wait. A stiffer scene — a taller stack, a heavier lid — leaves more residual and wants a larger threshold. The symptom of getting it wrong is a pile that is visibly still and measurably expensive, which is a bug nobody files.

The timer is what stops an oscillation being mistaken for rest: a crate rocking on one edge is momentarily motionless twice a cycle, and an engine sleeping on the instantaneous test freezes it mid-rock at a visible angle. Half a second, which is thirty frames.

Which is also why wake resets the clock and not only the flag — and the obvious test does not show it. A woken body that is then shoved does not care, because it is no longer a sleep candidate and update_sleep resets its timer on the first frame anyway: both versions travel 1.0027 m. The case that shows it is the quiet one. 

     what happens next     flag only    wake() as shipped
     shoved at 2 m/s        1.0027 m     1.0027 m
     woken and left alone      1 frames     30 frames until asleep again

One frame against thirty. A body woken so that gameplay can do something with it next frame is asleep again before gameplay gets there.

11. Where the solve goes in the step

Everything above assumes the contact solve happens between the two halves of a step: after the velocities have been integrated, before the positions have. body_world::step does both in one call, so until this lesson there was nowhere to put it.

You could solve before the step, but then gravity is applied afterwards and overrides whatever the solver approved. You could solve after — which is what every caller does when step is the only entry point — and the body then moves at the velocity gravity asked for rather than the one the contact allowed. That costs a closed form:

\[ \Delta y \;=\; v\,h \;=\; (g\,h)\,h \;=\; g\,h^2 \quad\text{per resting step} \] sink per step = (g*h)*h = g*h^2

Worked. g·h² = 9.81/3600 = 2.725 mm at 60 Hz. Every step, forever, for a body that is doing nothing at all. Four seconds of that is 654 mm, which is a crate through a half-metre floor. 

     slot             sink after 4 s    per step mm    g*h^2 mm
     mid step (ours)          0.0000        0.00000     2.72500
     end of step            654.0007        2.72500     2.72500
     measured 2.72500 mm per step against a predicted 2.72500 — ratio 1.0000

Four decimal places, and a ratio of exactly 1.0000. That is the kind of agreement you get when the thing being measured is arithmetic rather than physics, and it is worth having because it means the diagnosis is certain: if your bodies sink at 2.7 mm a step, your solve is in the wrong place, and no amount of iteration or correction will help.

Splitting the step, and checking that nothing moved

So body_world::step becomes two functions with a gap between them: 

world.integrate_velocities(h);
// broadphase, narrow phase, contact_solver::solve(h)
world.integrate_positions(h);

step(h) is kept and is now literally the composition of the two, because most of the engine has no contacts to insert and a caller who writes the two lines by hand has written a bug waiting for the day somebody reorders them.

The split is only possible because the integrator is semi-implicit Euler. Its two lines — v += a·h then x += v·h — are independent statements, so taking the first here and the second there is an identity. Explicit Euler's position line reads the velocity from before the update and velocity Verlet's needs the acceleration as well, so neither is separable; for those two rules integrate_velocities runs the whole integration and a contact impulse arrives one step late. That is another entry in 8.1's ledger against them, and it is not a reason to use them.

A refactor that is "obviously" behaviour-preserving is exactly the kind that is not, so the harness keeps a private copy of the monolithic function this replaced and runs both over the same scene — 400 bodies with mixed masses, damping, angular damping, gravity scales, gyroscopic modes and kinds, 120 steps, compared by memcmp: 

     bit-identical bodies: 400 of 400
     worst position difference: 0.000e+00 m

Two things in the split are not free, and both are named rather than hidden. The first is gyroscopic_mode::momentum, whose derivation advances the orientation in the middle of itself — a half step to estimate where the body will be pointing, then the full step, then the angular velocity re-derived at the new orientation. That block cannot be cut in two, so all of it runs in the velocity half and the position half skips the orientation of a body in that mode. The consequence is one frame of lag on spin for a contact impulse applied in the gap, on a mode that is off by default.

The second is a cost: 

     monolithic    58.167 us   14.542 ns/body
     split         79.750 us   19.938 ns/body   +37.1%

     AND WHAT PAYS FOR IT: the same 101-body yard, integrated
     asleep 0.125 us and awake 1.958 us — 15.7x.

A second mat3_from_quat per dynamic body per step, because two functions cannot share a local variable. 8.3 §12's largest single saving was hoisting exactly that call so the angular half built the rotation once and used it twice; splitting the function gives half of it back. Thirty-seven per cent is not nothing, and it is paid gladly, because §10's sleeping removes both halves for most of the bodies in a settled scene.

12. Writing it

Nine pieces, and the order below is the order they were written, because each one is testable before the next exists.

12.1 The position correction, as a target

Baumgarte's bias is the one prepared quantity that depends on the length of the step, which is why it is not computed in prepare_contacts. That function's other outputs — lever arms, effective masses, the restitution target — are facts about the contact at an instant; this is a fact about the contact and the schedule. 

const float inv_h = 1.0f / h;
for (int i = 0; i < batch.count; ++i)
{
    contact_constraint& c = batch.points[i];
    const float excess = std::max(0.0f, -c.separation - cfg.penetration_slop);
    c.bias = std::min(cfg.baumgarte * excess * inv_h, cfg.max_correction_speed);
}

Three decisions in three lines. The slop comes off first and the result is clamped at zero, so a contact resting at the slop asks for nothing (§8). The division by h turns a displacement into a velocity. And the min is §7's clamp.

12.2 …and where it is applied

The same number drives both corrections, and what differs is whose velocity it pushes on. solve_contacts adds it to the target only under Baumgarte: 

const bool use_bias = cfg.correction == position_correction::baumgarte;
...
report.normal_impulse += solve_one_normal(a, b, batch, c, use_bias ? c.bias : 0.0f);

and solve_one_normal gains one term: 

float delta = (c.target_normal_velocity + bias - vn) * c.normal_mass;

const float total = std::max(0.0f, c.normal_impulse + delta);
delta = total - c.normal_impulse;
c.normal_impulse = total;

12.3 The position solve, in the shadow

Structurally identical, against pseudo_velocity instead of the bodies. Notice what it does not take: 

float solve_positions(contact_batch& batch, pseudo_velocity& pa, pseudo_velocity& pb,
                      const solver_config& cfg)
{
    ...
    const vec3 u = (pb.linear + cross(pb.angular, c.r_b))
                   - (pa.linear + cross(pa.angular, c.r_a));
    const float vn = dot(u, batch.normal);

    float delta = (c.bias - vn) * c.normal_mass;

    const float total = std::max(0.0f, c.pseudo_impulse + delta);
    delta = total - c.pseudo_impulse;
    c.pseudo_impulse = total;
    ...
}

Everything it needs — the lever arms, the inverse masses, the world inverse inertia tensors, the effective masses — was hoisted into contact_batch by prepare_contacts, and the bodies themselves were only ever the place the velocity happened to live. That is the shape 8.11 generalises: a constraint is a Jacobian, an effective mass and a clamp, and the solver never needs to know what it is constraining.

Every point is solved, not just the penetrating ones. A point resting at the slop has a target of zero, and solving it is what stops a neighbouring point's correction from levering this corner further into the floor.

And the pseudo velocity moves the orientation as well as the position, which is not decoration: a crate resting on one corner is corrected at that corner, the correction is off-centre, and what the contact wants is for the crate to level. Translate only and it climbs out of the floor sideways, sliding along a surface it is not sliding on.

12.4 The tangent basis, rotated

§4's bug, fixed with two dot products: 

if (length_squared(m.tangent[0]) > 0.5f)
{
    const vec3 world = m.tangent[0] * cp.tangent_impulse[0]
                       + m.tangent[1] * cp.tangent_impulse[1];
    c.tangent_impulse[0] = dot(world, batch.tangent[0]);
    c.tangent_impulse[1] = dot(world, batch.tangent[1]);
}

Rebuild the world-space impulse in the frame it was measured in; re-measure it in this one. The length_squared > 0.5 is the "no basis stored" case — a manifold that has never been through a solver, whose impulses are zero and whose frame is therefore moot.

12.5 Islands, in place

Union–find with path halving, using the output array as the parent array, and the encoding trick §9's callout describes: 

for (int i = 0; i < n; ++i)
{
    const std::size_t u = static_cast<std::size_t>(i);
    if (island_of[u] >= 0) { island_of[u] = find_root(island_of, i); }
}

int count = 0;
for (int i = 0; i < n; ++i)
{
    const std::size_t u = static_cast<std::size_t>(i);
    if (island_of[u] == i) { island_of[u] = -count - 2; ++count; }
}

−count − 2, and the 2 is not a typo. The obvious −label − 1 sends label 0 to −1, which is already this array's sentinel for "no island" — so the first island in every scene silently ceased to exist, its contacts were never grouped and never solved, and a tower whose bottom crate happened to land in it fell through the floor while every other tower stood.

What found that was not the falling crate. A crate falling through a floor has a hundred plausible causes and the geometry pipeline is five lessons of them. What found it was stats.points == 0 on a frame where deepest() was reporting 57 mm — two instruments disagreeing with each other. Neither was wrong about what it measured; they could not both be measuring the same manifold. Keep enough counters that they can contradict one another.

12.6 The grouping

A counting sort, exactly as 8.8's grid sorts proxies into cells and for the same payoff: every island's members end up adjacent in one contiguous array, so the sleep pass can take a range and Module 9's job system can take one per thread. Count, prefix-sum, scatter — and the body_count field doubles as the fill cursor, restored by the scatter itself.

12.7 Waking, before; sleeping, after

wake_islands reads flags the previous step set and clears them on any island that is not entirely asleep. update_sleep advances the timers and puts whole islands out. §10 is why they are at opposite ends of solve. 

bool quiet = cfg.enabled;
for (int k = 0; quiet && k < isl.body_count; ++k)
{
    const rigid_body& b = bodies[island_bodies[isl.first_body + k]];
    if (!b.allow_sleep
        || !is_sleep_candidate(b, cfg.linear_threshold, cfg.angular_threshold))
    {
        quiet = false;
    }
}

One veto is enough, which is the all-or-nothing rule: a single moving crate at the bottom of a pile keeps the pile awake, and that is not a compromise — it is the correct answer, because the pile is about to move.

And a body that falls asleep has its velocities zeroed. That is not tidiness: a body frozen at 4 cm/s resumes at 4 cm/s when something wakes it half a second later, in a direction that no longer means anything, and until then its momentum keeps appearing in step_report so the scene's conservation laws report a drift that nothing is causing. Two lines, and they make "asleep" a state rather than a pause.

12.8 The loop

Thirty lines, and the only subtlety is what is inside it and what is outside: 

// 1. the contact graph, and the counting sort
// 2. wake_islands — before the solve
// 3. per awake manifold: prepare_contacts, prepare_bias, warm_start_contacts   ONCE
// 4. velocity_iterations x { per awake manifold: solve_contacts }
// 5. position_iterations x { per awake manifold: solve_positions }
//    then apply_pseudo_velocity per awake body
// 6. write_back per awake manifold
// 7. update_sleep — after the solve

Stage 3's warm start is once per step, and that is 8.9's scar: the first draft of that lesson put it inside the iteration loop, and with sixteen iterations the inherited impulse was applied sixteen times. A crate dropped on a 20-degree ramp slid 810 mm down a slope it should have gripped — and it did not look like "warm starting is applied too often", it looked like friction had stopped working, because the spurious normal impulses had launched the crate off the surface.

12.9 The caller's side

Worth writing out once in full, because the ordering constraints are all in it: 

cfg.restitution_bias = world.gravity() * h;

world.integrate_velocities(h);

// broadphase
grid.build(proxies, bp);

// narrow phase, with 8.7's cache in the middle
cache.begin_frame();
manifolds.reserve(grid.pairs().size());     // the solver holds POINTERS into this
for (const broadphase_pair& p : grid.pairs())
{
    contact_manifold m = collide_indices(p.a, p.b);
    if (m.count == 0) { continue; }
    if (const contact_manifold* previous = cache.find(pair_key(p.a, p.b)))
    {
        carry_impulses(m, *previous);
    }
    manifolds.push_back(m);
}

// the solve
solver.begin(world.bodies());
for (...) { solver.add(pair_a[i], pair_b[i], manifolds[i], material); }
stats = solver.solve(h, cfg, sleep);

for (...) { cache.store(keys[i], manifolds[i]); }
cache.end_frame();

world.integrate_positions(h);

The reserve is load-bearing and is the kind of thing that bites once: contact_solver::add keeps a pointer into that vector, and a reallocation half way through the loop hands the solver freed memory. The engine's contract says the manifold must outlive the step; that line is what honouring it looks like from the caller's side.

13. The budget

A settled yard: one floor, a hundred crates in twenty towers, a hundred manifolds and four hundred contact points, with sleeping switched off so that every stage is paid for. Minima over 200 repetitions with five discarded. 

     broadphase                17.208 us
     narrow phase              49.792 us
     solve (8 vel + 3 pos)    135.000 us
     total                    202.000 us of a 16667 us frame (1.21%)

The solve is two thirds of the contact work at this size, which is a reversal of 8.8's frame — there the narrow phase dominated, because there was no solve. Where the solve's time goes is then a straight-line fit, and the measurement is only meaningful if every row runs from the same state: 

     vel iters   pos iters    solve us
             0           0      14.958
             1           0      27.958
             2           0      40.833
             4           0      66.708
             8           0     118.292
            16           0     221.500
             8           1     123.916
             8           3     136.000
             8           6     153.958

     fixed cost (islands, sleep, prepare, warm start, write-back): 14.958 us
     one velocity iteration over 100 manifolds: 12.917 us
     one position iteration:                     5.903 us  (46% of a velocity one)

Worked: reading a cost model off a table. Zero iterations cost 14.958 µs, which is everything that is not the loop — the union–find, the counting sort, the sleep pass, a hundred prepare_contacts calls, the warm start and the write-back. From there each velocity iteration adds (118.292 − 14.958)/8 = 12.917 µs, and the 16-row predicts 14.958 + 16 × 12.917 = 221.6 against a measured 221.500. Three figures. A cost model that predicts a row it was not fitted to is a cost model with no surprises in it.

The first version of that table was nonsense and the reason is worth keeping. It changed the iteration count on a running scene, so the zero-iteration row dropped the whole yard through the floor and then measured the "cost" of a scene with no contacts left in it — which came out faster than eight. A timing sweep over a parameter that changes the simulation has to restore the simulation. And the snapshot has to include the manifold cache, because warm starting is precisely the feature that makes a step depend on more than its bodies.

At 8.8's scale — 2,001 bodies, 2,619 manifolds, 8,806 contact points — the whole contact pipeline is 4.35 ms of a 16.67 ms frame, 26% of the budget, of which the solve is 71%. With sleeping on and the pile settled it is 1.32 ms: 3.3× better, and the residue is the broadphase and narrow phase, which sleeping does not touch.

Which puts the iteration count in its place. A velocity iteration over a hundred manifolds is 12.9 µs, so 60 Hz would afford more than a thousand of them on that scene. Eight is not a performance decision at these sizes. What decides it is the next section.

14. What this solver leaves behind

Three limitations, and they are the same limitation.

How tall a stack this actually holds

     crates    minimum velocity iterations    drift at that count mm
          2                               2                      3.13
          3                               2                     20.17
          5                               4                    105.86
         10                              20                    402.15
         15                    more than 64                         -
         20                    more than 64                         -

The shipped default is eight, which holds five crates and does not hold ten. An under-swept tower does not sag — it leans, because the corrections arrive inconsistently and the inconsistency has a direction, and once it has leaned far enough it goes over. §13 measured the cost as linear in the iteration count and this table says the count needed grows faster than the height, so a tall stack is quadratic and a scene costs what its tallest pile costs.

Sleeping rescues the short ones, which is not something the solver can take credit for — it stops the drift by stopping the simulation, and only if the pile settles before it leans. A five-crate tower drifts 100 mm in twenty seconds awake and 16 mm with sleeping on (asleep at 1.07 s). A ten-crate tower is still leaning at 6.83 s when it falls asleep, and sleeping saves nothing.

Mass ratios

     ratio     light crate squashed mm    residual m/s
           1                     1.6860      3.5557e-09
          10                    51.3249      4.7792e-07
         100                   385.6325      3.1571e-09
        1000                   476.6946      1.7610e-10

A thousand-to-one ratio squashes the light crate 283 times further at the same iteration count. The impulse the heavy body needs is a thousand times larger and Gauss–Seidel gets there one sweep at a time. (The position correction hides most of this in practice, which is the third reason it is on by default — but hiding it is not fixing it.)

What all three have in common

A Gauss–Seidel sweep carries information across one contact. A chain of n contacts therefore needs of order n sweeps before its bottom knows what its top is doing, and a large mass ratio makes the error at each link larger. More iterations buy that linearly and nothing else does.

What fixes it is not more iterations. It is to stop treating each contact as an independent scalar and write the constraint as a Jacobian over the pair — a row that says which combination of both bodies' twelve velocity components the constraint cares about — at which point a contact is one kind of constraint among several, and solving a set of them jointly becomes a thing you can talk about. That is also, exactly, what a hinge needs. It is Lesson 8.11, and it is the reason this solver was written the way it was: solve_positions already takes no bodies, only a batch and two velocities, because the solver never needed to know what it was constraining.

And three things named rather than fixed

  • No block solver. Box2D solves a two-point manifold as a 2×2 LCP precisely because a flat contact solved sequentially is what makes boxes wobble. A 3D four-point manifold would want a 4×4, and nobody ships that. It would attack §14's first table directly.
  • No relaxation pass. Solvers that use Baumgarte often add a second velocity pass with the bias disabled, to remove the energy the bias added. This engine does not need one, because split impulse never adds any — which is one more argument for the default.
  • No speculative contacts. §6's arrival depth exists because collide_manifold reports nothing until the shapes actually overlap. A speculative contact is generated before they touch, with a positive separation, and the solver caps the approach speed at separation/h so the body stops exactly at the surface, adding no energy and needing no correction for the arrival case. It needs the narrow phase to report separated pairs, which is a change to 8.7's contract rather than to this file. manifold_config::keep_slop and broadphase_config::margin are the two knobs it would use, and both already exist.

17. Common pitfalls & debugging

Nothing ever sleeps, at any threshold.
Symptom: the sleep counter stays at zero on a scene that is visibly motionless, and lowering the threshold does not help — it makes it worse.
Cause: the sleep test is reading the velocity before the contact solve. Every dynamic body is then travelling at g·h = 0.1635 m/s at 60 Hz, which is above any sane threshold.
Fix: move the test to the end of the step. To confirm the diagnosis before you move anything, print the fastest dynamic body's speed at both points: §10 measures 0.1836 before and 0.0629 after on the same frame.
A crate hangs in the air after the thing under it is knocked away.
Symptom: the support is destroyed or knocked out and the body above it stays exactly where it was, indefinitely.
Cause: sleeping is being decided per body rather than per island. A sleeping body is an immovable body to everything else, and nothing woke it.
Fix: the island rule — an island is asleep only when every one of its bodies is, and any awake member wakes all of them. If the support was removed without a collision (a teleport, a delete, a velocity assignment), no graph can see it and you must call wake on the neighbours yourself.
A whole level stays awake because of one rolling marble.
Symptom: the island count is 1 on a scene with obviously separate piles, and the sleep counter never rises.
Cause: the union–find is joining bodies through the floor. A contact is an edge only when both ends can move.
Fix: skip the union when either body is fixed or kinematic. Print the island count on a scene of separated towers — it should equal the number of towers, and §9 measures 20 against 1 on the same contacts.
A stack slowly shears sideways and then falls over.
Symptom: each crate is offset a little further than the one below it, the offset grows, and the tower goes over after several seconds. Friction looks like it is not working.
Cause: two candidates, and they are distinguishable. Either the cached friction impulses are being applied in a different tangent basis from the one they were measured in (§4), or the tower simply needs more sweeps than it is getting (§14).
Fix: tell them apart before fixing either. Compute tangent_basis in a probe each frame and count how often t1 moves by more than thirty degrees; if that number is not tiny, store the basis. Then sweep the iteration count: if the tower stands at 20 and falls at 8, it is the chain length and no amount of anything else will fix it.
A body pops off the floor when the scene loads.
Symptom: objects placed slightly inside geometry jump into the air on the first frame.
Cause: Baumgarte, with a starting overlap deep enough for the push to beat the climb. §7 measures the threshold at 200 mm for a half-metre crate.
Fix: position_correction::split_impulse, which is the default; and keep max_correction_speed clamped, because a body spawned a metre inside a wall is otherwise told to leave at 12 m/s.
A stack breathes: everything jitters by a millimetre at 60 Hz.
Symptom: a settled pile visibly shimmers, and stats.points oscillates.
Cause: penetration_slop is zero or nearly so, and the contacts are competing for a separation none of them can have.
Fix: 2 mm was enough to stop it completely in §8 and 5 mm is the default. Note that a single resting contact does not need any slop at all, so if you see this on one body the cause is something else.
Everything is fine until the scene gets large, then the solver reads freed memory.
Symptom: a crash or garbage impulses that appear only above some contact count, and go away under a debugger.
Cause: contact_solver::add keeps a pointer to the manifold, and the caller is pushing manifolds into a std::vector that reallocates mid-loop.
Fix: reserve before the loop, or build the whole vector first and add afterwards. The engine's contract says the manifold must outlive the step; the caller has to mean it.
Bodies sink at exactly 2.7 mm per step.
Symptom: a resting body descends steadily, and the rate does not depend on the iteration count, the correction, or the mass.
Cause: the contact solve is running after the position integration.
Fix: put it between the halves. And recognise the number: g·h² is 2.725 mm at 60 Hz and 0.473 mm at 144, so the rate scaling with the square of the step is the confirmation.

18. Exercises

  1. Warm-up: predict the iteration count. §3 measured the contraction at 0.53 per sweep and a one-pass residual of 2.03 × 10⁻². How many sweeps to reach 10⁻⁶? Check against the table, then run ./build/demos/stack --scene 1 --iters N and see whether the tower's behaviour agrees with your arithmetic.

    Hint

    n = ln(r₁/r_target) / ln(1/0.53).

    Solution

    ln(2.03e-2 / 1e-6) = ln(20300) = 9.918, and ln(1/0.53) = 0.635, so n = 15.6 — sixteen sweeps. The table reads 1.68 × 10⁻⁶ at sixteen, which is as close as a prediction from a two-significant-figure rate gets. Note what the tower does not do: a single crate is already fine at eight, because its chain is one contact long.

  2. Break the island rule on purpose. In build_islands, delete the two lines that skip a union when either body is not dynamic. Run the yard scene and read the island count. Then predict, before running it, what the sleep counter will do when you press X to throw a crate at one tower.

    Solution sketch

    Twenty islands become one, and the thrown crate wakes all hundred crates rather than five. The demo's B key does this to the labels without touching the solve, so you can see the partition change while the physics stays put; doing it in the engine changes the physics too, and comparing the two is the point of the exercise.

  3. Find the arrival-depth distribution yourself. §6 claims the arrival depth is uniform on [0, v·h]. Instrument a single dropped crate to record depth / (v·h) on the frame the contact first appears, sweep the drop height finely, and histogram the result. Then explain why the smallest value in the table is 0.002 and not 0.

    Hint

    Read the speed after the velocity half, because that is the velocity the position line will travel at. Reading it before gives you a systematic error of g·h².

    Solution sketch

    The fraction is the leftover of a step, which is a real number in [0, 1) sampled by the drop height. It is never exactly 0 because that would require the body to cross the surface exactly at a step boundary, and with a finite sweep you never hit it — 0.002 is just the smallest of 200 samples. Sampling 20,000 drops gets closer to 0 and never reaches it.

  4. Engine challenge: the relaxation pass. Under position_correction::baumgarte, add a second velocity pass after the position stage with the bias set to zero — Box2D calls this "relax", and its job is to remove the energy the bias added. Measure §7's departure speed with and without it.

    Hint

    You need the accumulated impulses to survive into the relax pass but the bias not to, so the pass is solve_contacts with a solver_config whose correction is none. Do not reset the accumulators.

    Solution sketch

    It helps, substantially, and it never reaches zero — which is the honest reason this engine ships split impulse instead: the relax pass costs a whole extra velocity sweep (12.9 µs on §13's scene) to partially undo a problem that split impulse does not have, for the price of one position sweep at 5.9.

  5. Engine challenge: speculative contacts. §14 names them. Give manifold_config a speculative_margin; when GJK reports the shapes separated by less than it, build a one-point manifold from the witness points with a negative depth. Then, in prepare_contacts, cap the target so that a point with separation s > 0 may approach at no more than s/h. Measure the arrival depth again.

    Hint

    gjk_result already carries point_a, point_b, distance and direction — everything a one-point speculative manifold needs. The hard part is not the geometry, it is that contact_point::depth currently promises to be non-negative and half the engine reads it.

    Solution sketch

    Done properly the arrival depth goes to zero for any body slower than margin/h — 0.6 m/s at a 1 cm margin and 60 Hz — because the body is braked to land exactly on the surface rather than braked after crossing it. It adds no energy, which is why it is the more principled of the two answers to §6. It also costs a narrow phase that runs on pairs that are not touching, which is what the broadphase margin is for.

19. Recap & next

The engine can hold a stack up. That sentence is worth unpacking, because five separate pieces had to be true at once for it and each of them was measured rather than assumed.

Iteration turns four coupled constraints into a converging sequence, at a contraction of 0.53 per sweep — and a four-point manifold is already a system, so you do not need a stack to need a solver. Warm starting makes eight sweeps behave like eighty by handing the solve last frame's answer, which was already sitting in the manifold waiting for a reader; it is free, and it is an initial guess rather than a fact, which is why a deliberately wrong one costs more iterations than no guess at all. A position correction repairs the overlap that no velocity solve can see, and the default is the one that adds no energy, because the other one throws a crate spawned 200 mm deep ninety- nine millimetres into the air. Islands partition the world so that sleeping is a question with an answer, on one rule: a body that cannot move is not a bridge. Sleeping takes a settled yard's solve from 131 µs to 3 — and has to be tested after the solve, because before it every resting body in the scene is travelling at g·h.

Two things were fixed on the way that were not on the plan. body_world::step was split in two, because a contact solve has to happen between the halves and solving late costs g·h² per step forever; and three lessons of cached friction impulses turned out to be coordinates in a basis nobody had stored, which is a bug class worth more than the bug.

And the honest ending, in one table: eight sweeps hold five crates and do not hold ten. That is not a tuning problem and it is not a bug. A Gauss–Seidel sweep carries information across one contact, so a chain of n needs about n sweeps, and the cost is linear in the count — a tall stack is quadratic.
