Module 8 — Physics · Lesson 8.1

Integrators: Why One Explodes

Eighty-three lessons in, this engine can rasterize a triangle by hand, light it with a microfacet BRDF, cast a cascaded shadow into it, skin a character across it and put a sound beside it — and nothing in it moves on its own. Every position in every demo so far was authored: a sine of time, a key press, a keyframe, a slider. Module 8 is the subsystem that computes where things are from how they are moving, and before mass, before shapes, before contacts, there is one question in the way. You have a position, a velocity, and an acceleration. Advance all three by h seconds. Go. The answer that occurs to everybody first is unconditionally unstable — not inaccurate, not needs-a-smaller-step, but wrong at every step size there is, adding energy on every step forever. Swap two lines so the position moves at the velocity it has just been given and the same arithmetic preserves phase-space area exactly. Same loads, same multiplies, measured at 0.994 ns against 0.963 ns per body per step, which is a difference smaller than the run-to-run noise. This lesson is about why, and the why is one number: the determinant of a 2×2 matrix, which is 1 + h²ω² for one rule and exactly 1 for the other. On a 1 Hz spring at 60 Hz that surplus is 1.0966% per step, which is ×1.92 energy per second, ×1.13 × 10¹⁷ in one minute — a one-centimetre overlap becomes 3,357 km. Along the way: why the standard yardstick (order of accuracy) is blind to all of this and reads both Euler variants as respectable; what semi-implicit Euler actually conserves, which is not energy but a sheared cousin of it, held to 4.6 × 10⁻⁶ over 3,600 steps while the true energy wobbles by exactly hω; why that shadow quantity being an ellipse rather than a hyperbola is the stability limit hω < 2, verified by bisection at 0.3182939 against 0.3183099; why gravity alone forgives everything and your first falling-cube demo will look fine; and why v *= 0.99f makes four different games on four different monitors.

1. The problem: an engine where nothing moves on its own

Go back through the demos in this repository and ask of each one: what decided where that thing is? In hello_cube a sine of the clock. In gimbal three sliders. In rig a keyframe sampled and blended. In ecs_swarm a formula per entity. In audio a circle parameterised by time. In every single case the answer is the same: a person wrote down where it would be, and the program evaluated that function of time.

That is a perfectly good way to make things move, and it is what animation is. It is also the exact opposite of what physics is. A physics engine does not know where anything will be. It knows where everything is now, how fast each thing is going, and what forces are acting — and from those three it works out where everything is a moment later, and then does it again. Nobody authors the arc of a thrown box. The arc is what falls out.

So the first question of Module 8 is not about mass, or shapes, or contacts, or friction. It is the question that has to be answered before any of those mean anything:

You know x and v, and a force tells you how fast v is changing. Advance both by h seconds.

Half of that question has been answered since Module 1. Lesson 1.4 built engine::fixed_step, derived the accumulator, and established why the frame rate must not decide how far the simulation advances — a 144 Hz machine and a 30 Hz machine must agree about where the ball lands, so the simulation gets its own clock and a constant h. That class has been sitting in core/ for eight modules doing exactly one job, and this lesson does not touch it. It has always handed out the h; this lesson is about what runs inside it.

And here is the shape of the trouble, stated up front because it is not what you would guess. The two lines that first occur to you — 

position += velocity * h;      // using the velocity you HAD
velocity += acceleration * h;

— are explicit Euler, and on a spring they are not approximately right. They add energy on every step. Not sometimes, not when h is too big, not when the spring is too stiff: always, at every step size, by a factor you can write down in closed form. A simulation built on those two lines does not drift; it diverges, and it diverges exponentially. The fix is to swap them so the position moves at the velocity it has just been given, and the swap is free.

You might reasonably ask why, if this is so catastrophic, anybody ever ships it — and the honest answer is §7. Under gravity alone, all three rules in this lesson behave, and their error grows linearly at about eight centimetres per second of falling. Your first cube-falling demo will look completely correct. The disaster is reserved for forces that depend on where the body is, and by the end of this module essentially every force does: a contact that pushes a box out of the floor is a stiff spring, a joint that holds an elbow together is a stiff spring, a suspension arm and a soft-body edge and a spring-damper camera are all springs. a = −ω²x is not a toy problem chosen because it is easy to analyse. It is the linearisation of every restoring force in Module 8, and an integrator that cannot survive it cannot carry anything we build on top of it.

2. Building intuition: a step is a bet that the slope holds

Forget springs for a moment and think about a car. The speedometer says 60 km/h. Where are you in one minute?

One kilometre further on — obviously. And that answer is exactly right if and only if the needle does not move for the whole minute. If you are braking, you travel less than a kilometre; if you are accelerating, more. The error is not mysterious and it is not random: it is entirely accounted for by how much the speed changed during the minute.

That is an Euler step, complete. You take the rate you have, assume it holds for the duration of the step, and multiply. The whole of the method is the word assume, and the whole of the error is that the assumption is false: the rate you used is the rate at the start of the step, and by the end it is something else.

Figure 1 is that picture drawn properly. The grey curve is where the quantity actually goes; the red line is the tangent at the moment you sampled it, extended by h; the amber gap is what you got wrong. Notice what controls the gap. It is not the value, and it is not the slope — a straight line has a slope and no gap at all. It is the curvature: how fast the slope itself is changing. A step across a straight stretch is free; a step across a bend costs you the bend.

Now the part that is easy to miss. In a physics step there are two of these bets, not one, and they are usually described as though there were only one:

  • Position from velocity. You assume the velocity holds across the step. Its curvature is the acceleration.
  • Velocity from acceleration. You assume the acceleration holds across the step. Its curvature is how fast the force is changing, which for a spring is −ω²·v — proportional to how fast you are already going.

Two bets, and here is the question the rest of this lesson turns on: do you have to make them independently? The obvious code makes them independent — it computes both updates from the same starting state, as though the two lines were happening simultaneously. But they are not simultaneous; they are two lines in a program, and the second one runs after the first. Whichever you do second, you could do it using the answer from the first. That is free information, and explicit Euler throws it away.

3. The theory: Taylor, and what "first order" buys you

Let us make the picture into arithmetic. Write the true position a step later as a function of everything you know now. If the motion is smooth — and it is, between collisions — the value at t + h is the value at t plus the slope times h, plus a correction for the fact that the slope was changing, plus a correction for the fact that that was changing, and so on forever:

\[ x(t+h) \;=\; x(t) \;+\; h\,v(t) \;+\; \frac{h^{2}}{2}\,a(t) \;+\; \frac{h^{3}}{6}\,\dot a(t) \;+\; \cdots \]

In plain text: x(t+h) = x + h·v + (h²/2)·a + (h³/6)·ȧ + … — the position, plus the velocity's contribution over the step, plus the acceleration's, plus the jerk's, forever.

That is the Taylor series, and you do not have to take it on faith. Each term is the previous one's rate of change, given the same treatment: velocity is how fast position changes, so over h seconds it contributes h·v. Acceleration is how fast velocity changes, so the velocity was not really v for the whole step — it averaged v + (h/2)·a, which contributes the extra (h²/2)·a. The pattern continues, each term picking up another factor of h and another division.

Explicit Euler is that series truncated after two terms. That is the entire definition. It keeps x + h·v and throws away everything from (h²/2)·a onward, which — since h is small and the terms shrink — is dominated by the one you dropped first. So the error in a single step is about (h²/2)·a. This is called the local error, and it is O(h²).

But you do not take one step. To simulate T seconds you take T/h of them, and if each costs you O(h²) and there are T/h of them, the bill is T·O(h). One factor of h is paid back by having to take more steps. That is the global error, it is O(h), and "first order" means exactly this and nothing more: halve the step and the error after a fixed amount of simulated time roughly halves.

3.1 One step, by hand

Numbers, because a formula you have not pushed a number through is a formula you do not own. Take the spring this whole lesson uses: a 1 Hz oscillator, so ω = 2π = 6.283185 rad/s, and a = −ω²x = −39.478418·x. Start it pulled out to x = 1 m and released, so v = 0. Step size h = 1/60 s.

The exact answer is available in closed form, which is precisely why this problem is the test case: x(t) = cos(ωt) and v(t) = −ω·sin(ωt). At t = h = 0.0166667, ωt = 0.1047198, and so

\[ x_{\text{exact}} = \cos(0.1047198) = 0.9945219, \qquad v_{\text{exact}} = -6.283185\sin(0.1047198) = -0.6567717 \]

Now the three rules, each pushed through by hand. The acceleration at the start is a₀ = −39.478418 × 1 = −39.478418 m/s², and h·a₀ = −0.6579736.

First, the two Euler rules end up with exactly the same velocity, −0.6579736, because they run the identical velocity line — the only thing they disagree about is which velocity the position line was told to move at. Second, their position errors are equal and opposite, both of size 0.005488, and that number is ½h²ω²x = 0.5 × (1/3600) × 39.478418 = 0.0054831 — the Taylor term both of them dropped, appearing once with each sign. Explicit Euler did not move at all when it should have moved inward; semi-implicit Euler moved inward too far by the same amount.

Third, velocity Verlet's position error is 5.0 × 10⁻⁶, a thousand times smaller — because the term it adds, ½·a·h², is the term the other two dropped. What is left over is the next one down, the h⁴ term, and cos's fourth-order coefficient gives ω⁴h⁴/24 = 5.01 × 10⁻⁶. The arithmetic closes.

So far this looks like an ordinary accuracy story: three methods, three error sizes, pick the one you can afford. That story is wrong, and the next section is the measurement that shows it is wrong.

4. The yardstick that cannot see the bug

Order of accuracy is the number every numerical-methods course teaches first, and it is easy to measure: run to a fixed simulated time, halve the step, and see what the error does. Halving means first order. Quartering means second. Section A of verify_81.cpp does it on our spring, running exactly one full period — which makes the reference free of approximation, because after one period the exact answer is the state we started in.

Explicit Euler halves when the step halves, so it is first order, as advertised. Velocity Verlet quarters, so it is second order, as advertised. And semi-implicit Euler also quarters, which is not what any textbook will tell you, because semi-implicit Euler is a first-order method.

That is worth stopping on rather than waving away, and the explanation is the whole lesson in advance. Semi-implicit Euler's error on this problem has two parts: an amplitude error (is the orbit the right size?) and a phase error (has it gone round at the right rate?). Section 5 will show that the amplitude error is exactly zero — not small, zero — which leaves only the phase error, and the phase error turns out to be O(h²). So what you are reading in that column is not a promotion. It is a fact about the harmonic oscillator, and it is a fact caused by the very property this lesson is about. Measure the same method on a problem with no such structure and it is first order again.

The last row is the instrument, not the result. At h = 1/1920 one period is 1,920 float additions on numbers of size 1, and float cannot resolve below about 1e-6 after that much accumulation. Verlet's 1.061e-06 is not four times better than the row above it; it is noise. The harness says so rather than letting the column be read, and it is the same discipline as Lesson 7.8's −160 dB harmonics: a number at the noise floor is a fact about the meter.

4.1 …and now the other question

Look again at what A.1 asked. It held the simulated time fixed at one period and shrank the step. That is the mathematician's question — behaviour as h → 0 — and it is the right question if you are choosing a step size for a one-off calculation.

It is not the question a game asks. A game fixes h at 1/60 and never changes it, then runs for an hour. Section A.3 asks that question instead: same step, growing time.

Figure 2 puts the two questions side by side, and the point is the one written under the right-hand panel: nothing measured on the left can see what is happening on the right. Order of accuracy is a statement about the limit as the step goes to zero at a fixed time. Stability is a statement about the limit as time goes to infinity at a fixed step. They are different questions, both of them legitimate, and the second one is the one that decides whether your game works after the player has been in the level for five minutes.

So we need a different instrument. And the useful thing about the harmonic oscillator is that it hands us one.

5. Phase space, and the one number

Stop plotting position against time. Plot position against velocity instead — every state of the system is one point in that plane, and the simulation is a point moving around it. This is called phase space, and it is the single most useful change of viewpoint in this lesson.

Why it helps: for a spring, the exact trajectory in phase space is a circle. With x = A cos(ωt) and v = −Aω sin(ωt), plotting x horizontally and v/ω vertically gives x² + (v/ω)² = A² — a circle of radius A, traversed once per period, forever. There is nothing to interpret. A correct integrator produces points on that circle. An incorrect one produces points that are not on it, and you can see which way they are wrong at a glance.

Figure 3 takes one point on the circle and applies one step of each rule, with a deliberately coarse hω = 0.7 so the step is a visible arc rather than a dot. Look first at the grey dotted horizontal line: both endpoints are at the same height. That is not a coincidence and it is not an artefact of the drawing — both rules ran the identical velocity update, v ← v − hω²x, from the identical starting state. The only difference between them is which x the position line was told to move at, and it moves them apart horizontally and in no other way.

The red dot is outside the circle. Run the step again from there and it will be further outside, and again, and again. The green dot is not on the circle either — and that is the honest surprise of this figure, worth stating plainly before §6 makes it the main event: semi-implicit Euler does not put you back on the exact orbit. It puts you on the dashed green ellipse, which is a slightly sheared, slightly rotated cousin of the circle — and it keeps you there forever.

5.1 One step is a matrix

Here is the move that turns all of this from pictures into a number. For a linear force — a = −ω²x, which is the whole point of choosing this test — every integrator step is a linear transformation of the state. Write the state as a column of two numbers and the step is a matrix acting on it, exactly as in Lesson 2.5.

Explicit Euler, written out with the two lines in the order the code has them:

\[ x_{n+1} = x_n + h\,v_n, \qquad v_{n+1} = v_n - h\omega^{2}x_n \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \begin{bmatrix} x_{n+1} \\ v_{n+1} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & h \\ -h\omega^{2} & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x_n \\ v_n \end{bmatrix} \]

In plain text: the new state is [[1, h], [−hω², 1]] times the old one. The top row says "new position is old position plus h times old velocity"; the bottom row says "new velocity is old velocity minus h·ω² times old position".

Semi-implicit Euler differs in one place. Its position line uses vn+1, so substitute:

\[ x_{n+1} = x_n + h\,v_{n+1} = x_n + h\left(v_n - h\omega^{2}x_n\right) = \left(1 - h^{2}\omega^{2}\right)x_n + h\,v_n \]
\[ \begin{bmatrix} x_{n+1} \\ v_{n+1} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 - h^{2}\omega^{2} & h \\ -h\omega^{2} & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x_n \\ v_n \end{bmatrix} \]

In plain text: the same matrix except that the top-left entry is 1 − h²ω² instead of 1. That single entry is the whole difference between the two methods, and it appeared because the position line was allowed to see the velocity line's answer.

5.2 The determinant, and what it does to energy

A 2×2 matrix's determinant is the factor by which it scales area. Lesson 2.5 introduced a matrix as "where the basis vectors land"; take the unit square those basis vectors span, push it through the matrix, and the parallelogram that comes out has area equal to the determinant. The inset in figure 3 is literally that experiment, run on a small square of states.

Compute both, and it takes one line each:

\[ \det\begin{bmatrix} 1 & h \\ -h\omega^{2} & 1 \end{bmatrix} = 1\cdot 1 - h\cdot(-h\omega^{2}) = 1 + h^{2}\omega^{2} \]
\[ \det\begin{bmatrix} 1 - h^{2}\omega^{2} & h \\ -h\omega^{2} & 1 \end{bmatrix} = (1 - h^{2}\omega^{2}) - h\cdot(-h\omega^{2}) = 1 - h^{2}\omega^{2} + h^{2}\omega^{2} = 1 \]

In plain text: explicit Euler's determinant is 1 + h²ω², which is greater than 1 for any non-zero h and any non-zero ω. Semi-implicit Euler's is exactly 1 — the two h²ω² terms cancel, algebraically, with no approximation anywhere.

That cancellation is the whole lesson, so stare at it for a second. The −h²ω² in the top-left came from the position line using the new velocity. The +h²ω² came from the off-diagonal product. They are the same quantity because they have the same origin: the velocity update's effect, counted once in each place. Explicit Euler only gets one of them and so has a surplus.

Figure 4 lines all of this up, including a third method we are not implementing.

5.3 Backward Euler, the mirror image

The obvious next idea, once you know explicit Euler grows, is to use the new state on both lines: vn+1 = vn − hω²xn+1 and xn+1 = xn + h·vn+1. That is backward (implicit) Euler. It is an implicit equation — the unknown appears on both sides — and for this linear force it can be solved in closed form. Substituting and collecting gives

\[ \begin{bmatrix} x_{n+1} \\ v_{n+1} \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{1 + h^{2}\omega^{2}} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & h \\ -h\omega^{2} & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x_n \\ v_n \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \det = \frac{1 + h^{2}\omega^{2}}{\left(1 + h^{2}\omega^{2}\right)^{2}} = \frac{1}{1 + h^{2}\omega^{2}} \]

In plain text: backward Euler's matrix is explicit Euler's matrix divided by (1 + h²ω²), and its determinant is the exact reciprocal of explicit Euler's. Where one inflates, the other deflates, by precisely the same factor.

This is why backward Euler is described as unconditionally stable and why almost nothing in real time uses it. It cannot blow up, because it is losing energy on every step — a released pendulum grinds to a halt, a stack of boxes slowly sinks into the floor, and a ragdoll goes limp. And you pay for that damping: for anything other than a linear force there is no closed form, so each step becomes a root find. Costly and lossy. Where implicit methods do appear in games — cloth, hair — it is because that damping is exactly what is wanted. integrate.hpp therefore has no implicit_euler, and its enum says why in a comment rather than leaving the omission to look like an oversight.

The parallelogram is half a metre across, not infinitesimal, and that is deliberate. The update is a matrix — it is exactly linear — so the parallelogram's size cancels out of the ratio algebraically, and any size gives the same answer. The first version of this section used ε = 1e-3 on the reasonable-sounding grounds that a derivative wants a small step, and threw away three digits of precision to cancellation between nearly equal floats: it reported the control at 0.999974 instead of 1.000000. When the thing you are probing is genuinely linear, a small probe buys nothing and costs precision.

Now connect the determinant to something you care about. The area enclosed by a phase-space orbit of a spring is π·A·(Aω) = πωA², and the energy per unit mass is E = ½ω²A² — so the enclosed area is 2πE/ω, which is to say area is energy, up to a constant that never changes. A step that multiplies area by 1 + h²ω² multiplies energy by 1 + h²ω². After N steps it has multiplied energy by (1 + h²ω²)ᴺ, and that is not an estimate; it is what the matrix does.

At h = 1/60 and ω = 2π, h²ω² = 0.010966229. Over one second — 60 steps — that is 1.010966229⁶⁰ = 1.924. Over one minute, 3,600 steps:

Translated out of energy and into metres: a spring released from 1 m is at 3.36 × 10⁸ m after one minute. If that spring were the one-centimetre overlap between a box and the floor — which is exactly what a penalty-force contact is — the box would be 3,357 km away. And the first ten seconds of that run look completely normal, which is the trap: at t = 1 the energy is 1.92× and at t = 5 it is 26×, numbers a playtester describes as "the physics feels a bit bouncy".

5.5 And a smaller step does not fix it

The instinct at this point is to reach for h. It does not work, and the reason is visible in B.2: the determinant is 1 + h²ω², which approaches 1 as h shrinks but never reaches it. Every step still adds energy; you have only made the steps smaller and bought more of them.

Quantify it. Growth over a fixed simulated time T is (1 + h²ω²)T/h, and for small h the exponent times the log is about (T/h)·h²ω² = T·h·ω². The growth per second is proportional to h — linear, not exponential — so halving the step halves the exponent, and the time to double the energy is proportional to 1/h:

        60   0.0166667        1.067        1.059
       120   0.0083333        2.117        2.110
       240   0.0041667        4.217        4.215
      1000   0.0010000       17.559       17.558
      4000   0.0002500       70.230       70.231

     h = 4.201e-07 s, i.e. 2380529 steps per second.

Ten times the step rate buys ten times the delay. And read backwards — which is how an engineer would actually use it — holding the error to one per cent over a ten-minute level needs 2.4 million steps per second. For one spring, at 1 Hz, which is the gentlest restoring force anything in a game would ever have. The answer is not a smaller step. The answer is a different rule, and it costs nothing.

Why the 30 Hz row misses by 5% and the others do not. Nothing is wrong with the prediction; the measurement is quantised. The energy does not climb smoothly — it also wobbles once per oscillation, by hω in relative terms, which is 21% at 30 Hz. So the moment the run crosses 2× is caught at whatever phase of that wobble it happens to be in. By 120 Hz the wobble is 5% and the rows agree to three digits. The next section is where that wobble comes from, and it turns out to be the most interesting quantity in the lesson.

6. What semi-implicit Euler actually conserves

Look back at the C.1 table and read the semi-implicit column honestly. It is not 1.000000. It drifts: 0.99970, 0.99849, 0.99699, 0.99104, 0.98243. Over a minute the energy has fallen by 1.76%. If the determinant is exactly 1, why is the energy not exactly constant?

Because area being preserved is not the same as energy being preserved. The determinant says the map does not scale area. It says nothing about the map not shearing it — and a shear preserves area while moving every individual point. What semi-implicit Euler does to phase space is exactly that: it preserves area, and it shears.

So there is a question with a real answer: if not energy, what is constant? And the answer is a slightly sheared cousin of energy:

\[ \tilde E(x, v) \;=\; \tfrac{1}{2}\left(v^{2} + \omega^{2}x^{2} - h\omega^{2}\,x v\right) \]

In plain text: the usual energy ½(v² + ω²x²), minus a cross term ½·h·ω²·x·v that vanishes as the step size goes to zero. This is what shadow_energy() computes.

6.1 Checking it by hand

Use the state from §3.1. We started at x = 1, v = 0, and one semi-implicit step took us to x = 0.9890338, v = −0.6579736, with ω² = 39.478418 and h = 1/60.

At the start, v = 0 kills two of the three terms:

\[ 2\tilde E_0 = 0 + 39.478418\times 1 - 0 = 39.478418 \]

After the step, all three are alive. Taking them one at a time: v² = 0.6579736² = 0.4329293; ω²x² = 39.478418 × 0.9890338² = 39.478418 × 0.9781878 = 38.617301; and the cross term hω²xv = 0.6579736 × 0.9890338 × (−0.6579736) = −0.4281800, which is subtracted, so it comes in positive:

\[ 2\tilde E_1 = 0.4329293 + 38.617301 + 0.4281800 = 39.478410 \]

Against 39.478418. They agree to seven significant figures, and the residual is the rounding in the seven-digit arithmetic above rather than anything the method did. Meanwhile the true energy went from 19.739209 to 19.525115 — down 1.08% in a single step. One quantity moved and the other did not, and only one of them is the one people normally watch.

The harness does the same thing over 3,600 steps rather than one:

     true energy wobbles; the SHADOW energy does not.

   quantity            min          max      spread
   true energy     18.7570286  20.8298473   1.047e-01
   shadow energy   19.7391300  19.7392216   4.638e-06

     predicted spread of the true energy is exactly
     h*w = 0.104720. Measured 0.104723.

Four parts in a million over a simulated minute, in float. And the true energy's wobble is not merely bounded — it is hω peak-to-peak, exactly, predicted 0.104720 and measured 0.104723. That prediction is worth deriving, because it is what turns "it wobbles a bit" into a number you can budget against.

Write the shadow quantity in the coordinates figure 3 plots, p = ωx and q = v, and let ε = hω: 2Ẽ = q² + p² − ε·p·q. That is a quadratic form whose matrix is [[1, −ε/2], [−ε/2, 1]], with eigenvalues 1 − ε/2 and 1 + ε/2 along the two diagonals. On a level set 2Ẽ = C, the true energy E = ½(p² + q²) therefore ranges between C/(2(1 + ε/2)) and C/(2(1 − ε/2)), and the peak-to-peak spread divided by the midpoint is

\[ \frac{\dfrac{1}{1-\varepsilon/2} - \dfrac{1}{1+\varepsilon/2}} {\dfrac{1}{2}\left(\dfrac{1}{1-\varepsilon/2} + \dfrac{1}{1+\varepsilon/2}\right)} \;=\; \frac{\varepsilon/(1-\varepsilon^{2}/4)}{1/(1-\varepsilon^{2}/4)} \;=\; \varepsilon \]

In plain text: the relative peak-to-peak energy wobble is exactly hω. At 60 Hz on a 1 Hz spring that is 10.47%; at 60 Hz on a 5 Hz spring it is 52%.

Where the shadow quantity comes from, honestly. Above, it is checked rather than derived — you can verify by direct substitution that the update matrix leaves the form invariant (multiply it out; the v² coefficient stays 1, the x² coefficient stays ω², and the cross term stays −hω²), which is a proof, but it does not tell you how anybody found it. The general answer is backward error analysis: a symplectic integrator applied to one Hamiltonian system is, to exponentially high accuracy, the exact solution of a nearby one, and the shadow energy is that nearby system's energy. That is the ninety percent picture; the missing ten percent is a book, and it is Hairer, Lubich and Wanner's, cited at the end. What matters here is the consequence, which is the next paragraph.

6.2 Stability and that ellipse are the same statement

The trajectory sits on a level set of Ẽ. If that level set is a closed curve, the trajectory is trapped inside it and can never run away — no matter how long you run, no matter how many steps. If it is not a closed curve, there is nothing holding anything.

So: when is q² + p² − ε·p·q = C an ellipse? Exactly when the quadratic form is positive-definite, and for a 2×2 symmetric matrix that is one determinant:

\[ \det\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -\varepsilon/2 \\ -\varepsilon/2 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = 1 - \frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4} > 0 \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \varepsilon < 2 \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad h\omega < 2 \]

In plain text: the conserved quantity is an ellipse — and therefore traps the trajectory — if and only if h·ω < 2. Past that it is a hyperbola, its level sets run off to infinity, and so does the simulation.

Figure 6 draws both cases. That hω < 2 is the stability limit for semi-implicit Euler, and this is the second time this lesson has got a stability result out of a determinant: the first said the method does not inflate area, and this one says the thing whose area is preserved is a bounded shape. Both are one line of algebra. This is what it means to have the right instrument.

max_stable_step() returns 2/ω for both symplectic rules and zero for explicit Euler, and section F finds both numbers by bisection rather than trusting the header:

   verlet      0.3183099    0.3183099   1.00000

That control deserves a note, because it is the shape of a whole category of misleading test. Run the same bisection on explicit Euler and it returns an answer — 3.4 ms, which looks like a stability limit and is not one. What it actually found is the largest step at which the energy had not yet doubled within the 20,000 steps the test happened to run. Run 200,000 steps and the "limit" moves. The number is a property of the test's patience. A search that always returns something will return something even when the answer is "there is none".

hω < 2 is a hard wall, not a target. Section F.2 measures the worst energy excursion over ten simulated seconds as the step size creeps up on the wall, and compares it against 1/(1 − hω/2), which is where the shadow ellipse of §6.1 puts its far end:

That is not very stiff. This is the moment to say where Module 8 is going, because the conclusion is structural rather than numerical. Contacts in this engine will not be springs. Modelling a contact as a stiff penalty spring is the approach that this section has just priced, and the price is unpayable. Lesson 8.9 will treat a contact as an impulse — a velocity change applied instantly, with no stiffness to be unstable about — and 8.10 will iterate those impulses until a whole stack is consistent. The stability limit derived here is the reason that architecture exists, and it is worth having the number in hand before meeting the machinery.

7. Gravity, where the choice very nearly does not matter

Everything so far has been about a force that depends on position. Now do the opposite case — constant acceleration — and something surprising happens: the catastrophe disappears entirely.

With a constant, all three rules compute the same velocity, because all three velocity lines are v += a·h and there is no state for them to disagree about. So the only difference is position, and that difference can be summed exactly. Explicit Euler advances position with the velocity at the start of each step:

\[ x_n = x_0 + h\sum_{k=0}^{n-1} v_k = x_0 + h\left(n v_0 + a h\frac{n(n-1)}{2}\right) = x_0 + t v_0 + \tfrac{1}{2}a h^{2} n(n-1) \]

while the exact answer is x₀ + t·v₀ + ½a·t² with t = nh, that is x₀ + t·v₀ + ½a·h²n². Subtract:

\[ \text{error} = \tfrac{1}{2}a h^{2}\left(n(n-1) - n^{2}\right) = -\tfrac{1}{2}a h^{2} n = -\tfrac{1}{2}\,a\,h\,t \]

In plain text: explicit Euler's position error under constant acceleration is −½·a·h·t. Semi-implicit Euler sums the velocities at the end of each step instead, which shifts the sum by one term and gives +½·a·h·t — the same size, the other sign. Velocity Verlet keeps the ½a·h² term explicitly, which is exactly what is missing, so it is exact.

Numbers. Dropping from rest under g = 9.81 at h = 1/60, after one second the predicted error is ½ × 9.81 × (1/60) × 1 = 0.081750 m: 

E.1  after 1 second. Exact: 4.905000 m, speed 9.810000.

   rule          fallen (m)     error (m)    speed
   explicit      4.823249    -0.081751    9.81000
   semi-impl     4.986749    +0.081749    9.81000
   verlet        4.904999    -0.000001    9.81000

Eight centimetres, in either direction, on a 4.9 m drop — 1.7%. And figure 8's whole point is the shape of those lines: they are straight. The error grows linearly in time, not exponentially. After eight seconds it is 65 cm — and the fall is now 314 m, so the relative error has gone from 1.7% to 0.21%. That is the opposite of the spring: the error grows like t while the distance grows like t², so the longer you watch, the better it looks. It never compounds, because there is no feedback — nothing about being in the wrong place changes the force.

That single fact explains why this bug is in so much shipped code. Your first falling-cube demo is fine. Your projectile arc is fine, your jump is fine, your particle system is fine. Gravity is constant and gravity forgives. What does not forgive is the moment a force starts asking where are you? — and every contact, every joint, every constraint, every spring in the next twelve lessons asks exactly that.

A control that had to be exact, and the floor underneath it. Section E.4 checks that velocity Verlet is exact rather than merely close, because ½a·h² is algebraically the whole of the missing term. After eight seconds it reports −313.920807 m against an exact −313.920000 m: a residual of 8 × 10⁻⁴ m, which is 26 ulp of a float that size. The arithmetic is exact; the floor underneath it is still float's own. The harness prints the ulp count so that the qualifier cannot be skipped.

8. Velocity-dependent forces are a different problem

There is a restriction hiding in the signature of integrate, and it needs saying out loud because the failure it prevents is common and invisible: 

template <class Accel>
void integrate(motion& m, Accel&& accel, float h, integrator rule);
//                        ^^^^^ callable as vec3 accel(vec3 position)

Position only. The area-preserving property that makes semi-implicit Euler worth having is a statement about forces derived from a potential — forces that depend on where you are, not on how fast you are going. Air drag, a = −k·v, is not one of those. Feed it through the semi-implicit path and look at what happens: the velocity line runs first, computing v ← v + h·a(v) using the old v, which is plain explicit Euler. The clever reordering has nothing to reorder, because the position line was never involved.

And plain explicit Euler on drag has a stability limit of its own — |1 − hk| < 1, so h < 2/k — with a failure mode that is genuinely funny the first time you see it: 

H.1  a = -k v for one step of h = 1/60.

   k       h*k      1 - h*k      exp(-h*k)   verdict
       6   0.1000    +0.90000     0.904837   ok
      30   0.5000    +0.50000     0.606531   ok
      60   1.0000    +0.00000     0.367879   STOPS DEAD
      90   1.5000    -0.50000     0.223130   REVERSES
     150   2.5000    -1.50000     0.082085   DIVERGES

At hk = 1 the step removes the entire velocity in one go — not drag, a handbrake. At hk = 1.5 it removes 150% of it, so the body reverses direction on every step. Past hk = 2 it reverses and grows: a drag force that accelerates things.

None of this is necessary, because dv/dt = −k·v has a closed-form solution and always has: v(t) = v₀·e^(−kt). So apply_drag does not step anything. It evaluates the answer. 

void apply_drag(motion& m, float k, float h)
{
    m.velocity *= std::exp(-k * h);
}

8.1 v *= 0.99f, and the three games it makes

Which brings us to the most common frame-rate dependency in all of gameplay code, and the reason damping_factor exists. Somewhere in almost every codebase there is a line like vel *= 0.99f; in a per-step update, and it looks like nothing. It is explicit Euler on drag, written in a form where the k is invisible — comparing against v *= (1 − hk) gives k = (1 − 0.99)/h, which is a drag coefficient that changes when you change the step rate. 

G.1  velocity remaining after one simulated second.

   rate      per-step 0.99    pow(r, h)     target
      30 Hz      0.739700     0.547156    0.547157
      60 Hz      0.547157     0.547157    0.547157
     120 Hz      0.299381     0.547156    0.547157
     144 Hz      0.235217     0.547157    0.547157

Same constant, same code, four different games. A player on a 144 Hz machine keeps 24% of their velocity per second where a player on a 30 Hz machine keeps 74% — the car handles differently, the jump arc is different, and nothing in the source looks suspicious.

The fix is to say what you actually mean. You do not mean "multiply by 0.99 each step"; you mean "retain this fraction of the velocity per second", and the per-step factor that achieves it is pow(retained, h). That is damping_factor, four lines including the two degenerate cases, and the harness's control is the part that matters: 

G.2  CONTROL: at exactly 60 Hz the two paths must be
     the same computation, because that is the rate
     the target was derived at.
   0.99 vs pow(target, 1/60) = 0.990000000 vs 0.990000010
   difference: 9.537e-09

Nine nanoseconds' worth of float. That control is doing real work: without it, "the fix" could equally well be changing the tuned feel of the game rather than preserving it at every rate, and a designer who spent a week on that 0.99 would be entitled to an opinion about which one you did.

9. The implementation

engine::phys is the fifth new directory since the Module 5 refactor, and the second (after audio/) whose contents never touch a pixel. It starts smaller than any subsystem in this engine has: one header and one source file.

9.1 motion, and what is deliberately not in it

struct motion
{
    vec3 position{};
    vec3 velocity{};
};

That is the entire state. No mass, no orientation, no handle, no shape, no id. It is worth being explicit about why, because the temptation at the start of a physics module is to write the rigid_body you know is coming and integrate a field of it.

An integrator does not need any of that. It needs a quantity and its rate of change. Keeping the type down to two vectors is what let §5 be an argument about a 2×2 matrix instead of an argument about an engine — and it is also what makes the type reusable for things that are not bodies at all: a spring-damper camera, a smoothed UI value, a particle. Lesson 8.2 adds mass and turns F = ma into a rigid_body that has a motion; 8.3 adds orientation and an inertia tensor beside it.

The units are the ones fixed on the Conventions page in Module 2: metres and seconds. That has been a statement about rendering for six modules and becomes a statement about physics here — 8.2 is where "1 unit = 1 metre" stops being pedantry, because 9.81 is a number in those units and in no others.

9.2 Three rules, as close together as they will go

The stepping function is a template over the acceleration callable, and its three cases are written to sit directly under one another so the difference is impossible to miss: 

case integrator::explicit_euler:
{
    const vec3 a = accel(m.position);
    m.position += m.velocity * h;
    m.velocity += a * h;
    break;
}

case integrator::semi_implicit_euler:
{
    const vec3 a = accel(m.position);
    m.velocity += a * h;
    m.position += m.velocity * h;
    break;
}

Two lines, swapped. The course index calls this "one character's difference", and that is precise if you read it as a formula rather than as code — the update is xn+1 = xn + h·vn against xn+1 = xn + h·vn+1, and the difference is the subscript. In C++ it is a line order, which is if anything worse: a line order is the kind of thing an "obvious" refactor rearranges, and there is no compiler diagnostic anywhere that will tell you what you just did.

Velocity Verlet is the third case, and it does something the others do not — it evaluates the acceleration twice: 

case integrator::velocity_verlet:
{
    const vec3 a0 = accel(m.position);
    m.position += m.velocity * h + a0 * (0.5f * h * h);
    const vec3 a1 = accel(m.position);
    m.velocity += (a0 + a1) * (0.5f * h);
    break;
}

Move with the acceleration you have, then correct the velocity with the average of the accelerations at the two ends of the step. That average is a trapezium rule, which is why the method is second order, and the ½·a·h² in the position line is the Taylor term §3 identified as the one everyone else drops.

A production Verlet caches a1 and hands it to the next step as its a0, halving the force work. This one does not, and the reason is stated in the header rather than hidden: caching costs a state field that the other two rules have no use for, which would push a vec3 into motion for the benefit of one enumerator. That is the right trade when the force evaluation is a broadphase query, and the wrong one for a type whose whole job in this lesson is to be legible. §10 measures what the honesty costs, and it is 14%.

9.3 Why it is a template

Accel&& rather than std::function<vec3(vec3)>, and the justification is a measurement rather than a reflex. A std::function is an indirect call that the optimiser cannot see through, once per body per step: 

I.1  explicit           0.994 ns/body/step
     semi-impl          0.963
     verlet             1.136

I.3  std::function      1.591 ns/body/step

0.63 ns per body per step, which is 65% on top of the step itself — a function call whose body is one multiply, costing more than the arithmetic it wraps. In absolute terms it is small (ten thousand bodies buys it for 6 µs a frame), and the reason to care is that it scales with the same thing everything else in Module 8 scales with. And there is a second, sharper reading of the same table further down: 

I.4  integrate(m, g, ...)     1.222 ns/body/step
   SLOWER than the template in I.1, and that is the
   whole argument for the template: this one is a
   real call into libengine.a and cannot inline
   across the archive.

The non-template overload — the constant-acceleration one that lives in the .cpp and handles gravity — is the slowest of the lot, because it is a genuine call into libengine.a that cannot be inlined across the static archive. The simpler-looking path is the expensive one. That is worth remembering the next time "just put it in the .cpp" sounds like tidying up.

9.4 The diagnostics, and a zero that is an answer

Four functions in the header do no stepping at all, and they exist so that the analysis in this lesson is something the engine can do rather than something the lesson merely says: spring_energy, shadow_energy, area_factor and max_stable_step. Sections B, C and F of the harness check their own claims against these, which means the header is never allowed to drift away from the prose.

The one worth singling out is max_stable_step: 

case integrator::explicit_euler:      return 0.0f;

case integrator::semi_implicit_euler:
case integrator::velocity_verlet:     return 2.0f / omega;

A function called "largest stable step" returning zero looks like a bug and is the most honest line in the file. There is no stable step size for explicit Euler on an oscillator; the answer is not "small", it is "none". Returning some small positive number would be a lie the caller could act on. Returning zero means somebody who writes h = 0.5f * max_stable_step(rule, w) gets a simulation that does not move — which is a far better bug report than a simulation that quietly explodes twenty seconds into a playtest.

The omega <= 0 case returns HUGE_VALF rather than a large number, for the same reason: with no restoring force there is nothing to be unstable about, every rule reduces to straight-line motion, and a caller comparing against the result wants the real answer rather than a sentinel it has to know about.

9.5 The demo, and an accumulator inside an accumulator

demos/integrate draws phase space, because §5 is the argument and a bouncing dot is not. It also does one structural thing worth copying. 

void on_fixed_step(float h) override
{
    if (paused_) { return; }
    sim_.begin_frame(h);
    while (sim_.next_step()) { advance(sim_.h()); }
}

That is Lesson 1.4's fixed_step — the same class, unchanged — running inside the application's own fixed step. It looks redundant and it is the point: the application's rate is a platform decision, and the simulation's rate is the variable this demo exists to vary. Pressing Up changes what physics does without changing how often the screen updates, and the two concerns stay separable because 1.4 made the accumulator a type instead of four lines in a loop.

Two smaller decisions in that file earned their comments. The first is a ceiling on the view scale: the plot follows the worst track only up to three amplitudes, after which the red curve simply leaves. Without the ceiling a diverging explicit Euler pulls the camera back without limit and every other curve collapses to a dot within about ten seconds — the picture ends up showing nothing but the thing that is broken, at exactly the moment you want to compare it against the things that are not.

The second is that the curve leaving means the panel needs a real clip, so the file carries a twenty-line Cohen–Sutherland. engine::draw_line deliberately does not clip — Lesson 2.1 says so in a comment, walks every pixel and lets put_pixel's bounds check discard the ones outside — which is correct for the framebuffer and does nothing at all to stop the red spiral being drawn straight across the energy chart next door. That exercise from 2.1 came due here, seventy lessons later, for a reason nobody predicted.

10. The budget, and the free lunch

Section I times ten thousand bodies for a hundred steps, with the rule as a compile-time constant so that each of the three loops is a separate monomorphic function. That detail is not incidental — the first version passed the rule as a runtime integrator, and the measurement then depended on whether the optimiser felt like unswitching the loop, which it did on some runs and not others. Explicit came out 15% faster than semi-implicit in one run and 30% slower in the next. A benchmark whose answer changes run to run is measuring the compiler, not the code. 

I.1  rule            ns/body/step   force evals
   explicit           0.994          1
   semi-impl          0.963          1
   verlet             1.136          2

I.5  CONTROL: a loop that does nothing but walk the
     same array.
   touch only         0.608 ns/body/step

0.994 against 0.963. They differ by 3%, against a run-to-run spread of the same size, which is the measurement's way of saying they cost the same — as they must, because they are the same loads, the same multiplies and the same stores, differing only in which order two of them happen. The control underneath says 0.608 ns of that is walking the array at all, so the arithmetic itself is about a third of a nanosecond.

Which makes this one of the genuinely rare things in engineering: a free lunch. One of these two rules is unconditionally unstable and the other is stable up to a hard, computable limit, and they cost the same. There is no trade-off to weigh, no profile to consult, no "it depends on your workload". There is only a correct choice and an incorrect one.

And for scale: ten thousand bodies under gravity cost 0.012 ms of a 16.67 ms frame, which is 0.07% of it. Integration is not where physics time goes. Collision detection is, and Lessons 8.4 through 8.8 are about that — which is worth knowing now, so that nobody spends an afternoon hand-vectorising the cheapest loop in the module.

This lesson built the smallest honest thing, and the list of what it is not is longer than the list of what it is.

  • No mass, no forces. integrate takes an acceleration. Turning a force into one is a = F/m, and it needs a body to have a mass, which is Lesson 8.2.
  • No rotation. Angular motion has the same structure and a much worse-behaved version of it: the "mass" is a tensor that rotates with the body, and the equations have a term with no linear analogue at all. Lesson 8.3, and it needs the quaternions from 7.4.
  • No collision, no constraints. Which is why §6.3's stability limit does not immediately doom us. A contact is not going to be a stiff spring; it is going to be an impulse (8.9) inside an iterative solver (8.10), for precisely the reason measured there.
  • No adaptive step. A general-purpose ODE solver estimates its own error and shrinks the step when it is too large. Games do not do this — a variable step makes replays and lockstep networking impossible, and Lesson 1.4 is the whole argument. We choose h once and make the physics fit inside it.
  • No RK4. The fourth-order Runge–Kutta everybody meets first is more accurate than anything here per step, and it is not symplectic: it has the same disease as explicit Euler, milder. It costs four force evaluations, which for a physics engine is four broadphase queries. Exercise 5 measures its determinant, and the answer is worth the twenty minutes.
  • No velocity-dependent force in the symplectic path, for the reason §8 gives. Drag is handled exactly and separately, and anything more exotic — a magnetic force, a gyroscopic term — needs its own analysis rather than an assumption that the reordering helps.

14. Common pitfalls & debugging

  1. Symptom: everything is fine for thirty seconds, then the scene detonates. Cause: explicit Euler somewhere, plus a position-dependent force. The tell is the shape — it is not a jump, it is a smooth exponential ramp that crosses the visible threshold all at once. Fix: swap the two lines. To confirm before you fix, log spring_energy (or just length(velocity)) once a second and look for a constant ratio between consecutive samples. A constant ratio is exponential growth; a constant difference is something else entirely.

  2. Symptom: you swapped the lines and it still blows up, but slower. Cause: hω ≥ 2. Semi-implicit Euler is stable up to a limit and you are past it, which happens the moment somebody raises a stiffness. Fix: call max_stable_step(rule, natural_frequency(k, m)) and compare it against your h. If the answer is that you need 4 kHz, the fix is not a smaller step — go and read §6.3, because the thing you are modelling as a spring probably should not be one.

  3. Symptom: the simulation is stable but everything feels mushy and slightly dead. Cause: you reached for backward Euler, or for an over-damped drag, or for a "stabilisation" factor slightly below 1 that somebody added to stop pitfall 1. All three are the det < 1 case of §5.3, and they trade an explosion for a slow leak. Fix: the leak is not the price of stability — semi-implicit Euler is stable with det exactly 1. If you want damping, add it deliberately with apply_drag, where it has a coefficient you can tune and a name.

  4. Symptom: the game plays differently on a 144 Hz machine. Cause: almost always a per-step multiply like v *= 0.99f; occasionally a per-step additive tweak, which has the same disease. Fix: damping_factor(retained_per_second, h), and check your change against §8.1's control — at the rate the constant was tuned at, the two must agree to float precision, or you have changed the feel rather than preserved it.

  5. Symptom: a body passes through a thin floor at high speed. Cause: not the integrator. That is tunnelling, and it is a collision-detection problem — the body was on one side at step n and the other at n+1 and nothing looked in between. Fix: nothing in this lesson. Lesson 1.8 showed the swept test that catches it and 8.13 is where it returns for real. It is listed here because "the physics is wrong" is one symptom with several unrelated causes, and reaching for the integrator first is the common misdiagnosis.

  6. Symptom: your own energy meter says everything is perfect, forever. Cause: you are plotting shadow_energy, or you wrote a meter that evaluates the same expression the integrator is built from. Fix: plot spring_energy for the physical claim and shadow_energy for the numerical one, and expect them to disagree by hω. This is C.5's control in the harness: it runs the shadow meter on explicit Euler, where it must also fail, so that C.4's flat line is evidence about the method rather than about the meter.

15. Exercises

  1. Find the limit with your hands (guided). Run the demo, press 2 and 3 to leave only semi-implicit Euler, and hold Right to stiffen the spring while watching the h*w readout. Find the value at which the orbit stops being an ellipse. Then check it against the panel's max_stable_step.

    Hint: it will not look like an explosion at first — it will look like the ellipse getting narrower and more slanted, exactly as figure 6's left panel predicts, right up until there is no ellipse. Watch the shape, not the size. If you overshoot, R resets.

  2. The shadow energy, by substitution (guided). Verify algebraically that v² + ω²x² − hω²·xv is invariant under semi-implicit Euler. Substitute x' = (1 − h²ω²)x + hv and v' = v − hω²x, expand, and collect the coefficients of v², x² and xv.

    Hint: write u = h²ω² before you start and the algebra halves. The x² coefficient is the one that looks hopeless and collapses: ω²[u + (1−u)² + u(1−u)] = ω²[u + 1 − 2u + u² + u − u²] = ω². If your xv coefficient comes out as anything but −hω², check the sign of the cross term you got from squaring x'.

  3. Position Verlet. There is a third symplectic method in common use, and it stores no velocity at all: xn+1 = 2xn − xn−1 + a·h², with the velocity recovered when needed as (xn+1 − xn−1)/2h. Add it, measure its order and its determinant with sections A and B, and then find the thing it is bad at.

    Hint: to write it as a matrix you need a state of (xn, xn−1) rather than (x, v). The thing it is bad at is a change of step size — try halving h mid-run and watch what happens to a body in flight, then work out why from the fact that h is baked into the stored history rather than into a velocity.

  4. The exponent, empirically. §5.5 claims the time to double the energy is proportional to 1/h. Add a section to the harness that measures it as a function of ω instead, at fixed h, and find the power.

    Hint: predict before you measure. ln(2)/ln(1 + h²ω²) ≈ ln(2)/(h²ω²) steps, times h seconds each, so the time should go as 1/(hω²) — a slope of −2 in ω. Stiffness hurts twice as fast as step size helps, which is the real reason a stiff spring is the dangerous case.

  5. RK4 is not the answer (engine challenge). Implement classical fourth-order Runge–Kutta for this system and put it through sections A, B and C. You will find the best order in the lesson and a determinant that is not 1. Then decide, with numbers, whether you would ship it.

    Hint: its determinant is 1 − u³/72 + … in u = h²ω² — less than 1, so it is the backward-Euler disease in fifth-order clothing: it damps, very gently, forever. Now weigh that against four force evaluations per step, which in Module 8 means four broadphase queries. The interesting part of this exercise is the argument, not the code.

  6. An honest drag, inside the step (engine challenge). apply_drag is exact for the velocity but leaves the position to whichever order you call it in. Derive the exact position integral for a = −kv — it is x + (v/k)(1 − e^(−kh)) — implement an integrate_with_drag, and measure the position error against the current "call them one after the other" approach at 60 Hz and at 10 Hz.

    Hint: the interesting result is how small it is at 60 Hz and how fast it grows as hk approaches 1. Then ask the design question the measurement raises: is an exact drag integrator worth a special case in the step, or is the honest answer that anything with hk near 1 is already outside what this loop should be asked to do?

16. Recap & next

The engine can advance a state through time, and — just as usefully — it can tell you whether the step size you chose is one that works.

The capability is small: two vectors, three rules, and about three hundred lines including the comments. The argument is not. It began with a question that sounds like arithmetic — given a velocity, where are you a moment later? — and the answer turned on the fact that one step of an integrator is a matrix, and a matrix has a determinant. Explicit Euler's is 1 + h²ω², which is greater than one for every step size that exists, so it inflates phase space on every step forever: ×1.92 energy per second on a 1 Hz spring at 60 Hz, ×1.13 × 10¹⁷ in a minute, a one-centimetre overlap becoming 3,357 km. Moving one line above another makes it exactly one, and costs nothing — 0.994 ns against 0.963 ns, a difference smaller than the noise.

Four things are worth carrying forward past this page.

  • Order of accuracy could not see any of it. It is a statement about h → 0 at fixed time; a game asks about t → ∞ at fixed h. Both Euler rules measured respectably on the first question while one of them was multiplying its error by 1.9 every second on the second.
  • What is conserved is not what you assumed. Semi-implicit Euler does not preserve energy — the true energy wobbles by exactly hω, 10.5% at 60 Hz. It preserves a sheared cousin, to four parts in a million over 3,600 float steps, and the orbit is a level set of that. Which is why the wobble never accumulates into a trend.
  • Stability was the same statement twice. Once as "the map does not inflate area", once as "the conserved form is positive-definite, so its level sets are closed". Both are one determinant, and the second gives hω < 2 — measured by bisection at 0.3182939 against a predicted 0.3183099.
  • Gravity forgives and contacts do not. Under constant acceleration all three rules err by ±½·a·h·t, linearly, and the relative error gets better the longer you watch. The whole catastrophe is reserved for forces that depend on position, which is every force in the rest of this module.

That last point is also the bridge. §6.3 priced a stiff spring at 60 Hz and the answer was thousands of steps per second for a single contact — which is the argument, made before meeting the machinery, for why contacts in this engine will be impulses inside an iterative solver rather than penalty springs. Lesson 8.10 is where that architecture arrives; the number that justifies it was computed here.
