Module 6 — Advanced Rendering · Lesson 6.15

Skybox and Image-Based Lighting

Since Lesson 3.6 the engine has carried an apology called ambient: one constant radiance standing in for the sky, the floor, the walls and every bounce off all of them. Two things are wrong with it. It has no direction, so a face turned to the sky and one turned to the floor receive identical fill and nothing in the scene is ever grounded. And it has no specular half at all — a sentence shipped in this engine since 6.4, naming this lesson by number as the fix. An environment map replaces it with radiance as a function of direction, and the honest part of the technique is that the sky you see and the sky that lights you are one texture. Getting there means two integrals, and only one of them is exact: the diffuse half factors perfectly and reproduces Lesson 6.2's albedo × ambient to six decimals on a uniform sky, while the specular half does not factor and we pretend it does — at a price this lesson measures rather than hedges. 0.08% at roughness 0.10, 29.0% dark at roughness 1 seen edge-on, 73.0% once the sky has a sun in it. And the mapping from roughness to mip level, which every engine picks by taste, turns out to be derivable from 6.14's closed-form lobe width — to within 0.77 of a level of what everybody ships.

1The problem: an apology called ambient

Open engine/include/engine/gfx/light.hpp and scroll to the bottom of shade(). The last statement in the function has looked like this since Lesson 3.6:

f_r * E is everything this module has built — a microfacet BRDF fed by a directional light, with Fresnel coupling the two lobes and a shadow map deciding whether the light arrives at all. And then there is albedo * ambient, which is a constant.

Lesson 6.2 did that term a real service. It took the word ambient, gave it a physical reading — a uniform radiance La arriving from every direction of the hemisphere — and pushed it through the Lambert BRDF:

\[ L_o = \int_\Omega \frac{\text{albedo}}{\pi} \, L_a \cos\theta \, d\omega = \frac{\text{albedo}}{\pi} L_a \pi = \text{albedo} \cdot L_a \]
(The integral of albedo/π · L_a · cos θ over the hemisphere is albedo/π · L_a · π, which is albedo · L_a. The π cancels exactly.)

That is a genuinely nice result and it is why the expression above is not a fudge: it is the right answer for a uniform environment. What 6.2 also said, and what we are here to cash, is that the arithmetic was never the problem. The assumption was.

1.1 What the assumption costs, twice

The first failure is the one you can state in a sentence: a constant has no direction. Real bounced light does not arrive equally from everywhere. Outdoors, most of it comes from the sky, some from the ground, and the two are different colours and different brightnesses by an order of magnitude. Indoors it comes from whichever walls are lit. A surface facing up should receive more fill than one facing down, and in this engine, today, they receive exactly the same.

You have seen the consequence without necessarily naming it. Objects in our renders do not look placed. They look like they were cut out and laid on top of the scene, because nothing about their shading knows which way is up.

The second failure is worse and it has been on record for eleven lessons. Here is the comment that sits directly above that return statement, shipped in the engine since Lesson 6.4:

THE AMBIENT TERM IS STILL WRONG IN THE SAME WAY IT ALWAYS WAS, and Lesson 6.4 deliberately did not touch it: it has no specular counterpart, so a mirror in a bright uniform room still renders black except where the key light hits it. That missing term is the specular half of image-based lighting, and it is Lesson 6.15's subject.

Look at the expression again and you can see exactly why. albedo * ambient passes through no BRDF — it is the Lambert answer, with the π already cancelled. So the ambient fill is purely diffuse. And Lesson 6.4 established the metallic workflow: a conductor has no diffuse lobe at all, because it absorbs whatever crosses its interface within a few atoms. Its diffuse_albedo is zero by construction.

Put those two facts together. In this engine, a metal receives no ambient light whatsoever. Chrome standing in the middle of a brightly lit room is black except for one reflected lamp. That is not a subtle inaccuracy; it is the single largest error in the renderer, and Lesson 6.12 made it worse by introducing four polished metals to look at it with.

Why 6.4 left it alone on purpose. It would have been easy to give the ambient term a specular half by guessing — multiply f0 by ambient and move on. That would have produced a plausible picture and an unfalsifiable one. The reason to wait is that the specular half of ambient light is an integral over the environment, and until there is an environment to integrate, any expression you write is a number chosen to look right. 6.4 named the gap and left it; this lesson fills it with something that can be checked against a reference.

1.2 What we are going to build

Everything below follows from one change of representation. Instead of a single linear_rgb ambient, the engine will hold

\[ L_i(\mathbf{d}) \quad \text{— the radiance arriving from direction } \mathbf{d} \]
(a function from a direction to a quantity of light), sampled on the six faces of a cube.

That single object does two jobs, and the fact that it is the same object for both is the honest part of the whole technique:

  • It is the background. A ray through each pixel that hits no geometry reads the environment directly. That is the skybox.
  • It is the light. Every shaded point integrates it over the hemisphere. That is image-based lighting.

Because they are one texture, a reflection cannot disagree with its surroundings. You have seen games where a puddle reflects a sky that is not the sky above it; that happens when the two are authored separately. Here it is structurally impossible.

2Building intuition: light as a function of direction

Stand outside on an overcast day and look around slowly. In every direction you look, some amount of light is arriving at your eye. Straight up it is bright; toward the horizon it is brighter still and warmer; below the horizon it is whatever the ground is doing. If you could record the brightness in every direction, you would have a complete description of the light in that place — complete for the purpose of shading an object standing there, anyway.

That recording is an environment map. It is a function on the sphere of directions, and the entire subject is (a) how to store such a function and (b) how to integrate against it fast enough to do per pixel.

2.1 The integral we cannot afford

Lesson 6.2 wrote down what a shaded point owes. Here it is again, now with an environment rather than a single light on the right-hand side:

\[ L_o(\mathbf{v}) = \int_\Omega f_r(\mathbf{l}, \mathbf{v}) \; L_i(\mathbf{l}) \; (\mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{l}) \; d\omega \]
(The light leaving toward the eye is the integral, over the hemisphere above the surface, of the BRDF times the arriving radiance times the cosine.)

Put a number on that. A modest environment is 128×128 on each of six faces, which is 98,304 texels; half of them are in the hemisphere above any given normal. At 1920×1080 with one shaded fragment per pixel, evaluating this integral honestly is

2,073,600 pixels × 49,152 directions ≈ 1.0 × 1011 BRDF evaluations per frame.

At a generous ten per nanosecond that is about ten seconds a frame. This is not a per-frame budget problem; it is a per-frame impossibility, and it does not become possible on better hardware — it is five orders of magnitude away.

So the whole technique is two different ways of moving the integral offline. And the two halves of the BRDF permit that to very different degrees, which is the most important structural fact in this lesson and the thing most treatments blur.

2.2 A worked example before any formalism

Take the simplest environment there is: uniform, radiance 1 in every direction. A white Lambertian surface, albedo 0.8, facing anywhere at all.

What should come out? The surface reflects 0.8 of the light that hits it, diffusely. The light that hits it is the hemisphere's worth of radiance 1, projected by the cosine, which is the integral of cos θ over the hemisphere, which is exactly π. So the irradiance is π, the BRDF is 0.8/π, and the outgoing radiance is

\[ \frac{0.8}{\pi} \times \pi = 0.8 \]

Now notice what just happened: that is the number albedo × ambient has been producing since Lesson 3.6. The old term is not something the new machinery replaces; it is the special case the new machinery reduces to.

This gives us the single best test in the lesson, and it is the first one we will write: build a uniform environment, run the whole precomputation over it, shade a point, and the answer must be the old one. A new subsystem that reproduces the old one on the old one's own input is a subsystem you can trust with a new input. When we run it, the agreement is 1.3 × 10⁻⁵ relative.

This is the ninety-percent picture and here is the missing ten. An environment map records light arriving at one point in space — the point it was captured from. Using it everywhere assumes the environment is infinitely far away, which is exactly right for a sky and exactly wrong for a lamp two metres off. Real engines place many probes and blend between them, and add parallax correction so a reflection of a nearby wall moves as you do. Everything in this lesson is the single-distant-probe case; §11 points at the rest.

3The cube, and the texel that is worth five others

Before any integral, we need somewhere to put Li(d). The obvious answer is a rectangle: longitude across, latitude down, the way a world map works. That is called an equirectangular or lat-long map, it is easy to author, and most downloadable HDR environments come in that form.

It is also a bad container, for the same reason a Mercator projection makes Greenland the size of Africa. Every texel in the top row of a lat-long image covers the same point — the pole — so a 2:1 image spends an entire scanline on one direction, and roughly a quarter of its texels on the two polar caps. The distortion is unbounded: as you approach the pole, the solid angle per texel goes to zero.

A cube is better. Six flat squares wrapped around the sphere, each covering 90° by 90°. Its worst distortion is at a corner, and — as we are about to derive — that corner texel is about a fifth of a centre one. A factor of five is a bounded, knowable, correctable amount; an unbounded one is not. The hardware also samples cube maps natively, with filtering that works across face edges, which a lat-long lookup does not get.

3.1 Direction to face, and every sign in the table

The algorithm is one sentence: the largest component of the direction picks the face, and dividing the other two by its magnitude projects the direction onto that face.

Take d = (0.4, 0.3, −0.866). The magnitudes are 0.4, 0.3 and 0.866, so z is largest and it is negative: the −Z face. Dividing the other two by 0.866 gives (0.462, 0.346) on that face's plane — a point in [−1, 1]² — and mapping that to [0, 1] gives the texture coordinates.

Everything else is a sign convention, and the sign convention is the part that costs people a day. Here it is in full, exactly as the D3D and OpenGL specifications give it, where sc runs along the face's u axis, tc along its v axis, and ma is the major axis:

then u = 0.5·(sc/|ma| + 1) and v = 0.5·(tc/|ma| + 1).

Two things in that table are worth stopping on.

tc = −y on four of the six faces is where v grows downward comes from, and it is the same top-left texel origin every other image in this engine has used since Lesson 3.9 (texel_origin::top_left). That is not a coincidence — both conventions descend from the same lineage — but it is worth noticing that they agree, because it means a cube face and an ordinary texture are stored the same way round.

And the table was written for a LEFT-handed coordinate system, while conventions §2 pins this engine's world as right-handed, Y-up, −Z forward. This is exactly the class of silent bug §10 of the course spec warns about, so let us not assume — let us measure it. Look along +X in a right-handed Y-up world: your right hand points along forward × up = X × Y = +Z. The table puts the +X face's u axis along −z. So: a cube map whose faces were photographed or rendered with a right-handed camera and then sampled by this table comes out mirrored. On a sky that is almost invisible; on anything with writing in it, unmistakable. The fix is not to "correct" the table — the hardware implements it and we cannot change it — but to generate each face with the axes the table names. Our procedural sky does that by construction, because it evaluates a function of whatever direction cube_to_direction hands it, so the bake and the lookup agree by construction rather than by agreement.

3.2 The solid angle of a texel, derived

Here is the fact that every integral in this lesson depends on, and that is easy to skip: the texels of a cube face do not subtend equal solid angles.

Picture one face as a flat square at distance 1 from the origin, spanning [−1, 1] in each of its two axes. A texel at face coordinates (x, y) is at distance

\[ r = \sqrt{1 + x^2 + y^2} \]

from the origin — further away than the centre texel, which is at exactly 1. Two separate things follow, and they compound:

  1. Inverse square. Solid angle falls as 1/r², because that is what solid angle is.
  2. Foreshortening. The texel's own little patch of plane is tilted away from the line of sight. The face's normal is the major axis, and the angle between it and the direction has cos θ = 1/r — because the major-axis component of the un-normalised direction (x, y, 1) is 1 and its length is r.

Multiply them:

\[ d\omega = \frac{dA \cos\theta}{r^2} = \frac{dA}{r^3} \]
(the solid angle of a texel is its area divided by the cube of its distance)

Now the worked number. At the centre of a face, x = y = 0, so r = 1 and r³ = 1. At a corner, x = y = 1, so r = √3 = 1.7321 and

\[ r^3 = 3\sqrt{3} = 5.196 \]

The centre texel is worth 5.196 corner texels. That is the whole argument, and it is not a rounding detail — it is a factor of five sitting inside every sum you might write over a cube map.

The harness checks the asymptote directly, by measuring the ratio at increasing resolutions and watching it converge:

And the six faces' solid angles sum to 4π — the whole sphere — to within double-precision round-off, which is the one check that says the implementation transcribes the formula correctly. A sum that came to 4.01π would mean a sign or an index was wrong somewhere, and nothing else in the lesson would catch it.

Why the code uses a closed form and not dA/r³. The derivation above is exact in the limit of a small texel, and a texel at 8×8 is 22.5° across, which is not small. The implementation uses Lambert's formula for the solid angle of an axis-aligned rectangle, f(x, y) = atan2(xy, √(x²+y²+1)), differenced at the four corners — which is what a two-dimensional integral's antiderivative looks like. That is why the 4π check passes at 8×8 and not merely at 512.

3.3 What ignoring it costs, and why that is a bug and not an error

Suppose you write the obvious loop: walk every texel of every face, multiply by the cosine, add it up, divide by the number of texels. What do you get?

The harness measures it against the solid-angle-weighted answer, whose true value is known exactly: the mean of cos θ over a hemisphere is the integral of the cosine divided by the hemisphere's area, π / 2π = 0.5.

One per cent. That is small — smaller than you might fear from a factor of 5.196, because the over-weighted corners are relatively few and the cosine partly compensates. It is also worth being honest that one per cent of an ambient term is not going to be the thing you notice.

But look at the second row. Sixteen times as many texels, and the error did not move: 1.010005 became 1.010066. It got very slightly worse. That is the signature of a bias rather than a discretisation error, and the distinction is the whole point: a discretisation error is a number you can buy down with memory, and a bias is a number that will still be there when you have spent all the memory you own.

Which is exactly why cube_texel_solid_angle exists as a named public function rather than as an expression inside one loop. Every integral in the file calls it, and a reader can tell at a glance which ones do.

4The two integrals, and only one of them is exact

We have the environment stored and we know how to weight its texels. Now the integral, which we established in §2.1 cannot be evaluated at run time. Both halves of the BRDF get moved offline, and they get moved offline in completely different ways, because one of them permits it and the other does not.

4.1 The diffuse half factors, exactly

Start with Lambert, because Lambert is the easy case and the reason it is easy is worth naming. Lesson 6.2 derived its BRDF:

\[ f_r = \frac{\text{albedo}}{\pi} \]

Look at what is not in that expression: l. The Lambert BRDF does not depend on the incoming direction at all — that is what "diffuse" means. So it is a constant with respect to the integration variable, and a constant comes straight out of an integral:

\[ L_o = \int_\Omega \frac{\text{albedo}}{\pi} L_i(\mathbf{l}) (\mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{l}) \, d\omega = \frac{\text{albedo}}{\pi} \underbrace{\int_\Omega L_i(\mathbf{l}) (\mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{l}) \, d\omega}_{E(\mathbf{n})} \]
(pull the constant out, and what is left is the irradiance E(n))

And now look at what E(n) depends on: the environment, which is fixed, and n. Only the normal. Not the view, not the material, not the position. So it is a function on the sphere of directions, which is to say it is another cube map, which we can compute once and look up with one fetch.

There is no approximation anywhere in that paragraph. That is the sentence to take away. People say "IBL is approximate" and it is half true; the diffuse half is not approximate at all, it is a change in the order of two exact operations. The only error is discretisation, and we can measure it.

Feed the convolution a uniform environment of radiance 1 and it must return exactly π — because that is the integral of the cosine over the hemisphere and we did that arithmetic by hand in §2.2. Here is what the code returns:

4.2 The specular half does not factor, and we pretend it does

Now try the same move on the specular lobe. The Cook–Torrance BRDF from Lesson 6.4 is

\[ f_r(\mathbf{l}, \mathbf{v}) = \frac{D(\mathbf{h}) \, G(\mathbf{l}, \mathbf{v}) \, F(\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{h})} {4 (\mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{l})(\mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{v})} \]

and h is the halfway vector between l and v. Every term depends on l, and on l and v together. Nothing comes out of the integral. There is no E(n) to precompute.

The split-sum approximation (Karis, 2013) factors it anyway:

\[ \int_\Omega f_r L_i (\mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{l}) \, d\omega \;\approx\; \underbrace{\left[ \frac{\sum_k L_i(\mathbf{l}_k)(\mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{l}_k)}{\sum_k (\mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{l}_k)} \right]}_{\text{prefiltered environment}} \times \underbrace{\left[ \int_\Omega f_r (\mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{l}) \, d\omega \right]}_{\text{BRDF integral}} \]
(a weighted average of the environment over the lobe, times the integral of the BRDF alone)

Say plainly what just happened: an integral of a product was replaced by a product of integrals. That is not an identity. It is exactly true when one factor is constant over the region the other one cares about, and approximately true when it is nearly so — which for a narrow lobe on a smooth environment it very nearly is, and for a wide lobe on an environment with a sun in it very much is not. We will measure both cases.

What each bracket buys:

  • The left bracket is a blur. It is the environment averaged over the specular lobe's footprint, which is a function of roughness — so it becomes a mip chain indexed by roughness rather than by screen footprint. That is the direct connection to Lesson 6.10, and §5 makes the indexing derivable rather than chosen.
  • The right bracket is a two-dimensional table, and this is the part that is genuinely surprising. It contains no Li, so it does not depend on the environment. And — see below — Schlick's Fresnel makes it independent of F0 too. What is left depends only on (n·v) and roughness. One 64×64 image serves every material in every scene this engine will ever render.

Why the table is two-dimensional and not five

Schlick's approximation to Fresnel, from Lesson 6.4:

\[ F = F_0 + (1 - F_0)(1 - \mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{h})^5 \]

Write fc = (1 − v·h)⁵ and rearrange:

\[ F = F_0 (1 - f_c) + f_c \]

It is linear in F0, with the two coefficients depending only on the geometry. So the integral splits into a part that multiplies F0 and a part that does not, and neither of them mentions F0:

\[ \int f_r (\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{l}) \, d\omega = F_0 \underbrace{\int w (1 - f_c)}_{\text{scale}} + \underbrace{\int w f_c}_{\text{bias}} \]

and the shader does specular = prefiltered × (F0 × scale + bias). Gold, chrome and plastic differ only in F0, which never entered the table. This is the single nicest consequence of Schlick's approximation and it is a large part of why that approximation is universal.

Where D went

One detail in the implementation surprises everybody the first time: the BRDF integrator contains no NDF at all. Here is the estimator, and the whole of the algebra behind it:

We sample directions from GGX's own distribution — the inverse CDF is ggx_importance_sample — so the probability density of a sample is proportional to D(h)(n·h), times the Jacobian 1/(4 v·h) of the change of variables from half-vectors to light directions. A Monte Carlo estimator divides the integrand by the pdf, so

\[ \frac{f_r (\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{l})}{p(\mathbf{l})} = \frac{\dfrac{D G F}{4(\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{l})(\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{v})}(\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{l})} {\dfrac{D (\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{h})}{4(\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{h})}} = \frac{G F (\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{h})}{(\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{h})(\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{v})} \]

The D cancels exactly. So does the 4, and so do both (n·l) factors. What is left is four dot products and the geometry term — which is why the reference implementations of this function look like they forgot the most important part of the BRDF. They did not; importance sampling ate it.

4.3 The price, measured and decomposed

Now we make good on §4.2's promise. The harness runs the true integral by brute-force importance sampling at a million samples, and compares it against what the engine's shipped image_based_light returns. The environment is our procedural sky at 128×128 per face.

But it reports three numbers per row, not two, and the third is the point. The brute force is run twice: once against the analytic sky, and once against the baked cube map the engine actually reads. That separates two error sources that are normally reported as one:

  • truth → cube-truth is what the bake lost, which you can buy back with texels.
  • cube-truth → split-sum is the approximation, which you cannot.

  • At roughness 0.10 it is essentially exact — 0.08% at worst. A narrow lobe barely notices which direction it was centred on. The technique degrades smoothly away from a mirror, which is the opposite of how most approximations behave and is why it is used.
  • At near-normal incidence it never exceeds 5.8% at any roughness.
  • At a grazing view it reaches 29.0% dark. More than four times the error, on exactly the same material.

That asymmetry is not noise. It is the second approximation, which most treatments mention in passing and which does most of the visible damage.

The second assumption: n = v = r

The prefiltered chain is a cube map, so a lookup into it is indexed by one direction. But the true integral depends on two — the normal and the view. There is no way to index a cube map by a pair of directions, so the prefilter has to assume they coincide:

\[ \mathbf{n} = \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{r} \]

which is exactly true when you look straight down at a surface and progressively wronger as you look along it. Two things go wrong at a grazing view, and the measurement above shows both:

  1. The lobe is baked round when it should be stretched. A rough surface seen at a grazing angle shows a long, comet-shaped smear, not a disc. The prefilter cannot represent that, so you get a disc.
  2. The lobe is centred on the wrong thing. It is baked around the reflection direction, and at a grazing view the reflection points nearly along the horizon — so the prefilter's lobe reaches below it and averages in ground the real surface, whose normal points up, never sees at all. Our ground is dark, so the answer comes out dark. That is precisely the 29% in the last row.

And a third cause, which only appears with a sun

Run the same table with the sun disc in the sky and the error grows to 73.0%. That is a different mechanism again, and it is visible in the left bracket of the split sum: the prefilter weights radiance by (n·l) alone, where the true integral weights it by the whole BRDF. Those two weightings disagree most where the environment is nearly a delta function — which is exactly what a sun is.

So the error of this technique depends on the environment, not only on the material. That is not something you can read off the paper, and it is the practical reason production renderers keep the sun as a separate analytic light rather than baking it into the environment map. Our own engine already does that — the key light in scene_light_uniforms is separate — so in practice you would bake a sky without its sun and let the directional light handle it. The exercises ask you to measure the difference.

5Which level? Deriving what everybody guesses

The prefiltered chain has levels, each blurred by a different roughness. A shader with a material of roughness r has to pick one. Open any engine and you will find

…with no argument attached. It is a reasonable-looking line and it is in every reference implementation. Where does it come from, and how wrong is it?

Lesson 6.14 gives us the tools to answer that, which is the nicest piece of continuity in this module. It derived the GGX lobe's half-width in closed form:

\[ \sin t = \alpha \sqrt{\frac{\sqrt2 - 1}{1 - \alpha^2}} \]

Three lines of algebra, no fitting, exact at every roughness. What we need now is to compare that against a texel — and a half-angle is not commensurable with a texel. A solid angle is.

5.1 The derivation

The cap of a cone of half-angle t subtends

\[ \Omega_{\text{lobe}} = 2\pi (1 - \cos t) \]
(the spherical-cap area — the one piece of solid-angle geometry worth memorising; at t = 90° it gives 2π, the whole hemisphere, which is the check to run first)

And a texel at level k of a cube whose base face is n across: each level halves the side, so it quadruples the solid angle.

\[ \Omega_{\text{texel}}(k) = \omega_0 \cdot 4^k \]

Set them equal — which level's texels are about as wide as this lobe? — and take logs:

\[ k = \log_4\!\left( \frac{\Omega_{\text{lobe}}}{\omega_0} \right) = \tfrac12 \log_2\!\left( \frac{\Omega_{\text{lobe}}}{\omega_0} \right) \]

That is prefilter_level_for, and it is three lines of code with no free parameters in it.

One choice inside it has to be stated. ω0 is the solid angle of which level-0 texel? §3.2 showed they differ by 5.196. The code uses the centre texel, and the reason is that we are asking what resolution the map achieves where it is best: a level that cannot resolve the lobe even at the face centre certainly cannot at the corner. Using the corner instead would shift every answer by log₄(5.196) = 1.19 levels — which is more than the entire disagreement we are about to measure, so the choice is not a detail.

The folklore is not arbitrary. Over the whole range the two never differ by more than 0.770 of a level (worst at roughness 0.45), and they agree exactly at both ends. For a line with no derivation attached, that is a good line. For comparison, sqrt(roughness) — which also circulates — is off by 2.214 levels, nearly three times as much.

But we now know something the folklore does not tell you, which is the direction of the error:

  • At low roughness it over-blurs. At 0.10 the lobe wants level 0 and the mapping reads 0.700 — most of a level of blur applied to a near-mirror, which is exactly where you would notice it.
  • From 0.3 to 0.9 it under-blurs, by up to 0.77 of a level. Less visible, because a rough reflection has no detail to lose.

There is also a hard limit hiding in that table. At roughness 0.10 the lobe subtends 0.000130 sr, while a level-0 texel on a 128 face subtends about 0.000244 sr — the lobe is smaller than one texel. Below roughness ≈0.11 the base resolution, not the prefilter, is what limits the reflection. If you want a crisper mirror, the answer is a bigger cube, not a deeper chain.

So which one does the engine ship? The fitted one — and the engine ships the derived one too, as a separate function, with the boundary marked. This is 6.14's discipline applied again: that lesson kept its closed-form lobe width and Tokuyoshi & Kaplanyan's fitted constants in the same file with a comment saying which was which. The reason the lookup must use the linear mapping is not taste: the chain was built with roughness spread linearly across its levels, so reading it any other way would ask for level 4.2 of a table whose level 4.2 means something else. prefilter_level_for's job is to tell you whether your chain is deep enough, not to index it.

Everything above lands in one new pair of files, engine/gfx/cubemap.{hpp,cpp}, plus two small additions to headers that already exist. We will build it in the order the argument was made: the faces, the environment, the two integrals, and then the function that puts them together.

6.1 The face table, as data

6.2 A cube map is six hdr_buffers, and that is not a detail

The faces are hdr_buffer — Lesson 6.12's float container — and not engine::texture. The reason is the one number §D of the harness measures:

Radiance has no upper bound. The sky is around 0.5 and the sun in the same image is five thousand times brighter. Storing that in a [0,1] container clips the sun to white before any integral runs, and the prefilter then faithfully integrates a lie. This is the same argument gpu_post.hpp made for the HDR framebuffer in 6.12, arriving at the other end of the pipeline.

6.3 The irradiance convolution, and the one line it turns on

The diffuse integral is a double loop over output texels and input texels. The body is four lines; here is the part that matters:

p.weight is cube_texel_solid_angle, precomputed. Remove it and you have the 1.01% bias of §3.3, permanently.

Two implementation decisions in there are worth the words:

The input is precomputed into a flat array first. Every output texel integrates over every input texel, so anything depending only on the input — its direction and its solid angle — is hoisted. At 32 out and 128 in, that turns 6.0 × 10⁸ calls to cube_to_direction into 98,304 of them, and the convolution goes from minutes to about four seconds.

The accumulator is double. A sun disc contributes single samples around 6,000 into a sum whose other terms are around 10⁻⁴. In float, once the running total is large enough, the small terms are lost entirely — the classic catastrophic accumulation failure. The symptom would be an irradiance map that is correct near the sun and quietly too dark away from it, which is very hard to attribute.

6.4 Importance sampling, and why not rand()

The prefilter and the BRDF table both need to sample the GGX distribution. Sampling uniformly over the hemisphere would be catastrophic: at roughness 0.10 the lobe is 0.37° wide, so all but roughly one sample in 100,000 lands where D is numerically zero.

GGX's inverse CDF is a closed form — one square root, no rejection and no iteration — and that is a large part of why GGX won:

\[ \cos t = \sqrt{\frac{1 - u_2}{1 + (\alpha^2 - 1) u_2}}, \qquad \phi = 2\pi u_1 \]

The pair (u₁, u₂) comes from hammersley, not from rand(), and the difference is a convergence rate. Random sampling converges as 1/√N; a low-discrepancy sequence, which fills the square evenly rather than randomly, converges close to 1/N on these smooth integrands. §G of the harness shows the table converged to 8 × 10⁻⁴ at 1,024 samples and 3.7 × 10⁻⁴ at 4,096 — where random sampling would want roughly sixteen times as many for the same error.

The y coordinate is the radical inverse in base 2: write the index in binary and reflect it about the binary point, so 1 → 0.1₂ = 0.5, 2 → 0.01₂ = 0.25, 3 → 0.11₂ = 0.75. Consecutive points keep landing in the largest remaining gap, which is the whole idea, and the standard five-step bit twiddle does the reflection without a loop.

6.5 The one honest fudge in the prefilter

§4.2 said the prefilter averages radiance weighted by (n·l). Where does that weight come from? Importance sampling GGX already accounts for D and the Jacobian, and G and Fresnel belong to the other bracket of the split sum — so putting them here would double-count. What is left is (n·l), and the original 2013 course notes document it as producing a visibly better result than uniform weighting rather than as a consequence of anything.

So it is fitted, not derived, and the code says so at the point where it is applied. That is the second fitted step in this lesson — the first being the linear roughness-to-level mapping — and both are marked in the source, which is the habit 6.14 established when it separated its exact lobe width from Tokuyoshi & Kaplanyan's fitted constants.

6.6 Putting it together, and one subtraction

image_based_light is the function that replaces base * ambient. Both halves live in it, because both halves are ambient: the irradiance map through Lambert's BRDF, and the split sum. They are added, and the metallic workflow divides the albedo between them exactly as f0_of and diffuse_albedo_of already do for the direct light — which is the check that this is the same shading model and not a second one bolted on.

Wiring it into the software rasterizer takes one subtraction that is easy to get wrong:

shade() has already added the constant ambient. We cannot ask it for the direct lighting without that term, so we remove what it added before adding the directional version. Forget the subtraction and every surface gets fill light twice, which reads as "IBL is too bright" and sends you to tune env_intensity — a fix that makes the picture plausible and the model wrong.

ambient_only() is a named function three lines below the line it mirrors, rather than the expression written out a second time here, because the subtraction is only honest if it is character-for-character the addition. Two copies of albedo * ambient in two files can drift; two lines in the same header cannot drift far unnoticed.

And note the null check. style.env == nullptr is the whole of "this scene has no environment" — the sixth time fill_style has made that bargain, after inv_w = 1 (3.2), lights (3.8), an unbound albedo (3.9), shadows (6.8) and cascades (6.9). It is also what keeps the reference render byte-identical: the fixture never sets it, so shade() is reached by exactly the path it has been reached by for twenty-four lessons.

7Cube maps in SDL_GPU

Three new things on the device side: a texture type the engine has never created, a number format the CPU has never had to write, and a shader resource type that is neither Texture2D nor Texture2DArray.

7.1 The texture

A cube map is SDL_GPU_TEXTURETYPE_CUBE with layer_count_or_depth = 6 — exactly six, which SDL requires. The six layers are the six faces, and SDL_GPUCubeMapFace is documented as "can be passed in as the layer field", so the upload loop is Lesson 6.9's cascade-array code with a loop bound of six:

What differs is the shader side. A TextureCube takes a float3 direction where a Texture2DArray takes a float2 and an index, and the hardware does the face selection, the divide by the major axis, and the part software cannot reproduce: filtering across face edges.

Our CPU cube_map::sample clamps at a face edge instead of stepping onto the neighbour, so it has a seam. We measured it, and measuring it took three attempts — which is a story worth §9.

7.2 The mip chain is uploaded, not generated

This is the opposite of what create_sampled(..., mips = true) does two functions up in the same file, and the difference is the whole point of the lesson. SDL_GenerateMipmapsForGPUTexture builds a chain by box-filtering — the right answer when the levels mean "this texture, smaller". These levels mean "this environment, blurred by a GGX lobe of roughness r", which no box filter produces. They are computed by prefilter_environment and shipped up level by level.

7.3 Half floats, because HDR data has no other way to get there

Here is something the engine has never needed until now. Everything HDR so far has been produced on the GPU — gpu_post_stack renders into a k_hdr_format target and reads it back through a sampler, and no half float ever crosses the CPU boundary. An environment map is the first HDR data computed on the CPU that has to be uploaded, and SDL_UploadToGPUTexture takes bytes in the texture's own format. There is no half type in C++20 and no SDL helper, so float_to_half is ours to write.

The format in one line: 1 sign bit, 5 exponent bits biased by 15, 10 mantissa bits. Largest finite value 65,504, and about three decimal digits of precision — relative precision, which is exactly the property gpu_post.hpp argued makes half floats right for light. The harness confirms it across five decades:

2⁻¹¹ = 4.883 × 10⁻⁴, and we measured 4.85 × 10⁻⁴. That is half an ulp, which is the best a correctly-rounding conversion can do, and it is the number that says the rounding is round-to-nearest-even rather than truncation. Truncation would bias every value downward by half a step on average, which over a whole environment map is a systematic darkening rather than noise.

Three cases in that function are not the common one, and each produces a visible artefact if skipped:

  • Overflow clamps to 65,504 rather than becoming infinity. An inf in an environment map propagates through the prefilter into a whole mip level of NaN. A sun stored at 65,504 instead of 100,000 is wrong by a stop and a half; an inf is wrong by everything.
  • NaN stays NaN. Turning it into the largest finite half would hide the bug that produced it.
  • Subnormals. Below 2⁻¹⁴ the exponent cannot go lower and the mantissa absorbs the shift. Getting this wrong rounds small values to zero, which is invisible until you tonemap at a low exposure and find the shadows have quantised.

7.4 Three bindings, one call

The scene shader gains three resources at fragment slots 3, 4 and 5: two TextureCube and one Texture2D. On the C++ side they join the existing albedo, normal-map and shadow bindings, and the array goes from three entries to six in the same call:

Slots 3 to 5 do not change within a frame, so binding them here rather than once at the top is redundant work — but the rule Lesson 6.8 discovered still applies: a partial SDL_BindGPUFragmentSamplers does not merge with an earlier one, it REPLACES the range it names. Slot 3 would be unbound the moment slot 0 changed. One call for the whole set is the only spelling that is correct, and it costs the same.

When there is no environment, the renderer binds its own 1×1 black cube and a 1×1 black table. That is the fourth instance of this class's stated pattern — hand the shader the identity element of the feature it is missing. White was the identity for the albedo's multiply, lavender for a basis change (6.7), 1.0 for a depth comparison (6.8), and black is the identity for the addition the ambient term performs. The fallback is bound whether or not ibl_intensity is zero, because HLSL does not elide a resource because a branch did not reach it, and an unbound sampler slot is undefined rather than empty.

7.5 The skybox needs no geometry

A skybox is traditionally a cube: thirty-six indices, scaled up around the camera, drawn with its faces inward. That works and it has two problems. It can be clipped — too big and it reaches past the far plane, too small and the near plane enters it. And it has twelve internal edges, which Lesson 6.12's fullscreen.vert already told us costs double-shaded 2×2 quads.

So we reuse the oversized-triangle trick and turn each pixel into a ray. A pixel at NDC (nx, ny) sees through

\[ \mathbf{forward} + n_x \, \mathbf{right} + n_y \, \mathbf{up} \]

provided right and up are pre-scaled by the image plane's half-extents at unit distance — tan(fovy/2) vertically and aspect × tan(fovy/2) horizontally. The CPU does that scaling once per frame, so the shader is three multiplies and two adds. It is deliberately not an inverse view-projection multiply, which would cost a 4×4 inverse on the CPU and a matrix-vector product plus a divide per pixel to compute the same three numbers.

Two lines in that shader are load-bearing:

The depth is exactly 1. Conventions §4 puts this engine's device depth at 0 on the near plane and 1 on the far one, so the sky belongs at 1, and position = float4(nx, ny, 1, 1) puts it there. The pipeline uses LESS_OR_EQUAL with depth writes off, and the OR_EQUAL is not optional: the depth buffer was cleared to 1, so a strict LESS would reject every sky pixel. That failure produces a completely black sky in front of correctly lit objects, which reads as "the cube map failed to load" and sends you looking in the wrong place entirely.

It uses SampleLevel, not Sample, and the reason is not the debug feature it also enables. Sample picks a mip level from the screen-space derivatives of the coordinate, which is exactly right for a surface texture and meaningless here: the derivative of a view ray across the screen says how fast the camera is turning, not how much sky a pixel covers.

And it does not tonemap. The skybox renders into the HDR target Lesson 6.12 built, so what belongs there is a quantity of light; tonemap.frag.hlsl converts the finished image once, over everything. Tonemapping the sky in its own shader has a very recognisable signature: the sky looks right on its own and every reflection of it comes out brighter than the sky it reflects, because reflections read the untouched cube map while the background reads a compressed copy.

7.6 A uniform that finally cost something

scene_light_uniforms gains two floats. ibl_intensity lands in the pad slot Lesson 6.8 added to fill its eleventh register, so it costs zero bytes — the third free ride in a row after 6.7 and 6.14. ibl_max_level does not fit, and takes the block from 176 to 192 bytes.

That is worth naming rather than absorbing, because three lessons of free slots could reasonably have set an expectation. The affordability argument is 6.8's, unchanged: this is a per-frame push, so one extra register is 16 bytes a frame, where the same register in material_uniforms would be 16 bytes per draw.

And it cannot be a constant in the shader. The chain's depth is chosen at run time by bake_environment, and the roughness-to-level mapping is a property of how the chain was built — so a hard-coded 5 in HLSL would be a number that must agree with a number in C++, in another language, with nothing checking it. That is precisely the failure mode the static_asserts in gpu_uniform.hpp exist to make impossible.

10Common pitfalls & debugging

Four of the five below happened during this lesson. Two of them produced plausible output, which is the kind worth the most words.

10.1 A channel-order mistake that looks like "IBL is too dark"

Symptom. The specular half of IBL vanishes at normal incidence and survives at grazing angles. Head-on a metal sphere is black; toward its silhouette a rim of reflection appears. The diffuse half is perfect.

Cause. make_brdf_lut packed scale and bias with a hand-written shift — (bias << 8) | scale — reasoning about an RGBA word. engine::texture stores ARGB, so red is bits 16–23 and scale went into blue.

Why it was hard. Green is bits 8–15 in both layouts, so bias read back correctly. And the shader computes F0 × scale + bias: with scale reading zero, the specular term becomes bias alone — which is ~0 at normal incidence and ~0.078 at grazing. So the picture was not obviously broken. It was dim in a way that invited tuning.

Fix. Use pack_argb from colour.hpp, which has had the right answer since Lesson 1.6. A packing spelled out at the call site is a packing that can disagree with the one the engine uses — and the same mistake appeared a second time, in the harness's ARGB→RGBA unpack for the GPU upload.

How to catch it in five seconds. Print the table. scale at (n·v = 0.9, roughness = 0.1) must be 1.000: a smooth surface seen head-on reflects all of F0 and nothing more. If it is 0, you have this bug.

10.2 A Monte Carlo estimate that has not converged

Symptom. The split sum appears to be 20% dark at roughness 0.25 — a number of the right order, with no sign of being wrong.

Cause. The reference was brute force at 8,192 importance samples, against an environment containing a 6000:1 sun disc. With a lobe a couple of degrees wide and a source smaller than that, the estimator's variance is enormous: whether a given run's samples hit the disc changes the answer by a factor of two. Measured, 8,192 against 1,000,000 disagree by 1.98×; with the sun removed, the same two counts agree to 0.040%.

The rule. Lesson 6.14 established one: check that a measurement can produce a non-null result before believing a null one. This is its mirror — check that a non-null result has converged before believing it — and both are the same rule underneath: establish what your instrument can see before you read it. The cheap test is to run at two sample counts and compare; if they differ, the smaller one is measuring its own noise.

10.3 A seam test that measures nothing

Symptom. The CPU sampler's cross-face seam measures 0.0000%. Twice.

First cause. The probe directions never crossed a face boundary, so the test compared a face with itself. Fix: assert the crossing before measuring the difference — the harness now reports "31 of 31 probe pairs land on different faces" as its own check.

Second cause, and the better one. They did cross, and the answer was still zero — because sky_radiance depends only on d.y. Along a vertical face edge our sky is literally constant, so the two clamped edge texels hold the same value and there is nothing for a seam to break.

A null result from an environment that cannot exhibit the defect is not evidence that the defect is absent. The harness now measures on a probe environment that varies with azimuth — the axis a vertical face edge actually separates — and finds the seam at 12.09% at 16×16, one texel's worth of the function, exactly as a clamp instead of a face step should cost. It then reports the sky's 1.06 × 10⁻⁷ alongside, because "this environment happens to be seamless" is itself worth knowing.

10.4 A build that cannot find a shader it just compiled

Symptom. Adding skybox.vert and rebuilding gives

Cause. engine_use_shaders made the copy-stamp depend on shader files produced by custom commands in the top-level directory, while the stamp target lives in demos/. The Makefile generator cannot resolve a cross-directory file dependency without a target-level edge as well.

Why nobody hit it for fourteen lessons, which is the nastiest part. Once a shader has been built once, the file exists — and make is perfectly happy to depend on an existing file it has no rule for. So every shader added before this one worked from its second build onward, and the bug sat waiting for the next person to add one. A build from a genuinely clean tree would have fired it on all twenty-three at once.

Fix. One line: add_dependencies(${target}_shaders ${shader_targets}). Depend on the shader targets, not only on their outputs.

10.5 The ambient term applied twice

Symptom. With the environment on, everything is washed out and the shadows have no contrast — and turning env_intensity down to about 0.6 makes it look fine.

Cause. shade() already added albedo × ambient and the IBL term was added on top. Fix: subtract it (§6.6). And notice how close this one comes to escaping — tuning the intensity produces an acceptable picture, so the bug survives as a "magic number" that will be wrong the first time anyone changes lighting::ambient.

10.6 Two more, for the record

  • A cube map bound to a Texture2D. The validation layer catches it; on a driver without one, the result is a black reflection and no error. Check the shader's declared type before anything else.
  • A repeat-addressed BRDF table. An interpolated normal on a silhouette can give a very slightly negative n·v, which under any address mode but CLAMP wraps to the far edge of the table and reads the roughest, most grazing entry there is — a bright rim on exactly the pixels where it is most visible. The shader saturates it and the sampler clamps, which is belt and braces on purpose.

11Exercises

  1. 11.1 — Sixteen bits for the table (guided). make_brdf_lut stores scale and bias as bytes, which the code calls its one real compromise. Measure it properly: for each of a grid of (n·v, roughness), compare the sampled table against integrate_brdf directly, and report the worst absolute error and the worst error as a fraction of the specular term it multiplies. Then say which of the two numbers an artist would care about, and why they are different.

    Hint. §G already measures the first at 0.01118. The second needs the prefiltered radiance it multiplies, which varies by three orders of magnitude across the sky. The answer is that a fixed absolute error on a multiplier is a relative error on the result, so it is invisible in a bright reflection and significant in a dark one.

  2. 11.2 — Bake the sky without its sun (guided). §4.3 found the split sum 73% dark with a sun disc in the environment, and noted that production renderers keep the sun as a separate analytic light. Do that: bake the environment with sun_radiance = {0,0,0}, set the engine's directional light to match the disc's total power, and compare the result against brute force over the full sky.

    Hint. The disc's irradiance is its radiance times its solid angle, 2π(1 − cos θ) — at 1° that is 9.57 × 10⁻⁴ sr. The comparison should improve dramatically for rough materials and barely move for smooth ones; explaining why is the exercise.

  3. 11.3 — Spherical harmonics for the diffuse half. An irradiance map is a cube of 6 × 32² = 6,144 texels representing a function with no detail finer than about 60°. That is wildly redundant. Nine spherical-harmonic coefficients (order 2, three channels) reproduce it to within a couple of per cent — Ramamoorthi & Hanrahan's result, in §12's reading. Implement the projection and the evaluation, and measure the error against irradiance_map texel for texel.

    Hint. Projection is one pass over the source cube accumulating Li(d) · Ylm(d) · dω — the same loop irradiance_map already has, with the cosine replaced by a basis function. Evaluation is a quadratic form in the normal. You should find 27 floats replacing 73 KB, and the bake time going from four seconds to a few milliseconds.

  4. 11.4 — Prefilter on the GPU (engine challenge). Five seconds is acceptable for a lesson and unacceptable for a loading screen. Move the prefilter onto the device: render each face of each level with a fragment shader that importance-samples the previous level, using the COLOR_TARGET usage create_cube does not currently request and SDL_GPUColorTargetInfo's layer_or_depth_plane and mip_level fields.

    Hint. Six faces × six levels is 36 render passes with a full-screen triangle each, which sounds like a lot and is a millisecond. Use the CPU result as the reference: the two should agree to within the sampler's filtering differences, and where they do not, the likely cause is the handedness note in §3.1.

  5. 11.5 — Multi-scattering compensation (engine challenge). §G measures the BRDF table summing to 0.3276 at roughness 1 — 67% of the energy is missing, because the Smith G models single scattering only. Implement Fdez-Agüera's single-term compensation (§12), and check two things: that the table's energy comes back to near 1, and that a rough white metal under a uniform environment now reflects close to the radiance it is standing in.

    Hint. The compensation is built from the same scale + bias you already have, so it costs no new table. Watch out that it must not push the table above 1 — a surface that reflects more than arrives is the failure Lesson 6.2 measured at 1.1386 and 6.4 fixed.

12Recap & next

The engine's ambient term is no longer a constant. It is an environment map — radiance as a function of direction, on the six faces of a cube — and the same texture is the sky behind the scene and the light falling on it, so a reflection cannot disagree with its surroundings.

What the engine gained:

  • Cube maps, CPU and GPU, with the face table written once as data and asserted against SDL's enum, and with the right-handed/left-handed mirror measured rather than assumed.
  • An irradiance map — the diffuse half, which is exact for Lambert and which reproduces Lesson 6.2's albedo × ambient to 1.3 × 10⁻⁵ on the environment 6.2 assumed.
  • A prefiltered environment chain and a BRDF table — the split sum, whose two approximations are named separately and whose error is measured, decomposed into the bake's contribution and the technique's, and shown to have a shape: 0.08% at roughness 0.10, 5.8% at near-normal incidence, 29.0% at grazing, 73.0% with a sun.
  • A derived roughness-to-level mapping, from 6.14's closed-form lobe width — and the finding that the folklore everybody ships is within 0.77 of a level of it.
  • Half floats, because HDR data has no other way onto the device.
  • A skybox with no geometry, on both renderers.

And three habits carried forward. The identity element pattern got its fourth instance (black, for an addition). The derived versus fitted boundary is marked in the source in two places. And 6.14's rule about null measurements acquired its mirror — check a result has converged before you believe it — which caught a 20% error that was entirely Monte Carlo noise.

What is still missing, stated plainly. One probe, infinitely far away: no parallax, no local reflections, no second room. No multi-scattering compensation, so two thirds of the energy is lost at full roughness. And the prefilter runs on the CPU in five seconds, which is a loading screen you would not ship. All three are exercises, and all three are what a production IBL system adds on top of exactly what we built.

Next — the renderer now draws everything in the scene every frame, including the objects behind the camera. Lesson 6.16 extracts the frustum's six planes, tests the AABBs Lesson 6.8 already computes against them, and feeds what survives into the instanced draws that have been sitting built and unused since 4.5.

13Further reading

  • Brian Karis, “Real Shading in Unreal Engine 4” (SIGGRAPH 2013 Physically Based Shading course notes). The source of the split-sum approximation, the (n·l) prefilter weighting, and the two-term BRDF table. Short, and the pages on what was tried and rejected are the valuable ones.
  • Sébastien Lagarde & Charles de Rousiers, “Moving Frostbite to PBR” (SIGGRAPH 2014). The most thorough published account of an IBL pipeline, including the n = v = r assumption's consequences and what Frostbite does about them. §4.9 is the section to read first.
  • Ravi Ramamoorthi & Pat Hanrahan, “An Efficient Representation for Irradiance Environment Maps” (SIGGRAPH 2001). Nine coefficients for the diffuse half, with the proof that the cosine convolution kills everything above order 2. Exercise 11.3.
  • Carmelo Fdez-Agüera, “A Multiple-Scattering Microfacet Model for Real-Time Image-Based Lighting” (JCGT 2019). The energy compensation Exercise 11.5 asks for, in a form that reuses the table you already have.
  • Real-Time Rendering, 4th edition, §10.3 (environment mapping) and §10.5 (irradiance environment mapping). The standard textbook treatment, and the place to read about the alternatives to a cube — octahedral maps in particular.
  • The OpenGL specification, §8.13 (“Cube Map Texture Selection”). The sc/tc/ma table of §3.1, normatively. Worth reading once so you know where the signs come from rather than copying them.
  • Preetham, Shirley & Smits, “A Practical Analytic Model for Daylight” (SIGGRAPH 1999), and Hosek & Wilkie's 2012 successor. Physically-based sky models, for when the gradient-and-a-disc of sky_settings stops being enough.
  • SDL3 wiki: SDL_GPUTextureCreateInfo, SDL_GPUCubeMapFace, SDL_GPUTextureRegion. The three pages behind §7.1, and the place to confirm that layer_count_or_depth must be exactly 6.
