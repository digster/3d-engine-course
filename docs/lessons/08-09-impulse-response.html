Impulse Response: Restitution and Friction

Five lessons of collision detection have built a machine that answers questions. 8.4 asks whether two boxes overlap; 8.5, how far apart two convex shapes are; 8.6, how deep they are inside each other and which way is out; 8.7, at which four points; 8.8, which pairs are worth asking about at all. Run the whole of it on a crate falling onto a floor and the crate falls through the floor, because nothing in engine::phys has ever written a velocity. This lesson writes velocities. The obvious way — a spring proportional to the overlap — is four lines long, and §1 gives it every chance and measures what it costs: 164 tuned settings, and the best of them lands the crate perfectly at 108 mm of penetration, or keeps it at 12.6 mm while it bounces 367 mm forever, and not both. A penalty contact is not broken; it is tuned, and the tuning belongs to the scene rather than to the material — the same spring under a 200 kg crate lets it 962 mm into a half-metre floor. The impulse formulation has no stiffness to be stable about, because it does not model a contact as a thing that lasts: a collision is an event, and the question is what single change in velocity leaves the surfaces moving the way a contact requires. That is one linear equation per contact in one scalar unknown, and the constant in it — the effective mass — is the whole of the lesson's arithmetic. We derive it, then rearrange it into a form that proves the division is safe, and check the two forms against each other over 200,000 random contacts. Restitution rides on the same equation as a change of target velocity, and brings the strangest bug in Module 8 with it: because gravity is applied before the solver looks, the approach speed is already too large by g·h, so a ball settles into a hop that never dies — 2.2 cm at e = 0.8, 58 cm at e = 0.95 — and §5 predicts the exact speed it converges to in closed form and measures it at 1.000×. Friction is a tangential impulse clipped to Coulomb's cone, and the square that every second engine uses instead of the disc is measured here at 42.7% direction-dependence, in a basis chosen from the floor's normal. Along the way: a slope test that disagrees with atan(μ) by four degrees and turns out to be measuring the fixture; a sphere that stops sliding at exactly 5/7 of the speed it landed with, whatever μ is, with the 0.06% it misses by predicted from the manifold's own contact-point convention; a solve order that, at one pass, has no friction at all; and an honest ending — one pass over four contact points does not hold a crate up. It sinks 275 mm in fifteen seconds. That is Lesson 8.10's reason to exist, arriving early enough to be measured rather than promised.

1. The problem: the spring you cannot afford

Here is the state of the engine at the end of Lesson 8.8. Drop a crate on a floor. The broadphase reports the pair; GJK says they are touching; EPA says how deep and which way out; the manifold gives four contact points with ids that survive across frames. Every one of those stages runs, correctly, at 60 Hz. And the crate goes straight through the floor, because not one line of engine::phys has ever assigned to a velocity. The whole subsystem could have been marked const.

So: something has to push. The response that occurs to everybody first — and it is a good instinct, because it reuses everything Lesson 8.2 built — is a spring. You know the overlap; push the bodies apart with a force proportional to it; hand that force to add_force_at and let F = ma do the rest. It is called a penalty method, it is four lines long, and the honest thing to do is to try it properly before rejecting it.

The stiffness is not a free parameter

Start with the easiest possible question: a crate of mass m resting on the floor, not moving. For it to rest, the spring must exactly carry its weight, so at a penetration x,

\[ k\,x = m g \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad k = \frac{mg}{x} \] k * x = m * g, so k = m * g / x

Choose how far you are willing to let things sink and the stiffness is decided for you. Now ask what that stiffness does to the simulation. A spring of stiffness k acting on a mass m oscillates at

\[ \omega = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}} = \sqrt{\frac{mg/x}{m}} = \sqrt{\frac{g}{x}} \] omega = sqrt(k/m) = sqrt(g/x)

The mass cancels. Read that again, because it is the whole argument: the rate at which a penalty contact oscillates does not depend on what is resting on it. You cannot buy stability by making the crate heavier, or by making it lighter. The penetration you are willing to see decides the rate, for every body in the scene, and Lesson 8.1's stability limit for semi-implicit Euler — h·ω < 2, derived there from a determinant and returned by max_stable_step — turns that rate into a minimum frame rate for the entire simulation.

Worked, with real numbers. A 20 kg crate, g = 9.81.
Allow 1 mm of sink: k = 20 × 9.81 / 0.001 = 196,200 N/m.
Then ω = √(9.81 / 0.001) = √9810 = 99.05 rad/s.
8.1's limit: h < 2/ω = 0.02019 s, which is 49.5 Hz.
Turn it round: at 60 Hz, ω < 2/h = 120 rad/s, so x > g/ω² = 9.81/14400 = 0.681 mm.
At 60 Hz, the finest a stable penalty spring can hold anything is 0.68 mm.

And “stable” is not “right”. 8.1 §6 measured an orbit at h·ω = 1.95 that was perfectly bounded and swung by a factor of forty. Sitting at the edge of the stability limit buys you a contact that does not explode and does visibly throb.

And the static number was the optimistic one

Everything above was about a crate that is already at rest. A crate that is arriving is a different question, and the spring has no say in it at all. All of the impact's kinetic energy has to go into the spring before the crate stops, so

\[ \tfrac12 m v^2 = \tfrac12 k x^2 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad x = v\sqrt{\frac{m}{k}} \] half m v squared = half k x squared, so x = v * sqrt(m/k)

There is no g in that and the mass does not cancel. The dynamic penetration is set by the impact speed and the weight, and the spring you tuned to hold a resting crate at 1 mm has no opinion about it.

Worked. Our crate falls 20 cm onto the floor, so it arrives at v = √(2 × 9.81 × 0.2) = 1.981 m/s.
With k = 196,200: x = 1.981 × √(20/196200) = 1.981 × 0.010096 = 20.0 mm.
Twenty times the millimetre it was tuned for, before any discretisation. The harness measures 44.1 mm, because a 60 Hz step overshoots the turning point.
To get 1 mm out of that impact you would need k = m v²/x² = 20 × 3.924 / 10⁻⁶ = 7.85 × 10⁷ N/m, which oscillates at ω = 1981 rad/s and demands at least 990 Hz.

Giving it its best shot

That is the argument as it is usually made, and it is not quite fair, because we have not turned the other knob. An undamped spring gives back every joule it took, so a penalty contact with no damping is a contact with e = 1: the crate returns to its drop height and keeps doing so. Damping is what turns a bounce into a landing. And a softer spring is calmer and sinks further, so there are two knobs and they trade against each other.

So §A of the harness sweeps both: four stiffnesses, forty-one dampings each, three seconds of simulation apiece, 164 settings, and reports the best result for each stiffness. The right-hand table of Figure 1 is what came back, and the section was drafted expecting to show that no setting works. It does not show that.

The part that actually kills it

And then the measurement that settles the argument, which is not about any of the numbers above. Take the best setting found — the one tuned on a 20 kg crate dropped from 1.2 m — and apply it, unchanged, to scenes it was not tuned on: 

  scene                        penalty depth penalty swing impulse depth
  20 kg from 1.2 m (tuned)         108.60 mm      0.000 mm     12.554 mm
  200 kg from 1.2 m                962.13 mm    152.444 mm     12.554 mm
  2 kg from 1.2 m                   20.43 mm    314.988 mm     12.554 mm
  20 kg from 5 m                   369.54 mm      0.289 mm     46.627 mm

The 200 kg crate goes 962 mm into a floor half a metre thick: it is through, and out the other side. The 2 kg crate does not sink but bounces 315 mm forever. The same crate dropped from 5 m instead of 1.2 m goes 370 mm in. Meanwhile the impulse column does not move at all except where the physics says it should — the 5 m drop arrives faster, so it travels further in the step before anything notices, which is a statement about the time step rather than about the solver, and §10 comes back to it.

That is the case against penalty contacts, and it is not “they are unstable”. They are tunable, and the tuning is a property of the scene — of the masses in it and the speeds they arrive at — rather than of the materials in contact. A physics engine is supposed to take a designer's scene and simulate it; one whose contacts must be re-tuned every time somebody changes a crate's weight is not an engine, it is a rehearsal.

Penalty methods are not a historical curiosity, and this section is not a hit piece. Soft-body and cloth solvers use them constantly, because a cloth is a mesh of springs and the stiffness is the material rather than a tuning constant. Position-based dynamics — the family behind most cloth in shipping games — can be read as a penalty method moved into position space to dodge exactly the stability limit above. What they are bad at is rigid contact, where the “spring” has no physical stiffness to be and the correct answer is infinity.

2. Building intuition: a collision is an event, not an interval

The penalty method's mistake is not the spring. It is a modelling assumption so quiet that nobody states it: that the contact is a thing that lasts, with a force acting throughout. Ask what force acts during a collision and you are already committed to the contact having a duration for the force to act over, and to your simulation resolving that duration.

Look at what a real collision does. A steel ball bounces off a steel plate in something like a hundred microseconds. At 60 Hz your step is 16,667 microseconds — one hundred and sixty times longer. Whatever happened inside that collision, your simulation was not present for it. You do not know the force, you cannot resolve the force, and you do not need the force. What you can observe is the outcome: the ball arrived going one way and left going another.

So stop asking about force. Ask instead: what single, instantaneous change in velocity leaves the two surfaces moving the way a contact requires? That quantity — a change in momentum with no duration attached — is an impulse, and Lesson 8.2 §6 already argued the general case for it. A force needs a step size to become a velocity change, so the same physical event written as a one-step force produces different results at 30 Hz and 144 Hz; 8.2 measured a jump spanning 3.85 m at 30 Hz and 16.7 cm at 144, a factor of 23. Written as an impulse it has no h in it at all:

\[ \Delta \mathbf{v} = \frac{\mathbf{J}}{m}, \qquad \Delta \boldsymbol{\omega} = \mathbf{I}^{-1}(\mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{J}) \] delta v = J / m, and delta omega = inverse_inertia * (r cross J)

Both of those are already in the engine: add_impulse_at has been in rigid_body.hpp since 8.3, and its doc comment has said for two lessons that “everything Lesson 8.9 adds is deciding what J should be”. That is now literally the task.

Where the force went. If it bothers you that a force has vanished from a Newtonian simulation, it has not: an impulse is the integral of a force over the time it acts, J = ∫F dt. The impulse formulation keeps that integral and throws away the integrand, on the grounds that the integrand was never observable at this time step and the integral is. It is the same move as replacing a rendered motion-blur streak with a single sample — except that here the integral is exactly what the dynamics needs, so nothing is approximated at all.

3. Three demands on one velocity

A contact happens at a point — one of the four that 8.7's manifold produced — and the velocity that matters there is not either body's centre-of-mass velocity. It is the velocity of the material points that are touching, which for a rigid body is v + ω × r: the formula point_velocity has implemented since 8.2, and the one that makes a rigid body rigid.

Define the relative velocity at the contact, of body b's point with respect to body a's:

\[ \mathbf{u} = \big(\mathbf{v}_b + \boldsymbol{\omega}_b \times \mathbf{r}_b\big) - \big(\mathbf{v}_a + \boldsymbol{\omega}_a \times \mathbf{r}_a\big) \] u = (v_b + omega_b cross r_b) - (v_a + omega_a cross r_a)

with r_a and r_b the lever arms from each centre of mass to the contact point. The manifold's normal n points from a toward b — collide.hpp's convention, unchanged since 8.4 — so u·n is negative when the two are approaching, positive when separating, and zero at a resting contact. Get that sign backwards and every contact in the scene sucks instead of pushing, which is a memorable ten minutes.

Now: everything a contact wants is a statement about u, and there are exactly three of them.

Non-penetration

The surfaces may separate and may slide past each other; they may not approach. So after the impulse, u·n ≥ 0. And a contact can only push: a floor does not reach up and grab a crate that is leaving. That makes the impulse along the normal non-negative, j ≥ 0, and that clamp is the only nonlinearity in the entire normal solve. Nothing else in this lesson has an inequality in it.

Restitution

A bouncy contact leaves at some fraction of the speed it arrived with. Newton's experimental law, which is a definition dressed as a law: the coefficient of restitution e is the ratio of separation speed to approach speed, so we want u·n = −e·(u·n)before.

Notice what kind of demand that is. It is the same shape as non-penetration — “make u·n equal to this number” — with a different number on the right. So restitution needs no second equation and no second impulse; it changes the target from zero to −e·(approach) and the same solve delivers it. That is why e will not appear anywhere in the solver code below: it appears once, when the target is computed, and never again.

Friction

The third demand lives in the plane perpendicular to n. “Not sliding” means the tangential part of u is zero, so the natural demand is to drive it there — but only as far as the surfaces can manage. Coulomb's condition, which is four centuries old and still the model every engine ships, says the tangential impulse cannot exceed a fixed multiple of the normal one:

\[ \|\mathbf{j}_t\| \;\le\; \mu\, j_n \] the magnitude of the tangential impulse is at most mu times the normal impulse

j_t is a two-dimensional vector in the contact plane, so the admissible set is a disc of radius μ·j_n. Stack that disc over the normal axis — the radius grows in proportion to the height — and you have swept out a cone, which is where the phrase friction cone comes from. §6 is about what happens when you use a square instead.

And notice the coupling, because it is the reason the two solves cannot be independent: the cone's radius is μ times the answer to the normal demand. Friction cannot be computed in parallel with the normal impulse, because until the normal impulse exists there is no cone to clip against. §9 measures what happens when you get that order wrong, and the answer is worse than “slightly less friction”.

Why each is one equation in one unknown

Three demands, and each turns out to need one scalar. The reason is linearity, and it is worth seeing explicitly before we do any algebra.

Apply an impulse J = j·d along some direction d at the contact. Each body's linear velocity changes by ±j·d/m and each angular velocity by ±j·I⁻¹(r × d) — both proportional to j, because every step from impulse to velocity is a linear map. So the change in u·d is proportional to j too:

\[ \Delta(\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{d}) \;=\; j\,k_{\mathbf{d}} \] the change in u along d equals j times a constant k that depends only on d

for some number k_d that depends on the masses, the inertia tensors, the lever arms and the direction — but not on j. Which means “how big an impulse do I need to change this velocity by that much?” is a division, not a solve. The whole of the next section is working out what that number is.

4. The effective mass

We want the number k_d from the end of the last section: apply an impulse J = j·d at the contact, and u·d changes by j·k_d. Let us just do it.

By Newton's third law the impulse acts on b as +J and on a as −J — one impulse, two bodies, opposite signs. Each body's velocities change by the two formulas from §2:

\[ \begin{aligned} \Delta \mathbf{v}_b &= +j\,\frac{\mathbf{d}}{m_b}, &\qquad \Delta \boldsymbol{\omega}_b &= +j\,\mathbf{I}_b^{-1}(\mathbf{r}_b \times \mathbf{d}) \\ \Delta \mathbf{v}_a &= -j\,\frac{\mathbf{d}}{m_a}, &\qquad \Delta \boldsymbol{\omega}_a &= -j\,\mathbf{I}_a^{-1}(\mathbf{r}_a \times \mathbf{d}) \end{aligned} \] b gains j*d/m_b of velocity and j * inv_I_b * (r_b cross d) of angular velocity; a loses the same with its own mass and tensor

Substitute into the definition of u and take the component along d. The j factors out of every term, leaving

\[ k_{\mathbf{d}} = \frac{1}{m_a} + \frac{1}{m_b} + \mathbf{d}\cdot\Big[\big(\mathbf{I}_a^{-1}(\mathbf{r}_a\times\mathbf{d})\big)\times\mathbf{r}_a\Big] + \mathbf{d}\cdot\Big[\big(\mathbf{I}_b^{-1}(\mathbf{r}_b\times\mathbf{d})\big)\times\mathbf{r}_b\Big] \] k = 1/m_a + 1/m_b + dot(d, cross(inv_I_a * cross(r_a, d), r_a)) + dot(d, cross(inv_I_b * cross(r_b, d), r_b))

That is correct and it is not the form to implement. Look at one angular term and apply the scalar triple product identity a·(b × c) = b·(c × a), with w = I⁻¹(r × d):

\[ \mathbf{d}\cdot(\mathbf{w}\times\mathbf{r}) \;=\; \mathbf{w}\cdot(\mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{d}) \;=\; \big(\mathbf{I}^{-1}(\mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{d})\big)\cdot(\mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{d}) \] dot(d, cross(w, r)) = dot(w, cross(r, d)) = dot(inv_I * (r cross d), (r cross d))

Write w = r × d and the effective mass becomes

\[ k = \frac{1}{m_a} + \frac{1}{m_b} + \mathbf{w}_a\cdot\big(\mathbf{I}_a^{-1}\mathbf{w}_a\big) + \mathbf{w}_b\cdot\big(\mathbf{I}_b^{-1}\mathbf{w}_b\big) \] k = 1/m_a + 1/m_b + dot(w_a, inv_I_a * w_a) + dot(w_b, inv_I_b * w_b)

Why the rearrangement is worth doing. Not to save a cross product — it saves one, which is nothing. It is worth doing because the two angular terms are now each of the form w·(M w) with M an inverse inertia tensor, and an inertia tensor is positive semi-definite — it is a sum of terms m(|r|²𝟙 − r⊗r), and 8.3 built it that way. A positive semi-definite matrix has w·(Mw) ≥ 0 for every w, by definition. So:

\[ k \;\ge\; \frac{1}{m_a} + \frac{1}{m_b} \;\ge\; 0 \] k is never smaller than the sum of the two inverse masses, and never negative

which means the division by k is safe, always, with one exception we can name exactly: k is zero precisely when both inverse masses are zero, which is two immovable bodies overlapping. That is a level-design bug and the right response is to apply no impulse, which falls out of returning zero rather than an infinity, with no branch. The same reciprocal-mass storage 8.2 §3 argued for, doing its job again.

Measured. The harness evaluates both algebraic forms on 200,000 random contacts between freely oriented boxes of 0.2 to 400 kg, in two independently written code paths. Worst relative disagreement: 5.016 × 10⁻⁷. Worst value of k − (1/m_a + 1/m_b) over the same 200,000: exactly +0.000 × 10⁰ — it never once went negative, which a sign error in either cross product would have caused, and which the agreement between the two forms could not have caught, because both forms would have had it.

What it means

1/k has units of mass and behaves like one, so the code calls it the effective mass: the mass the contact appears to have when pushed along d. It is not the body's mass, and the difference is the entire content of the angular terms.

Two limits make it concrete, and both are measured as controls.

A contact straight through both centres of mass. Then r is parallel to d, so r × d = 0, so both angular terms vanish exactly and k = 1/m_a + 1/m_b. Its reciprocal is m_a·m_b/(m_a + m_b) — the reduced mass of elementary mechanics, which is the whole of what a one-dimensional collision needs. For 3 kg against 7 kg the harness measures 2.100000 kg against an analytic 2.1.

An off-centre contact is lighter. Push a 10 kg plank in the middle and the contact sees 10 kg. Push it 1.9 m out and part of the impulse turns the plank instead of lifting it, so less of it arrives where the contact is: the harness measures 2.702 kg, 3.70× lighter. If you ever find an implementation reporting the same effective mass at the centre and at the end, the angular terms are silently zero — which is a common bug and one that leaves momentum perfectly conserved, so it hides from the obvious test.

5. The normal impulse, by hand

Now the solve, which is two lines. We want the relative normal velocity after the impulse to equal some target u*. It is currently u_n = u·n, and applying j along n changes it by j·k_n. So

\[ u_n + j\,k_n = u^{*} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad j = \frac{u^{*} - u_n}{k_n} = (u^{*} - u_n)\,m_{\text{eff}} \] j = (target - current normal velocity) / k, which is (target - u_n) times the effective mass

and then clamp the total to be non-negative, because a contact may only push. That is the whole normal solve. For a resting contact u* = 0; for a bouncing one u* = −e·(approach), which is the next section.

Worked, all the way through, with a case that has a closed form. A 3 kg sphere travelling at +4 m/s strikes a 7 kg sphere travelling at −1 m/s, head on along x, perfectly elastically (e = 1). The contact is through both centres, so there are no angular terms.

k = 1/3 + 1/7 = 0.333333 + 0.142857 = 0.476190, so m_eff = 1/k = 2.100000 kg.
u_n = v_b − v_a = (−1) − (+4) = −5 m/s (negative: approaching, correct).
Target: u* = −e·u_n = −1 × (−5) = +5 m/s.
j = (5 − (−5)) × 2.1 = 10 × 2.1 = 21.0 N·s.

Apply it: v_a → 4 − 21/3 = 4 − 7 = −3 m/s, v_b → −1 + 21/7 = −1 + 3 = +2 m/s.
Check against the textbook elastic formula v₁′ = ((m₁−m₂)v₁ + 2m₂v₂)/(m₁+m₂) = ((−4)(4) + 14(−1))/10 = −3.0. ✓
Check the separation speed: 2 − (−3) = 5 m/s, which is e times the approach speed of 5. ✓
Check kinetic energy: before ½(3·16 + 7·1) = 27.5 J; after ½(3·9 + 7·4) = ½(27 + 28) = 27.5 J. ✓

Two conservation checks, and only one of them means anything

It is traditional to report that a contact solver conserves momentum. Ours does, to 3.81 × 10⁻⁷ relative. That number is worthless as evidence, and it is worth being explicit about why, because a reader who takes it as reassurance has been misled by a tautology.

Momentum is conserved because apply_impulse_pair adds the same J to one body and subtracts it from the other. The total change in mv is J − J, which is zero in exact arithmetic and zero to the last bit in floating point, because it is literally the same value used twice. It would come out at 10⁻⁷ if every number in the impulse were wrong.

Energy is the check. Nothing in the solver enforces it: there is no energy term anywhere in the code. So “an elastic collision conserved kinetic energy to 1.21 × 10⁻⁷” is a real constraint on the effective mass, the lever arms and the target velocity all at once. The harness's control makes the distinction stark — halve the effective mass on purpose, which is the single most plausible mistake in this file (forget one body's inverse mass) and see what each instrument says: 

  CONTROL, effective mass halved on purpose:
    momentum relative change 1.91e-07   <- still perfect
    energy   relative change 9.55e-01   <- caught

A detector problem, found while building that fixture. The obvious way to write the test above is to run two real spheres through collide_manifold and let the whole 8.5–8.7 chain produce the contact. It does not work, and the reason is not in this lesson's code: the manifold normal for two spheres comes back 4.2602° off the line of centres, and the error is constant across four decades of overlap, so it is not convergence noise. It is epa_config::max_iterations — 8.6 set it to 32 on the evidence of a worst case of 21 expansions over 200,000 pairs of boxes, and a sphere has no flat face for EPA to terminate on. At 64 iterations the error drops to 1.06°. 8.7 hides it whenever one shape is a box, because the manifold takes the reference face's normal rather than EPA's — which is why a ball on a floor is bit-exact and two balls are four degrees out. This lesson does not change epa.hpp: it is 8.6's knob, the fix is one number, and changing it needs 8.6's own 200,000-pair measurement rerun to say what it costs. What §5 does instead is take the detector out of the loop, which is exactly why build_manifold takes the normal and depth as arguments.

6. Restitution, and the gravity already in the velocity

Restitution needed no new equation — only a target. So this section is not about the arithmetic. It is about which velocity you apply it to, which turns out to be the most surprising thing in Module 8.

Here is the artifact. Drop a ball with e = 0.8 and let it bounce for thirty seconds. The closed form is immediate: each bounce keeps e of the speed, energy goes as speed squared, height goes as energy, so the n-th peak is e²ⁿ·h₀ and the ball is at rest in finite time with infinitely many bounces (Zeno's ball, and a nice thing to notice). What actually happens:

The uncorrected run climbs away from its own prediction and flattens out. By the tenth bounce it is at 3.918× the closed form, and it never comes down: over thirty seconds its last bounce still rebounds at 0.654 m/s. The ball hops, forever, about 2.2 centimetres high.

Where it comes from

Look at the order of operations in a semi-implicit Euler step — 8.1's settled default, and the order body_world::step has used since 8.2: 

  velocity += acceleration * h;     <- gravity applied HERE
  ... contacts solved ...           <- the solver looks HERE
  position += velocity * h;

By the time the solver reads the relative velocity, this step's gravity is already in it. A ball that truly arrived at the floor with speed v presents to the solver as arriving with v + g·h, and at 60 Hz that is an extra 16.35 cm/s. Restitution faithfully returns e times a speed that is too big.

That would be a rounding-scale nuisance if the error shrank with the bounce. It does not: the error is additive and the bounce is multiplicative, so as the ball slows down the extra g·h becomes a larger and larger fraction of what it has. The bounce has a fixed point — a speed at which the energy gained per bounce exactly balances the energy lost:

\[ v = e\,(v + g h) \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad v_{\infty} = \frac{e\,g h}{1 - e} \] v = e*(v + g*h) solves to v = e*g*h/(1 - e)

Worked, and then measured. At 60 Hz, g·h = 9.81/60 = 0.1635 m/s.
e = 0.50: v∞ = 0.5 × 0.1635 / 0.5 = 0.1635 m/s → a hop of v²/2g = 1.36 mm.
e = 0.80: v∞ = 0.8 × 0.1635 / 0.2 = 0.6540 m/s → 21.8 mm.
e = 0.95: v∞ = 0.95 × 0.1635 / 0.05 = 3.1065 m/s → 492 mm. Half a metre, forever.

Measured terminal rebound speeds, taken directly from the step on which the velocity flipped so that no peak has to be interpolated: 

       e      v predicted       v measured     hop height      ratio
    0.50        0.1635 m/s        0.1635 m/s        1.36 mm      1.000
    0.80        0.6540 m/s        0.6540 m/s       21.80 mm      1.000
    0.95        3.1065 m/s        3.3759 m/s      580.87 mm      1.087

Exact at the first two. The 0.95 row is 8.7% high because thirty seconds was not long enough for it to converge — the approach to the fixed point is geometric with ratio e, and 0.95 is slow.

Fixing it exactly

The cure is to apply restitution to the velocity the contact arrived with rather than the one it has when the solver looks. solver_config::restitution_bias is where the caller declares the velocity this step's external acceleration added — pass gravity * h — and the arrival speed is the observed approach minus that bias.

There is a trap in implementing it, and the harness has a control for exactly that trap. The first thing one writes is “subtract g·h from the approach speed”. That is wrong whenever both bodies are dynamic: gravity gave both of them the same velocity, so the relative velocity it produced is exactly zero and there is nothing to correct. The right expression is the difference of the two bodies' biases: 

const float bias_n = dot(step_bias_of(b, cfg.restitution_bias)
                             - step_bias_of(a, cfg.restitution_bias),
                         batch.normal);

with step_bias_of returning zero for anything that is not dynamic and scaling by the body's own gravity_scale. The control: two dynamic balls meeting in free fall, separation speed 3.066632748 m/s with the correction and 3.066632748 m/s without — identical, as they must be. The naive version would have been out by the full 16 cm/s.

And the threshold, which is the same fix made approximately

Every physics engine ships a restitution threshold: below some approach speed, usually 1 m/s, restitution is switched off. The reason given is always the same — without it, a resting body with any e > 0 buzzes on the floor forever, because gravity gives it a few millimetres per second of approach each step and the solver dutifully gives some back.

Which should sound familiar, because it is the same artifact. A resting body's approach speed is this step's g·h. Subtracting the bias makes the arrival speed exactly zero, and zero is below any threshold. So the harness measures them against each other: 

       e    threshold       bias      peak |vy|           buzz
    0.60      0.00 m/s        off     0.23976 m/s      6.9259 mm
    0.60      1.00 m/s        off     0.00000 m/s      0.0000 mm
    0.60      0.00 m/s         on     0.00000 m/s      0.0000 mm
    0.60      1.00 m/s         on     0.00000 m/s      0.0000 mm

The bias alone does the threshold's whole job, with no magic number in it. The threshold is the approximate version of the same correction, and it was invented first.

That is not an argument for deleting it, and the harness says why. The bias cancels the acceleration the caller declared. Anything else pressing the two bodies together in the same step — a thruster, a spring, a conveyor, another crate in a stack — produces an approach speed the solver has no way to know about. Put a 300 N downward thruster on the same crate, leave the bias set to g·h, and the buzz comes back at 9.9 mm with the threshold off and vanishes with it on. Keep both: the bias because it is exact where it applies, the threshold because it covers everywhere else — and a stack of crates is everywhere else.

While we are being honest about e: it is not a constant of a material, which every table of coefficients quietly omits. A real coefficient of restitution falls as the impact speed rises, because more of the energy goes into permanently deforming the thing — a steel ball is nearly elastic at 0.1 m/s and visibly not at 10. Games use one number per material because the error is invisible next to the error in everything else. This is the ninety-per-cent picture; the missing ten per cent is speed-dependent restitution, and Stronge's Impact Mechanics is where to go for it.

7. Friction: the cone and the square

Friction is the same solve twice, in two directions perpendicular to the normal, with a clip on the end. The arithmetic is short; the interesting part is the clip.

First we need the two directions. There is no canonical answer — vec3.hpp has said since Lesson 1.7 that in three dimensions “perpendicular to this” names a whole circle of directions — so any orthonormal pair spanning the contact plane will do. The standard construction picks whichever coordinate axis the normal is least aligned with and crosses with that: 

const vec3 seed = (std::abs(n.x) <= std::abs(n.y) && std::abs(n.x) <= std::abs(n.z))
                      ? vec3{1.0f, 0.0f, 0.0f}
                      : (std::abs(n.y) <= std::abs(n.z) ? vec3{0.0f, 1.0f, 0.0f}
                                                        : vec3{0.0f, 0.0f, 1.0f});
t1 = normalised(cross(n, seed));
t2 = cross(n, t1);

The “least aligned” part is the whole trick. A cross product of two nearly parallel vectors is nearly zero, and normalising a nearly-zero vector amplifies whatever rounding error it carries, so the construction must never take one. Choosing the smallest component guarantees the seed is at least 54.7° away — the worst case being a normal pointing along a cube's diagonal, where all three components are equal — so the cross product never has length below sin(54.7°) = 0.816. And t2 needs no sqrt at all: the cross product of two perpendicular unit vectors is already unit. Over a million random normals the harness measures the worst |dot| among the three pairs at 6.573 × 10⁻⁸ and the worst |length − 1| at 2.384 × 10⁻⁷, without that second normalisation.

The clip

Solve each tangent exactly as the normal was solved, but with target zero — “stop sliding” — and then enforce Coulomb. Both tangential components are computed from the same sample of the relative velocity rather than one after the other, because solving them sequentially would make the answer depend on which of the two arbitrary axes came first.

Then the clip, and there are two ways to write it:

\[ \text{cone:}\quad \|\mathbf{j}_t\| \le \mu j_n \qquad\qquad \text{box:}\quad |j_{t_1}| \le \mu j_n \;\wedge\; |j_{t_2}| \le \mu j_n \] cone: the length of the tangential impulse is at most mu*j_n. box: each component separately is at most mu*j_n.

The first is a disc. The second is the square that circumscribes that disc, so it permits up to √2 · μ·j_n of friction — but only along the diagonals of a basis that tangent_basis picked from the normal's smallest component. The box model is one clamp per axis instead of a sqrt, which is why it is common; what it buys is a physical anisotropy with no physical cause.

§F of the harness measures it the honest way: push a 5 kg crate along a flat floor at 4 m/s in 361 different directions and record how far it slides. Under the cone the answer cannot depend on the direction, because a disc is round. Under the box it must, because a square is not. And note that sliding at angle θ against a fixed basis is the same experiment as sliding along a fixed direction with the basis rotated by −θ, so this sweep is the measurement of basis dependence. 

       theta        box model       cone model
       0.0 deg         1.5977 m         1.5977 m
      22.5 deg         1.3801 m         1.5977 m
      45.0 deg         1.1202 m         1.5977 m
      67.5 deg         1.3800 m         1.5977 m
      90.0 deg         1.5977 m         1.5977 m

  over 361 directions:
    box  : 1.1202 m to 1.5983 m   spread 42.68%
    cone : 1.5977 m to 1.5977 m   spread 0.00%

42.68% direction-dependence against 0.00%. And the extremes are where the geometry says: the ratio of shortest to longest stopping distance comes out 0.70085 against a predicted 1/√2 = 0.70711. A puck slid north travels 43% further than one slid north-east, and which way is “north” was decided by the floor's normal.

The two controls are what make that mean something. With μ = 0 the crate travels 80.1 m and is still going, so the sweep is measuring friction rather than some settling artifact; and with μ = 2.0, where the crate grips and the clip never binds, the two models end 2.6 × 10⁻⁷ m apart — so the box model is not wrong in some other way, it is wrong in exactly this way.

§13 measures the price of the cone's sqrt: 6.8% of a manifold's prepare-and-solve. Six point eight per cent to remove a 43% anisotropy is not a close decision, and the default is the cone.

The cone clip here scales both components by one factor, which is the projection onto the disc in impulse space. The mathematically exact projection would be onto an ellipse, because the two tangent effective masses differ whenever the lever arm is anisotropic. Every engine does it this way; the error makes the friction slightly wrong in direction rather than wrong in magnitude, and it disappears entirely on the contacts where it would matter most — a flat face on a flat floor, where the two tangents are symmetric. This is the ninety-per-cent picture; the missing ten per cent lives in the literature under “anisotropic friction”.

8. The slope, and what μ actually means

μ is a ratio of impulses, which is not a thing anyone has intuition about. Here is the thing it really says, and it is the only statement about friction that contains no mass, no area and no speed: a body sits still on a slope up to atan(μ) and slides past it.

The derivation is one line of statics. On a slope of angle θ, gravity resolves into mg·sin θ down the slope and mg·cos θ into it. The normal force carries the second, so friction has at most μ·mg·cos θ to offer against the first:

\[ mg\sin\theta \;\le\; \mu\, mg\cos\theta \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \tan\theta \le \mu \] m g sin(theta) is at most mu m g cos(theta), so tan(theta) is at most mu

The mass cancels — a heavy crate and a light one let go at the same angle, which is counter-intuitive until you notice that friction and weight scale together.

So the test writes itself: bisect the angle at which the engine's crate starts to drift, and compare with atan(μ). Here is what came back the first time: 

        mu       atan(mu)       cube (1:1)
      0.20    11.3099 deg     11.3063 deg
      0.35    19.2900 deg     19.2494 deg
      0.50    26.5651 deg     26.1909 deg
      0.75    36.8699 deg     35.7921 deg
      1.00    45.0000 deg     41.2495 deg

Four thousandths of a degree out at μ = 0.2, and 3.75 degrees out at μ = 1.0. That is not noise and it is not a tolerance: it is a systematic error that grows with the parameter, and that shape is the clue. A wrong constant somewhere in the friction code would have been wrong at μ = 0.2 too.

The fixture was a cube, and a block on a slope has two critical angles. It slides when tan θ > μ, and it tips when its centre of mass passes outside its base — which is tan θ > w/h. It does whichever happens first. A cube has w/h = 1, so it tips at exactly 45°, and μ = 1 wants to slide at exactly 45°: the two limits collide, and above about μ = 0.5 the measurement stops being about friction at all.

Rerun with a slab whose width is ten-thirds of its height, which tips at 73.3° and therefore stays out of the way, and the column comes back:

A fixture that cannot express the quantity is not evidence about it. Combining two materials, and a default this course does not inherit

One loose end from §3. μ is a property of a pair of surfaces: rubber on rubber is 1.16, ice on ice is about 0.10, and rubber on ice is 0.15, which no function of 1.16 and 0.10 reproduces — because there is no such function. Every engine stores one number per body and combines them anyway, because a pair table is O(n²) in materials and nobody authors one. The combination is a modelling choice, and this course puts it in the call (combine_material) rather than burying it in rigid_body, so that a reader can see a choice was made.

No simulation can settle which rule is right. What can be checked is arithmetic, against the surface pairs whose three coefficients — a on a, b on b, and a on b — are all published:

A designer raising rubber's friction sees nothing at all change against ice. The geometric mean responds to both inputs, which is a usability argument rather than a physical one — and it is a real argument. The knob is in the call; §8's table is why the default points where it does.

9. Sliding becomes rolling

One more consequence of friction, and the prettiest closed form in the lesson. Drop a ball onto the floor with forward speed and no spin — a bowling ball, before it starts rolling.

Friction acts at the contact point, which is below the centre of mass. So it does two things at once: it slows the centre down, at μg, and it spins the ball up, because r × J is not zero. The surface speed ω·r climbs from zero while the centre speed falls. When they meet, the contact point is instantaneously stationary — that is exactly what “rolling without slipping” means — and friction has nothing left to act on.

Where do they meet? For a body with I = c·m·r², friction's torque gives it angular acceleration μmg·r/(c m r²) = μg/(c r), so the surface speed climbs at μg/c. The gap v − ω r therefore closes at μg(1 + 1/c), and setting v₀ − μgt = (μg/c)t gives

\[ t_{\text{roll}} = \frac{v_0}{\mu g\,(1 + 1/c)}, \qquad v_{\text{roll}} = \frac{v_0}{1 + c} \] the transition takes v0 / (mu*g*(1 + 1/c)) and ends at v0/(1+c)

μ cancels out of the second one. The speed a sliding ball ends up rolling at does not depend on how rough the floor is — friction decides only how long the transition takes. For a solid sphere c = 2/5, so v_roll = v₀/(7/5) = 5/7 · v₀; for a hollow shell c = 2/3 and it is 3/5.

Measured, and the controls are the point. A 2 kg ball landing at 6 m/s: 

  body             mu   v/v0 exact     v/v0 sim      t exact        t sim
  solid ball     0.20     0.714286     0.713877     0.8737 s     0.8833 s
  solid ball     0.40     0.714286     0.713877     0.4369 s     0.4500 s
  solid ball     0.80     0.714286     0.713877     0.2184 s     0.2333 s
  hollow shell   0.20     0.600000     0.599519     1.2232 s     1.2333 s
  hollow shell   0.40     0.600000     0.599519     0.6116 s     0.6167 s
  hollow shell   0.80     0.600000     0.599519     0.3058 s     0.3167 s

Quadruple μ and the end speed moves by 0.0000% while the transition time goes from 0.8833 s to 0.2333 s, a factor of 3.79. With μ = 0 the ball neither slows nor spins up at all, for four thousand steps. Nothing but friction can do this, and friction does exactly the half of it the algebra says.

The 0.06% that is not noise

The simulated fraction is 0.713877 where 5/7 is 0.714286 — six hundredths of a per cent out, and identical at every μ, which is the signature of something systematic. It is.

That is the lever arm the solver actually uses, so the effective inertia coefficient is c·(R/r_c)² rather than c. Put that into 1/(1+c) and sweep the penetration over a factor of 150:

10. The order of the two solves

§3 said friction's clip needs a normal impulse to clip against. Here is what happens if you do not believe it.

Drop a 20 kg slab from 30 cm onto a 20° ramp with μ = 0.5. The critical angle is 26.57°, so the slab should grip on the frame it lands and not move again. Measure how far it travels down the slope over the first ten frames of contact, solving friction first and the normal impulse second: 

                                       1 pass       4 passes      16 passes
  normal then friction             18.9620 mm      3.2878 mm      0.0436 mm
  friction then normal             51.2602 mm      6.0293 mm      0.0605 mm
  friction then normal, warm        6.7872 mm      3.4705 mm      0.0120 mm
  normal then friction, warm        3.3812 mm      0.3691 mm      0.0044 mm

Look at the first column, which is what this lesson ships. 51.26 mm against 18.96 mm — and 51.26 mm is exactly what the same slab does with μ set to zero. Not less friction: no friction at all. Every frame starts with a zero accumulator, so every frame's friction solve clips against a cone of radius zero, so no tangential impulse is ever applied. The engine has a friction coefficient, a friction model, and a friction impulse that is always the zero vector.

Two things make this bug survive, and both are visible in the table.

Iteration hides it. With sixteen passes the friction solve is starved only on the first of them, and the two orders converge to 0.044 mm and 0.061 mm. A solver that iterates enough gets away with the wrong order — and then somebody lowers the iteration count for performance and the crates start sliding off the ramps.

Warm starting hides it too, and more interestingly. With warm starting the accumulator arrives holding last frame's impulse, so the cone has a radius on every frame but the first: the wrong order goes from 51.3 mm to 6.8 mm. It is an accident that the bug is nearly fixed by an unrelated optimisation, and it means the failure only shows up on the frame a contact is born — as a single lurch per landing rather than a continuous creep, which is exactly the kind of thing that gets filed as “physics feels a bit loose”.

And the control: on a flat floor the two orders agree to 10⁻⁶ m. With no down-slope velocity there is nothing for friction to fail to resist, so every test written on flat ground passes. That is the other reason this bug survives.

11. Writing it

Everything above is now four short functions. We build them in the order a caller uses them.

The material, and where it lives

8.8's handover predicted that this lesson would put restitution and friction on rigid_body. It does not, for §8's reason: friction belongs to a pair, and a per-body field with a hidden combine rule makes an approximation look like a lookup. So the material is an argument, and the rule is named in the call. 

struct contact_material
{
    float restitution = 0.0f;   // e, dimensionless
    float friction = 0.5f;      // mu, dimensionless
};

[[nodiscard]] contact_material combine_material(
    contact_material a, contact_material b,
    combine_rule restitution_rule = combine_rule::minimum,
    combine_rule friction_rule = combine_rule::minimum);

(8.6 §12's rule applies to handover predictions as much as to knobs: a prediction that turns out wrong is worth recording, because the reason it was wrong is the content. This is the second time in three lessons — 8.6 predicted that 8.7 would widen epa_result, and 8.7 widened shape.hpp instead.)

The effective mass

§4's rearranged form, transcribed. Note that it takes raw inverse masses and tensors rather than bodies — that is not generality for its own sake, it is what lets prepare_contacts hoist the basis change, and §13 measures why that matters. 

float effective_mass(float inv_mass_a, const mat3& inv_inertia_a, vec3 r_a,
                     float inv_mass_b, const mat3& inv_inertia_b, vec3 r_b,
                     vec3 direction)
{
    const vec3 wa = cross(r_a, direction);
    const vec3 wb = cross(r_b, direction);

    const float k = inv_mass_a + inv_mass_b
                    + dot(wa, inv_inertia_a * wa)
                    + dot(wb, inv_inertia_b * wb);

    return k > 0.0f ? 1.0f / k : 0.0f;
}

The k > 0 guard is not defensive programming against a case that cannot happen — §4 established that k is zero exactly when both bodies are immovable, which is a thing a level can contain. Returning zero means the impulse computed from it is zero, which is the right answer, with no branch anywhere downstream.

Prepare: everything that does not change during the solve

The split between contact_point and contact_constraint is the split between geometry and solving. A contact point says where the surfaces touch; a constraint says what an impulse there would do, which needs both bodies' masses and tensors. 8.10 will call the solve many times against one prepare, so everything constant across iterations belongs here — and the orientations do not change during a solve, so the tensors and therefore all three effective masses are constant. 

batch.inv_mass_a = a.inv_mass;
batch.inv_mass_b = b.inv_mass;
batch.inv_inertia_a = world_inv_inertia(a);   // two matrix products, ONCE
batch.inv_inertia_b = world_inv_inertia(b);

tangent_basis(batch.normal, batch.tangent[0], batch.tangent[1]);

One basis per manifold rather than one per point: every point shares the normal, so every point shares the plane.

Restitution is decided once for the manifold, from the deepest-approaching point, and that is a decision rather than an economy. Per-point restitution sounds more careful and is worse: the four corners of a crate landing flat approach at slightly different speeds, so a per-point threshold makes two corners bouncy and two dead on whichever frame the speed straddles it, and the crate takes off sideways. 

// The step's own contribution to the RELATIVE normal velocity. Note the
// subtraction: two dynamic bodies both got the same gravity, so the relative
// velocity it produced is exactly zero and this expression says so.
const float bias_n = dot(step_bias_of(b, cfg.restitution_bias)
                             - step_bias_of(a, cfg.restitution_bias),
                         batch.normal);

const float arrival = worst_approach - bias_n;
batch.restitution_used = (arrival < -cfg.restitution_threshold) ? material.restitution : 0.0f;

Solve: the normal

Three lines and a max, and one of those lines is where the classic bug lives. 

const float vn = dot(relative_at(a, b, c.r_a, c.r_b), batch.normal);
float delta = (c.target_normal_velocity - vn) * c.normal_mass;

// *** CLAMP THE ACCUMULATED IMPULSE, NOT THE INCREMENT. ***
const float total = std::max(0.0f, c.normal_impulse + delta);
delta = total - c.normal_impulse;
c.normal_impulse = total;

apply_impulse_pair(a, b, batch, c, batch.normal * delta);

A contact may push and may not pull, so the total must be non-negative — but an individual increment is allowed to be negative, because a later point may have overshot and this point is entitled to take some of its own impulse back. Clamping delta instead makes every increment a push, the accumulator ratchets upward, and a stack of boxes slowly launches itself.

On the single pass this lesson makes, with the accumulator starting at zero, the two are identical. The file is written for the version that is coming anyway — that is what it means to design a type for a caller you have not written yet, and it costs two lines.

Solve: friction

const float limit = batch.material.friction * c.normal_impulse;
const vec3 u = relative_at(a, b, c.r_a, c.r_b);

float total[2];
for (int t = 0; t < 2; ++t)
{
    total[t] = c.tangent_impulse[t] - dot(u, batch.tangent[t]) * c.tangent_mass[t];
}

if (model == friction_model::cone)
{
    const float magnitude = std::sqrt(total[0] * total[0] + total[1] * total[1]);
    if (magnitude > limit)
    {
        const float scale = magnitude > 0.0f ? limit / magnitude : 0.0f;
        total[0] *= scale;
        total[1] *= scale;
        c.sliding = true;
    }
}

Coulomb's radius is μ times this point's accumulated normal impulse. Not the manifold's total, and not last frame's: a corner that is barely touching gets barely any friction, which is what stops a tilting crate gripping on the corner that has just left the floor.

Where warm starting goes, and where it does not

prepare_contacts seeds the accumulators from the manifold when warm_start is set — carry_impulses put last frame's values there, on matching ids, back in 8.7. Something then has to apply them to the bodies. It is a separate function, and that is load-bearing: 

void warm_start_contacts(rigid_body& a, rigid_body& b, contact_batch& batch);

The first draft of this file put that loop at the top of solve_contacts, which is the obvious place and is wrong. With sixteen iterations the inherited impulse was applied sixteen times. The symptom did not look like “warm starting runs too often”: it looked like friction had stopped working, because the spurious normal impulses launched the crate off the ramp — §10's table showed the warm-started rows sliding 810 mm down a slope they should have gripped, while the cold rows behaved. Warm starting is a once-per-step action, and a once-per-step action inside an iteration loop is a multiplication.

12. What one pass leaves behind

A lesson could stop here, and it would be lying by omission. Put a crate on a floor and leave it for fifteen seconds.

One pass over a four-point manifold does not hold a crate up. It leaves a couple of centimetres per second of residual approach speed, and the crate sinks for as long as you care to watch: 275 mm in fifteen seconds, more than a quarter of its own height. Thirty-two passes converge and the depth freezes. 

    passes    depth at 15 s              creep         residual
         1      274.5697 mm       15.48667 mm/s     2.633e-02 m/s
         2       61.1845 mm        3.40328 mm/s     2.898e-02 m/s
         4       22.5282 mm        0.76687 mm/s     5.506e-03 m/s
         8       10.2386 mm        0.20159 mm/s     2.347e-04 m/s
        16        7.2457 mm        0.00138 mm/s     1.585e-06 m/s
        32        7.2252 mm        0.00001 mm/s     9.866e-09 m/s

There are two different problems in that table, and they need different machinery.

The first: one pass is not a converged solve

Each point's own solve lands its velocity exactly on target. Then the next point's impulse moves it again, because the two share a body and an impulse at one point changes the angular velocity that the other point's velocity depends on. One pass is one sweep of Gauss–Seidel over a system that has not been solved.

And the coupling is not only between points, which is the part worth measuring separately: 

    points      1 pass, no mu   1 pass, mu = 0.5 4 passes, mu = 0.5
         1       3.576e-07 m/s       8.457e-01 m/s       8.017e-02 m/s
         2       4.822e-01 m/s       8.712e-01 m/s       2.642e-01 m/s
         3       1.372e+00 m/s       1.708e+00 m/s       3.866e-01 m/s
         4       7.410e-01 m/s       1.356e+00 m/s       2.600e-01 m/s

Read the first row. With friction off, one point has no residual at all — nothing disturbs it. Turn friction on and the same single point has one, because the tangential impulse acts through a lever arm, changes the angular velocity, and thereby changes the normal velocity at the very point that was just solved. The coupling that makes a solver iterate exists within a single contact, between its normal and its friction.

The second: velocity cannot undo position

Look at where the converged column stops: 7.23 mm, and it does not improve with more passes. That is the penetration the crate arrived with on the frame it was first detected, and no velocity solve will ever repair it. The manifold is produced from the positions; the solve sets the velocity to zero; the position then does not change. The overlap is frozen, not fixed.

How big is it? One step of approach travel. A crate arriving at v has moved v·h since the last time anything looked, and collide_manifold reports nothing at all until the shapes actually overlap — manifold.hpp says so explicitly: “a pair that is a micron short of touching produces nothing”. That is why §1's table shows the impulse solver at 12.55 mm for a 1.2 m drop and 46.6 mm for a 5 m one. It is a statement about the time step, not about the solver, and the fixes are 8.10's: a position correction to push the overlap out, and 8.8's broadphase_config::margin plus 8.7's keep_slop to see the contact one frame early.

And a third thing, which is free to get right

Where the solve sits inside the step is not a detail. Put it after the position update instead of before, and: 

  seconds      mid, 1 pass   mid, 16 passes      end, 1 pass  end, 16 passes
     1.0 s        33.153 mm         7.226 mm       190.669 mm      151.651 mm
     2.0 s        55.822 mm         7.228 mm       395.092 mm      315.151 mm
     5.0 s       119.703 mm         7.232 mm       996.443 mm      805.652 mm
    10.0 s       207.218 mm         7.239 mm     FELL THROUGH     FELL THROUGH

The end-of-step arm sinks by g·h² every step no matter how well the solve converges — predicted 2.7250 mm per step, measured 2.7250 mm — because the position half has already travelled at the velocity the solver is about to cancel. At 60 Hz that is 16 cm a second, and the crate is through a half-metre floor in four. The order is: 

  1. integrate velocities   (gravity, forces)
  2. collide and SOLVE      <- here
  3. integrate positions

13. The budget

A thousand manifolds off a realistic scene of settling crates, 2,163 contact points, timed as a minimum over sixty repetitions with five discarded warm-ups. (The warm-ups are not decoration: the first run measures 173 ns where every later one measures 139.5, a 24% difference that is the instruction cache and the CPU's clock ramp rather than the solver.) 

                                             per manifold    per point
  prepare + solve, cone friction                 139.4 ns      64.4 ns
  prepare + solve, box friction                  130.5 ns      60.3 ns
  prepare + solve, no friction                    89.8 ns      41.5 ns
  prepare only, tensors hoisted                   52.3 ns      24.2 ns
  the same three effective masses, unhoisted     134.1 ns      62.0 ns

Three things fall out of that table.

The cone costs 6.8% more than the box, which §7 already spent. Friction at all costs 55% of a solve without it, which is the honest price of the feature.

The hoist is the whole of prepare_contacts. Computing the same three effective masses per point through the convenience overload — which recomputes both world inverse inertia tensors inside every call, six basis changes per point instead of two per manifold — costs 2.56× the entire prepare, and 96% of prepare and solve together. The convenience overload exists and is documented as not being what the solver calls; this is the measurement behind that sentence.

And the stage fits. 8.8's frame was 2,000 bodies reduced to 983 candidate pairs, with 0.091 ms of broadphase and 0.426 ms of narrow phase. Putting 983 manifolds through this solver costs 0.137 ms, so collision detection plus one solver pass is 0.654 ms of a 16.67 ms frame — under four per cent. Which is worth holding on to, because §12 just established that one pass is not enough: there is room in the budget for the iterations 8.10 is going to want.

16. Common pitfalls & debugging

Everything sticks to everything, or contacts pull instead of push.
Symptom: objects glue themselves to surfaces, or fly into the floor on contact.
Cause: a sign. Either the impulse is applied the same way to both bodies instead of oppositely, or u was computed as a-minus-b where the normal points a-toward-b. Both give a solver that removes the wrong sign of velocity.
Fix: assert the convention once, in a test: put a body above a floor moving down, and check dot(contact_velocity(floor, body, p), normal) < 0. Then check that momentum is conserved — if it is not, the two bodies are getting the same sign.
A stack of boxes slowly launches itself into the air.
Symptom: things creep upward over seconds, gaining energy from nowhere.
Cause: clamping the impulse increment to be non-negative instead of the accumulated total. Every iteration then adds a push and none can take one back, so the accumulator ratchets.
Fix: the three lines in §11 — clamp normal_impulse + delta, then recover delta from the difference. Invisible at one pass, fatal at sixteen.
A ball bounces forever at a constant small height.
Symptom: the bounce decays nicely and then stops decaying, hopping a centimetre or two indefinitely.
Cause: §6. Restitution is being applied to a velocity that already contains this step's g·h.
Fix: set solver_config::restitution_bias to gravity * h. Confirm with the closed form: the hop you are seeing should be (e·g·h/(1−e))²/2g, and if it is, that is the bug and not a coincidence.
Friction does nothing at all, but μ is set.
Symptom: everything slides on everything as though μ were zero, and the friction impulse in the debug panel is always the zero vector.
Cause: §10. The friction solve is running before the normal solve, so Coulomb's limit is μ × 0.
Fix: normal impulses for every point, then friction for every point. It will not reproduce on a flat floor, so write the test on a slope.
Friction is direction-dependent, and the direction makes no sense.
Symptom: a puck slides further north than north-east on the same floor; the preferred directions change when the floor is re-oriented.
Cause: the box clip. The admissible set is a square in a basis that came out of the surface normal.
Fix: friction_model::cone, for 6.8%.
Everything sinks a few millimetres a second, forever.
Symptom: bodies at rest slowly descend into whatever they are resting on.
Cause: §12 — one pass over a multi-point manifold is not a converged solve. Check the residual before assuming anything else: if solve_report::max_residual is millimetres per second rather than 10⁻⁶, the solver has not finished.
Fix: 8.10. In the meantime, iterate.
Bodies sink at exactly 16 cm a second.
Symptom: a much faster and more regular sink than the one above, and it does not improve when you iterate.
Cause: the solve is running after the position update. Each step, the position advances at the velocity the solver then cancels, so the body loses g·h² = 2.7 mm per step whatever the solver does.
Fix: velocities, then contacts, then positions. The regularity is the diagnostic — divide the sink rate by h and see whether you get g·h.
Warm starting makes everything worse.
Symptom: turning on warm starting launches bodies off surfaces.
Cause: the inherited impulses are being applied inside the iteration loop, so they are applied once per iteration. §11's callout — it cost this lesson an afternoon and presented as friction failing.
Fix: warm_start_contacts once per step, outside the loop.
Two spheres bounce off each other at a slight angle.
Symptom: a perfectly head-on collision between two balls produces sideways motion and spin.
Cause: not the solver. §5's aside — EPA's normal for a sphere-sphere pair is 4.26° off, because epa_config::max_iterations was tuned on boxes and a sphere has no flat face to terminate on.
Fix: raise max_iterations to 64 for curved pairs and measure what it costs, or accept it; 8.7's reference-face snapping already hides it whenever one shape is a box.

17. Exercises

  1. Warm-up: predict the permanent hop. Run ./build/demos/impulse --scene 1 --e 0.9 --no-bias. Before you look at the panel, compute the height the ball will settle into hopping at, from e·g·h/(1−e) with h = 1/60. Then check.

    Hint

    g·h = 0.1635 m/s. The terminal speed is a velocity; the height is v²/2g.

    Solution

    v∞ = 0.9 × 0.1635 / 0.1 = 1.4715 m/s, so the hop is 1.4715²/(2 × 9.81) = 0.1104 m — eleven centimetres, forever. Turn the bias back on with [B] and it decays to nothing.

  2. Break the clamp on purpose. In solve_one_normal, replace the three-line accumulator clamp with delta = std::max(0.0f, delta) — clamping the increment rather than the total. Verify it changes nothing at one pass, then run the demo's creep scene with sixteen passes and watch what happens. Explain why the bug is invisible in the configuration this lesson ships.

    Hint

    What is the accumulator's value at the start of the first iteration?

    Solution sketch

    At one pass the accumulator starts at zero, so total = max(0, 0 + delta) and delta = max(0, delta) are the same expression. From the second iteration on they diverge: the correct version lets a point give back impulse that a later point's solve made unnecessary, and the broken one cannot, so the total only ever grows. The crate gains a small upward velocity every step and lifts off. This is the bug worth having seen once — it is silent in every single-iteration test you could write.

  3. Measure the box model's anisotropy yourself, on a different quantity. §7 measured sliding distance. Write a fixture that measures the sliding direction instead: give a crate a tangential velocity at 22.5° to tangent[0] and record the angle of the friction impulse that comes back. Under the cone it must be exactly opposite the velocity; under the box it will not be. How far off does it get, and at what angle is the error largest?

    Hint

    The box clips each component independently, so it pulls the impulse toward whichever diagonal is nearest. The error in direction is zero on the axes and on the diagonals — look between them.

    Solution sketch

    The worst direction error is near 22.5° from an axis, and for a fully sliding contact it reaches about 8°. That is friction pointing eight degrees away from directly opposing the motion, which means the box model does not merely apply the wrong amount of friction — it curves a sliding object's path. Worth knowing before you use it for a driving game.

  4. Engine challenge: a speculative contact. §12 established that the frozen penetration is one step of approach travel, because the narrow phase reports nothing until the shapes actually overlap. Use the two hooks that already exist — broadphase_config::margin from 8.8 and manifold_config::keep_slop from 8.7 — to see the contact one frame early, and give the solver a target velocity that closes the remaining gap in exactly one step rather than zero (u* = −separation/h when the point is separated). Measure the resting penetration before and after.

    Hint

    A separated point has a negative depth. The target velocity for it is “approach at exactly the rate that closes the gap this step, and no faster” — which is a cap, not a push, so it must not be allowed to make a point approach faster than it already is.

    Solution sketch

    This is the technique Box2D calls a speculative contact and it is genuinely the right answer to §12's third problem — better than a position correction, because it never adds energy. The resting penetration should fall to near zero for bodies arriving below some speed, and the failure mode to watch for is a body that arrives faster than margin/h and tunnels anyway. 8.10 revisits it with the position correction beside it so the two can be compared.

  5. Open: is the minimum really the right combine rule? §8 measured five published surface pairs and found min beating sqrt(a·b) 100% of the time under ±30% perturbation. Find more pairs whose three coefficients are all published — the constraint is that you need a on a, b on b and a on b from a source that measured all three the same way — and see whether the result survives. Then argue the other side: construct a scene where the minimum's insensitivity to the grippier material is a usability problem a designer would actually hit.

    Solution sketch

    The measurement is thin — five pairs — and the honest answer is that it is weak evidence strongly held. Metals on metals are where more data lives, and they are also where the minimum does worst (copper on steel: the minimum says 0.74 where the real number is 0.53, because two dissimilar metals are less adhesive than either is with itself). A defensible position is that the minimum is right whenever one surface is lubricious and wrong whenever both are clean metals, and that the geometric mean is a compromise that is never very right and never very wrong. If you can make that case with data, change the default back — and write down what it cost, because that is what this section is for.

18. Recap & next

The engine can resolve a contact. That is the first thing engine::phys has ever done that changes the world rather than describing it, and it took one equation: the relative velocity at a point, the effective mass along a direction, and a division.

What the engine gained:

  • A crate dropped on a floor lands on it and stays there, at 60 Hz, with no stiffness to tune and no per-scene constants.
  • Restitution that reproduces e²ⁿ·h₀ to a millimetre, with the gravity contamination removed exactly rather than masked by a threshold.
  • Coulomb friction with an isotropic cone clip, holding a slab on a slope to within half a degree of atan(μ), and turning a sliding sphere into a rolling one at exactly 5/7 of the speed it landed with.
  • A prepared-constraint shape that hoists the per-body work out of the inner loop, at 139 ns per manifold — so collision detection and one solver pass together are 0.65 ms of a 16.67 ms frame.
  • And the impulse fields carry_impulses has been matching across frames since 8.7 finally have numbers in them.

And what it did not gain, stated plainly because §12 measured it: one pass over a four-point manifold does not hold a crate up. It sinks 275 mm in fifteen seconds. Sixteen passes stop the sinking and leave the crate 7.2 mm inside the floor — the depth it arrived with, which no velocity solve can repair. Two different problems, needing two different pieces of machinery.

Lesson 8.10 builds both. Sequential impulses turn the single pass into an iteration that converges; warm starting — whose plumbing is already here and already measured — is what makes a handful of iterations enough rather than hundreds, by starting each contact from the impulse that held it last frame. Then a position correction for the frozen overlap, and islands and sleeping so that the crates that have settled stop costing anything at all. That is the lesson where a stack becomes possible, and it will redeem the promise Lesson 1.8 made in Module 1: “one impact per step for now — Module 8 iterates until the budget is spent.”
