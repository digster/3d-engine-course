Module 7 — Motion: Rotation, Animation, Audio · Lesson 7.5

Slerp, and the Storage Swap

Three lessons have now derived the same three steps in three different algebras, and this one writes them down for the fourth time in three lines — because a·(a⁻¹b)ᵗ is not a fact about quaternions, it is the definition of a geodesic on any group that has an inverse, a product and a power. What is genuinely new is the half that has no analogue in the plane. The double cover means every pair of orientations has two arcs between it, one of them wrong, and half of all pairs name the wrong one; the fix is one comparison and the bug without it is a character spinning 359° between two keyframes a degree apart. Then the same fact pays a dividend nobody advertises: because that comparison forces the arc into one hemisphere, nlerp's schedule error — unbounded in the plane, where Lesson 7.3 measured it at a factor of 13,131 — is capped here at exactly 2, and at 8.1491° of pose. The thing that looked like a nuisance is what bounds the error. Everything is measured, and two of the measurements refused the claim they were written to confirm. The lesson's own metric had to be rewritten before anything could be measured at all: angle_between shipped in 7.4 as 2·acos|a·b|, which is the formula every reference gives and is blind near zero, and it scored slerp's own geodesic at −41.10% of its own arc. And a table of small-arc errors printed a clean 0.0000e+00 for a function that was returning NaN, because std::max(x, NaN) returns x. Then the swap this engine has promised since Module 2: transform::rotation becomes a quat, twenty-five call sites move, and the narrower type finds — as it found one in 7.4 — two more places that had never put a rotation in a field called rotation, one of them a published demo whose camera boom had been storing a sheared basis since Lesson 5.12.

1. The problem: a rotation you cannot blend

Here is a thing an engine has to do several thousand times a frame, and which this engine currently cannot do at all. A character's left elbow is at one orientation in keyframe 41 and a different one in keyframe 42. The clock says you are 37% of the way between them. Where is the elbow?

Try it with what transform holds today. Its rotation field is a mat3, so you have two matrices and a number, and the obvious thing to do with two matrices and a number is to average their entries:

\[ M(t) \;=\; (1-t)\,A \;+\; t\,B \]

That is not a rotation. It is not nearly a rotation, either — it is a matrix that shears and shrinks, and you can see how badly in one number. The determinant of a rotation is exactly 1, because a rotation preserves volume; the determinant of the entrywise average of the two poses this lesson's harness uses is 0.00007. Feed that to a vertex shader and the character does not merely bend oddly. It collapses to a plane.

Why this is not a fixable defect of matrices

You could Gram-Schmidt the average back into a rotation, and people do. The result is not the halfway pose — it is a pose, somewhere near the halfway one, arrived at by a procedure with no geometric meaning, whose error grows with the angle between the endpoints and which has no closed form you can reason about. The problem is not that the average is wrong. It is that the set of rotation matrices is not closed under addition, so there is nothing between two of them to find. Nine numbers is six too many, and the six extra are constraints, not freedom.

This is the last of the four costs Lesson 7.1 opened the module by listing, and it is the one that decided the storage. Lesson 7.2 could compose but not store cheaply; Lesson 7.3 proved in the plane that a number system does the job and that four components are forced in space; Lesson 7.4 built the four components and measured everything about them except the one operation the animation system actually needs. This lesson does that operation, and then makes the engine store the thing that can do it.

2. Building intuition: the shortest way round a sphere

Forget quaternions for a paragraph and think about the Earth. You are in London and you want to be in Tokyo, and you want to fly the shortest route and arrive at a steady speed. Two questions, and they are different: which way do I go, and where am I at each moment.

The first has a famous answer. The shortest path between two points on a sphere is the arc of the great circle through them — the circle you get by slicing the sphere with the plane that contains both points and the centre. Any other route is longer, and the reason is worth one sentence because the same sentence is going to do all the work later: the great circle is the only path that never turns. Every other route is curving within the surface as well as with it.

The second question is the one people skip. Having chosen the arc, you still have to say how far along it you are at time t, and "37% of the way" can mean 37% of the angle or 37% of something else. Those are different points, and telling them apart is most of §5.

2.1 Why a rotation is a point on a sphere

Lesson 7.4 gave us the object: a rotation of θ about the unit axis n̂ is the quaternion

\[ q \;=\; \cos(\theta/2) \;+\; \sin(\theta/2)\,\hat n \]

and its norm is 1, because cos²+sin²=1 and n̂ is a unit vector. So every rotation is a point at distance 1 from the origin in four-dimensional space — a point on the unit 3-sphere, exactly as every rotation of the plane was a point on the unit circle in Lesson 7.3. The London-to-Tokyo problem is not an analogy for what we are about to do. It is the same problem with one more coordinate.

And with one wrinkle that the Earth does not have, which §6 is entirely about: every orientation is two points on this sphere, q and −q, at opposite ends of a diameter. Imagine arriving in Tokyo and being told there is a second Tokyo on the far side of the planet that is also Tokyo. Which one you aim at changes your flight plan completely, and nothing in the destination's name tells you.

2.2 One formula, now in its third algebra

Lesson 7.2 built rotation_slerp for matrices and Lesson 7.3 built complex_slerp for the plane, and 7.3 pointed out — without cashing it in — that the two are the same three steps. Here they are for the last time, and the third column is this lesson's whole implementation:

\[ \operatorname{slerp}(a, b, t) \;=\; a \cdot \big(a^{-1} b\big)^{t} \]

Undo a; take t of the difference; redo a. Nothing in that sentence mentions a dimension, a representation, or a number of components. It needs an inverse, a product, and a way to raise something to a real power — and a unit quaternion has all three.

The bottom of figure 1 is the part that makes this a lesson rather than a claim. Lesson 7.2's rotation_slerp works on mat3 in a notation that has never heard of a quaternion, and 7.3's complex_slerp works in two dimensions on two floats. Run all three over the same journey and they agree to 4.172e−07 of a matrix entry and 2.384e−07 radians respectively — which is float resolution, three times over. Those two numbers are §7's instruments as much as they are its results: the new function is checked against two older ones that share no code with it and no code with each other.

The word "geodesic", earned

A geodesic is the straightest path a curved space allows. Lesson 7.2 introduced the word with matrices and measured what it buys: 0.00% of excess turning, against Euler-angle interpolation's +14% on a generic pair and +209% near gimbal lock. §7.2 of this lesson re-measures the same pose pair with quaternions and gets +14.10% for the Euler route — the same number, from a completely different representation, with a completely different metric. That agreement is not a coincidence and it is not a tautology: it is two implementations of "how much turning did this path actually do?" landing on the same answer.

3. The power of a unit quaternion

The formula needs (a⁻¹b)ᵗ, so before anything else we need to know what it means to raise a rotation to a real power. It means exactly what you would hope: do a fraction of it. Raise a quarter-turn to the power 0.5 and you get an eighth-turn; to the power 2 and you get a half-turn; to the power −1 and you get the quarter-turn backwards.

Deriving it is one line, because 7.4 already did the work. A unit quaternion is its axis and its angle:

\[ q \;=\; \cos(\theta/2) + \sin(\theta/2)\,\hat n \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad q^{t} \;=\; \cos(t\theta/2) + \sin(t\theta/2)\,\hat n \]

Same axis, t times the angle. There is nothing to prove — scaling a rotation means scaling its angle, and that is the definition. The work is all in extraction: getting θ/2 and n̂ back out of four floats, and putting them back in.

3.1 Reading the angle: atan2, not acos

The scalar part is cos(θ/2), so the obvious move is θ/2 = acos(w). Do not do it. This course has now met the reason three times — in a metric (7.1 §6.4), in an extraction (7.2 §7.3), and in a test that printed 0.000e+00 in every row because of it (7.4 §G.3) — and it is about to meet it a fourth time in §7.3 of this lesson, inside the engine.

The cosine is flat at its maximum. Near Ω = 0,

\[ \cos\Omega \;=\; 1 - \tfrac{\Omega^2}{2} + \dots \qquad\text{while}\qquad \sin\Omega \;=\; \Omega - \tfrac{\Omega^3}{6} + \dots \]

The first is quadratic in the angle and the second is linear. A small angle barely moves the cosine, so by the time you have stored it in a float most of it is gone; the same angle moves the sine proportionally, so it survives. The vector part's length is sin(θ/2), sitting right there in the other three floats, so:

\[ \theta/2 \;=\; \operatorname{atan2}\big(|v|,\;w\big) \]

which is accurate at both ends, is quadrant-correct — so θ comes back in [0, 2π) rather than [0, π], which 7.4 §9.2 argued is information rather than inconsistency — and costs a square root the acos route was going to pay anyway to get the axis.

3.2 The division the plane did not have

Lesson 7.3's complex_pow_unit is two trig calls and no division, because in the plane the axis is the same axis every time and there is nothing to recover. Here there is: n̂ = v / |v|, and |v| = sin(θ/2) goes to zero as the rotation goes to nothing. That is exactly where an animation system spends most of its time.

So look at what the code actually computes. The new vector part is

\[ \sin(t\theta/2)\;\hat n \;=\; \frac{\sin(t\theta/2)}{|v|}\; v \;=\; \frac{\sin(t\theta/2)}{\sin(\theta/2)}\; v \]

The factor is a ratio of two sines of proportional angles, and as θ → 0 it tends to t — because sin x → x. It is finite. It is, moreover, exactly the right answer: a tiny rotation scaled by t is t times as tiny about the same axis.

So there is no epsilon, no branch, and no "if the angle is small, lerp instead"

That last one is in a great deal of shipped code and it is a patch over a problem this form does not have. The expression is continuous through the identity. §7.4 measures it down to an arc of 0 — literally zero, the identity blended with itself — and the error is 0.0000e+00, while the formula every textbook prints returns NaN for every arc below 0.0279°.

There is one guard, and it is for a different case: |v| = 0 exactly. Two inputs do that. q = 1 is the identity, θ = 0, and every axis is correct — so the identity is the answer for every t and the guard returns it. q = −1 is a full turn about something nobody recorded; q^t should be a turn of 2πt about an axis that does not exist, there is no continuous answer, and the guard returns the identity as a placeholder on exactly the same terms as normalised(vec3{}). §4.2 shows why quat_slerp can never reach it.

3.3 A worked example, by hand

Take a quarter-turn about +y and halve it. The quaternion is cos45° + sin45°·ŷ, so

\[ q \;=\; (0.7071,\; 0,\; 0.7071,\; 0) \]

Now push it through the code, in order:

  • |v| = √(0² + 0.7071² + 0²) = 0.7071.
  • θ/2 = atan2(0.7071, 0.7071) = 45° = 0.7854 rad. So θ = 90°, which is the turn we started with — the extraction is working.
  • t = 0.5, so the scaled half-angle is 22.5° = 0.3927 rad.
  • cos(22.5°) = 0.9239, sin(22.5°) = 0.3827.
  • The ratio is 0.3827 / 0.7071 = 0.5412, and v · 0.5412 = (0, 0.3827, 0).
\[ q^{0.5} \;=\; (0.9239,\; 0,\; 0.3827,\; 0) \]

Which is cos(22.5°) + sin(22.5°)·ŷ — a 45° turn about +y, half of the 90° we started with. Note that 0.9239 is not halfway between 0.7071 and 1 (that would be 0.8536). Halving the angle is not halving the cosine, which is the whole reason this function exists rather than a lerp.

4. Implementation: slerp, in three lines

With the power in hand the interpolation is the formula, transcribed: 

[[nodiscard]] inline quat quat_slerp(quat a, quat b, float t)
{
    return a * quat_pow_unit(conjugate(a) * nearest(a, b), t);
}

Read right to left, as always: nearest(a, b) picks which of the two names for b to aim at (§6 — it is the only thing here the plane did not need); conjugate(a) is a⁻¹ because a is unit; the product is the single turn from one to the other; the power takes t of it; and multiplying by a on the left starts the journey where it should start.

4.1 Constant speed, and one line that never moves

Two properties fall out of the shape of that expression rather than needing proof, which is the nicest kind of correctness argument.

The speed is constant. The only thing in the whole expression that depends on t is the angle inside quat_pow_unit, and that angle is t times a constant. So the turn accumulated by time t is t·Ω_total, linear in t, by construction. Measured over 1,001 steps of a 179.05° blend, the worst deviation from t·arc is 4.159e−07 radians.

The axis never moves. quat_pow_unit scales an angle and leaves v's direction alone, so the difference quaternion's axis is the same line at every t. That is the teal line Lesson 7.2's B mode draws and the thing to watch during a blend: the geodesic ghost rotates rigidly about one fixed line for the entire journey, and the Euler ghost does not.

The axis measurement that had to be read twice

The harness first reported the axis wandering by 2.027e−05 over the blend, which is twenty times float resolution and looked like a bug. It is not: the whole of it is at t < 0.05, where the accumulated turn is under 9° and n̂ = v/sin(θ/2) is dividing by something small. Restricted to t ≥ 0.05 the wobble is 6.546e−07.

And the wobble is harmless for a reason Lesson 7.2 §8 already derived and put a formula on: an axis error of φ on a turn of θ moves the pose by 2·sin(θ/2)·φ, which vanishes exactly where the axis becomes unrecoverable. Multiply the measured error by that factor at each step and the worst orientation cost over the whole blend is 2.309e−07 radians. The harness prints all three columns, because "the axis is noisy" and "the pose is wrong" are different claims and only one of them is true.

4.2 Not clamped, and unreachable cases

t outside [0, 1] extrapolates along the same great circle, which is occasionally exactly what a caller wants — an overshooting ease-out, a one-frame prediction — and is never what it wants by accident. Clamping here would make the useful case impossible and hide the accidental one. The engine's convention since lerp in Lesson 1.6 is that the caller owns its parameter.

And note what nearest buys besides the short arc: after it, the difference quaternion conj(a)·b has a non-negative scalar part, so its half-angle is at most 90° and quat_pow_unit's two axis-free inputs are both unreachable from quat_slerp. q = −1 needs a scalar part of −1, which nearest has just excluded. The guard exists for direct callers only.

5. The right road on the wrong schedule

There is a cheaper thing you can do with two quaternions and a number, and unlike the matrix version it is not nonsense. Lerp the four components and normalise: 

[[nodiscard]] inline quat quat_nlerp(quat a, quat b, float t)
{
    const quat to = nearest(a, b);
    return normalised(quat{a.w + (to.w - a.w) * t,
                           a.v + (to.v - a.v) * t});
}

No trigonometry, no atan2, one reciprocal square root. Measured at 4.763 ns against slerp's 16.288 ns — 3.42× cheaper. And it is used everywhere, and it is mostly right, and the two halves of "mostly" need separating with some care because the folklore gets both of them wrong in opposite directions.

5.1 The path is exactly the geodesic

This is the half that surprises people, and Lesson 7.3 proved it in the plane with an argument that never mentions dimension — so it carries here unchanged and this lesson's job is to check it rather than re-derive it.

The straight chord from a to b lies in the two-dimensional plane spanned by those two points and the origin. Normalising a point moves it along its own radius, and a radius through a point of that plane stays in that plane. So every point quat_nlerp returns lies on the intersection of that plane with the unit sphere — which is the great circle through a and b. It is the same curve quat_slerp walks. It cannot be anything else.

Measured two ways. The departure from the a–b plane over 501 samples is 1.605e−07; and the total turning along the path, summed over 4,096 steps against the separation of the endpoints, is −0.00% of excess — the identical number slerp scores, on the same pair, with the same instrument, where Euler interpolation scores +14.10%.

5.2 The schedule is not

Look again at figure 2's gold chord. The eleven dots on it are equally spaced — that is what lerping does. Project them radially onto the circle and they are not: they crowd together at both ends and spread out through the middle.

The geometry is the whole explanation. A step along the chord near the middle of the arc is nearly perpendicular to the radius, so almost all of it turns into angle. A step near either end is angled away, so part of it goes into length — which normalising then throws away. Equal steps along a chord subtend unequal angles at the centre.

5.3 Putting a closed form on it

Set up coordinates in the plane of the arc, with a at angle 0 and b at angle Ω. The lerped point is (1−t)·a + t·b, and its angle from a is the argument of that vector:

\[ \varphi(t) \;=\; \arctan\!\left(\frac{t\,\sin\Omega}{(1-t) + t\,\cos\Omega}\right) \]

Differentiate and the algebra collapses (Lesson 7.3 §10.3 does it in full). What comes out is that φ'(t) is largest at the midpoint and smallest at the two ends, and the ratio between those extremes is

\[ \frac{\varphi'(1/2)}{\varphi'(0)} \;=\; \sec^2(\Omega/2) \]

nlerp moves fastest through the middle of the arc, by exactly sec²(Ω/2). At Ω = 45° that is 1.17; at Ω = 90° it is exactly 2; at Ω = 179° it is 13,131.

And Ω is not θ — this is where a quaternion differs from the plane

In Lesson 7.3 the angle between two unit complex numbers was the rotation between the two poses. Here it is half of it, because q = cos(θ/2) + sin(θ/2)n̂ — the sphere arc is Ω = θ/2. So every number 7.3 quoted has to be re-indexed before it can be used, and the harness re-measures rather than re-quoting. A rotation arc of 150° is a sphere arc of 75°, and the schedule error that matters is measured back in degrees of pose, which doubles it again.

5.4 The number to design with

A speed ratio is hard to act on. What a caller wants is: if I use the cheap one, how wrong is the picture? That is the gap in pose, at the same t, at the worst t — and it is what §7 measures.

The measured points sit on the derived curve. Read them as a rule of thumb:

  • Under about 60° of arc, nlerp is within a quarter of a degree. A 30 Hz animation clip's adjacent keyframes are far below that. Use nlerp; it is 3.42× cheaper and the difference is not visible.
  • Between 60° and 120°, it is a judgement — up to 2.2° of lag through the middle of the turn, which reads as a slight lurch on something the eye is tracking.
  • Over 120°, and for anything a designer chose rather than a clip recorded — a turret swinging to a new target, a ragdoll snapping to a pose — use slerp. At 150° of arc the lag is 4.5°.

The harness turns that into one threshold: nlerp stays within half a degree of slerp up to an arc of 73.50°. That is a number you can put in a comment beside a call.

6. The double cover, twice: a sign and a bound

Everything so far would have worked in Lesson 7.3 with complex substituted for quat. This section would not, and it is the reason 7.4 deliberately left slerp unwritten.

6.1 Two names for one destination

Lesson 7.4 §8 established, and measured bitwise over 20,000 rotations, that q and −q are the same rotation. The sandwich q v q̄ is quadratic in q, so a global sign cannot survive it: |M(q) − M(−q)| = 0.000e+00, not a tolerance.

That is a fact about orientations. Interpolation is not about orientations; it is about points, and q and −q are antipodal points on the unit 3-sphere. So there are two great-circle arcs between any two orientations — one through b and one through −b — whose angles sum to 2π. Exactly one of them is the short way, and nothing in the two endpoints' identity as orientations says which.

6.2 The fix is one comparison

[[nodiscard]] constexpr quat nearest(quat reference, quat q)
{
    return (reference.w * q.w + dot(reference.v, q.v) < 0.0f) ? -q : q;
}

The four-component dot product is the cosine of the angle between the two points, and it is also the scalar part of conj(a)·b — one of this algebra's small coincidences, and the reason every slerp implementation in the world opens with a dot product. Negative means the angle between the points exceeds 90°, which means the rotation between them exceeds 180°, which means the other way round is shorter.

Worked, with numbers

Take a = 1 (the identity) and b a turn of 350° about +y. Then b = cos175° + sin175°·ŷ = (−0.99619, 0, 0.08716, 0). The dot product with the identity is just b.w = −0.99619, which is negative, so nearest returns −b = (0.99619, 0, −0.08716, 0).

Read that back: cos(5°) + sin(5°)·(−ŷ) — a 10° turn about −y. Which is the same place. The blend now covers ten degrees instead of three hundred and fifty, and the only thing that changed was a sign on a number the caller never sees.

6.3 The bug you ship without it

This is not a theoretical tidiness. Here is the shape it takes in practice, and it does not look like a sign error when you meet it.

An exporter writes an animation clip as a list of quaternion keyframes. It has no reason to make their signs consistent — q and −q are the same pose, the file format says nothing, and the maths that produced them (a matrix extraction, say, with a pivot that changed between frames) does not care. So two adjacent keyframes describing poses one degree apart can perfectly well be stored with opposite signs.

Blend them without the comparison and the character takes the 359° route between two poses that were a degree apart: a whole-body spin lasting exactly one keyframe interval, in the middle of a walk cycle. It reads as "the animation is corrupt", not as "somebody forgot a dot product", and it is the single most common quaternion bug in shipped code.

The harness measures the frequency, because "sometimes" is not a design input: 9,923 of 20,000 uniformly sampled pairs — 49.6% — need the flip. It is not an edge case. It is a coin toss.

nlerp's version of the same failure is worse, and worth seeing separately. The chord between two antipodal representatives passes through the origin, where there is no direction left to normalise. On the keyframe pair above, the raw chord passes within 0.00436 of zero — so the blend does not merely take the long way, it goes through a point where the answer is whatever normalised's zero policy happens to be.

6.4 And now the dividend

Here is the part that is genuinely new, that 7.3 could not have found, and that turns the double cover from a tax into a guarantee.

nearest forces the four-component dot product to be non-negative. That is the same as saying the sphere arc Ω is at most 90°. Substitute into §5.3's closed form:

\[ \sec^2(\Omega/2) \;\le\; \sec^2(45°) \;=\; \frac{1}{\cos^2 45°} \;=\; \frac{1}{1/2} \;=\; 2 \]

Exactly 2. Not approximately, not usually — the worst speed ratio nlerp can ever have, over every pair of orientations that exists, is a factor of two.

Compare what the plane could do. In Lesson 7.3 there was no double cover to exploit: z and −z are genuinely different rotations of the plane, so Ω could reach 180°, sec²(Ω/2) could reach 13,131, and the positional gap could reach 26.34°. None of those are reachable here. The gap caps at 8.1491°, and the harness measures it over 20,000 random pairs: largest sphere arc seen 89.9969° against a bound of 90; largest gap seen 8.1345° against a bound of 8.1491.

The control is the same 20,000 pairs with the comparison deleted, where the arc reaches 176.3020° and no bound applies at all.

Why this is worth stopping on

"nlerp is an approximation" is the usual summary, and an approximation with no error bound is not something you can build on. This one has a bound, it is tight, and it comes from the very feature that made the sign comparison necessary. The cost and the guarantee are the same fact. That is worth more than either half: it means a system can use nlerp everywhere, know its worst case is 8.15° of pose, and decide with a threshold on the arc rather than with a preference.

7. Measuring it, and fixing the instrument first

Every claim above is now a number. Getting those numbers required rewriting the thing that measures them, which is the finding this lesson did not plan for.

7.1 The demo: a dial with two rows of ticks

gimbal gains S. It blends between two orientations 150° apart and puts three craft on the arc: slerp in pale blue, nlerp in amber, and — in magenta — the same pair of poses blended the way round that nearest exists to avoid.

The two poses are at most 8° apart, which at this radius is a third of a unit, so two aircraft would be indistinguishable. The ticks carry the argument. Eleven equally spaced values of t are marked twice on the same circle — outward for slerp, inward for nlerp — and the difference between an evenly spaced row and a bunched one is visible from across the room.

Two drawing decisions in that figure are load-bearing and both were mistakes first. The rotation axis points at the camera, so the nose's path renders as a circle rather than an ellipse — an ellipse's arc lengths are not proportional to its angles, so the tick spacing would have been compressed at the ends by perspective and not by nlerp, and no reader could tell which. That is Lesson 7.4's dial lesson arriving a second time. And both rows of ticks touch the same circle, one pointing out and one in: drawing the second row on a smaller circle would have said the two paths differ, which is the single most common misconception about nlerp and the thing this mode exists to kill.

The right-hand panel is t = 0.5, and there is only one glyph in it. That is not a rendering failure: 

gimbal: sched t 0.500  arc 150.00 deg
gimbal:   slerp 75.0000, nlerp 75.0000 deg turned
gimbal:   nlerp lag 0.0000 deg
gimbal:   long way 105.0000 deg turned

nlerp and slerp agree exactly at the midpoint, and it is a symmetry rather than a coincidence: the chord's midpoint is equidistant from both endpoints, and so is the arc's, and on a circle there is only one such point on the short side. The harness checks it at seven arcs from 10° to 179° and the worst chord between the two midpoints is 6.664e−08.

Which makes the obvious unit test useless

A test that samples t = 0, t = 0.5 and t = 1 certifies nlerp as slerp, because those are precisely the three values at which they are the same function. The gap at t = 0.5 is 0.0000°; at t = 0.229 it is 4.5140°.

This is Lesson 7.4's finding one level up. That lesson found renormalised_fast exact at |q| = 1 and at |q| = 2 and catastrophic between, and drew the moral choose the sample points. Here the sample points that hide the difference are the three a reasonable person would choose first.

7.2 The path, against a number Lesson 7.1 measured

Total turning along a path, at a fixed step count, against the straight-line separation of the endpoints. Lesson 7.1 built this instrument for matrices and Lesson 7.2 used it to prove slerp's geodesic property. Here it is again, on the same pose pair, in a different representation: 

   geodesic  179.0527 deg
   slerp     179.0524 deg   excess   -0.00%
   nlerp     179.0524 deg   excess   -0.00%
   euler     204.2903 deg   excess  +14.10%

The Euler row is the check, and it is a stronger one than it looks. Lesson 7.2's harness measures the same three quantities on the same pose pair using mat3 throughout and angle_between_rotations as its metric — code that has never heard of a quaternion, written three lessons ago: 

  generic pair       path   geodesic     excess    speed
  Euler lerp      204.29°    179.05°     14.10%   1.556x  <- CONTROL (7.1's number)
  slerp           179.05°    179.05°     -0.00%   1.000x

Every figure agrees to the digit. Two representations, two metrics, two harnesses, no shared code — and that agreement is what makes the other two rows believable, because a path integral that reports −0.00% for a function you wrote yourself is exactly the kind of number that should be distrusted until something independent confirms it. This lesson's first attempt at that measurement did not survive that scepticism, and §7.3 is what it found.

7.3 The instrument that had to be rewritten first

Those numbers are the second set. The first set said this: 

   geodesic  179.0527 deg
   slerp     164.7134 deg   excess   -8.01%
   nlerp     162.4646 deg   excess   -9.26%
   euler     188.0517 deg   excess   +5.03%

A path integral reporting that slerp's own geodesic is 8% shorter than the distance between its endpoints is not a finding about slerp. It is a broken ruler, and it took a while to believe that rather than the numbers.

The culprit is angle_between(quat, quat), which Lesson 7.4 shipped as the formula every reference gives:

\[ \theta \;=\; 2\arccos\big|\,a \cdot b\,\big| \]

Look at the left panel of figure 7. Near zero the cosine is quadratic in the angle and the sine is linear. So for a small separation the dot product lands within a few float ulps of 1, and acos is being asked to read the angle off a quantity that has already lost almost all of it. The noise floor is computable: the ulp below 1 is eps/2, so

\[ \theta_{\text{floor}} \;\approx\; 2\sqrt{2\,\varepsilon} \;=\; 2\sqrt{2 \times 1.192\times10^{-7}} \;=\; 0.0560° \]

A path integral over 4,096 steps of a 150° arc takes steps of 0.037° — every one of them below that floor. So every step is quantised, and quantised downward, and 4,096 biased errors do not average out. The right panel of figure 7 is the result: 

   endpoints are 150.0000 deg apart
   slerp path by 7.4's acos form: 88.3437 deg  (-41.10%)
   slerp path by the atan2 form:  150.0000 deg  (-0.00%)

−41.10%. The metric was failing slerp for not walking the path it was walking.

The repair is the form the same file already uses eleven lines higher. axis_angle_from_quat reads its angle with atan2(|v|, w) and its doc comment says why, in as many words — and then angle_between, directly below it, uses acos. Writing the difference quaternion's two parts out by hand:

\[ \operatorname{conj}(a)\,b \;=\; \big(\,a\!\cdot\!b,\;\; a_w b_v - b_w a_v - a_v \times b_v \,\big) \qquad \theta \;=\; 2\operatorname{atan2}\big(|v|,\;|a\!\cdot\!b|\,\big) \]

About twelve multiplies where the old form was four. For a metric — called in tests, in editors and in path integrals, and never per vertex — that is the right side of the trade, and the acos form is kept in the file as angle_between_by_cosine so the two can be measured against each other rather than argued about. That is exactly the arrangement math/rotation.hpp has had for matrices since Lesson 7.1.

The lesson underneath, which is not about acos

7.4 wrote angle_between for a separation — how far apart are these two poses — where the answer is large and acos is fine. A path integral asks the same function for a step, where the answer is tiny. The function did not change; the question did, and nothing in the type system or the name marks the difference. When you reuse a numerical routine at a different scale, re-derive its conditioning at that scale. Fourth appearance of this trap in Module 7, and the first inside the engine.

7.4 The textbook form, and a table that lied

Every reference prints slerp like this:

\[ \operatorname{slerp}(a,b,t) \;=\; \frac{\sin\big((1-t)\Omega\big)\,a \;+\; \sin\big(t\Omega\big)\,b}{\sin \Omega} \]

It is the same function — measured agreement 1.192e−07, one float ulp, over a 120° arc. It is also worse, and in a way that matters exactly where animation lives.

It divides by sin Ω. As the arc closes, sin Ω → 0 and so does the numerator, and the cancellation is not arranged — both parts are computed separately and then divided. Worse, Ω itself comes from an acos, so it becomes exactly 0 as soon as the dot product rounds to 1. Then sin 0 = 0 and the whole thing is 0/0.

We can predict where. The ulp below 1 is eps/2, so round-to-nearest takes anything within eps/4 of 1; 1 − cos Ω ≈ Ω²/2, so the cliff is at Ω = √(eps/2) and the rotation arc is twice that:

\[ \theta_{\text{cliff}} \;=\; 2\sqrt{\varepsilon/2} \;=\; 2\sqrt{5.96\times10^{-8}} \;=\; 0.0280° \]
   arc (deg)     textbook       ours err
      1.0000     1.1921e-07     5.7032e-10
      0.1000     1.1921e-07     7.1290e-11
      0.0100     101 NaN of 101   7.7173e-12
      0.0001     101 NaN of 101   6.5122e-14
      0.0000     101 NaN of 101   0.0000e+00
   textbook form goes NaN below an arc of 0.0279 deg
   predicted: 2*sqrt(eps/2) = 0.0280 deg

0.0279 measured against 0.0280 predicted. And a NaN pose does not stay local — it propagates through every child in the skeleton, and the symptom is an entire character disappearing on one frame.

And the first version of that table said 0.0000e+00 in all four rows

It accumulated the worst error with std::max. Every comparison against a NaN is false, so std::max(x, NaN) returns x: a NaN can never win a maximum. The textbook column therefore printed a clean, perfect, entirely fictional zero for a function that was failing in all 101 samples of all four rows — and the row it printed correctly, at 1°, showed it beating our version, which made the table look like an argument for the textbook form.

A NaN has to be counted, never maximised. Fifth blind instrument in Module 7, and the first one in a harness rather than in engine code.

7.5 What it costs

Three interpolations, three lessons, one benchmark. quat_nlerp at 4.763 ns, quat_slerp at 16.288 ns, and Lesson 7.2's rotation_slerp on mat3 at 45.752 ns — which is 2.81× the quaternion version, because it extracts an axis and an angle with axis_angle_from_rotation, scales the angle, and rebuilds with Rodrigues.

The representation is cheaper than the one it replaced, at the job it replaced it for, which is not something Lesson 7.4 could say: that lesson found quaternions 1.57× cheaper to compose and 1.57× dearer to apply to a vector. Interpolation is the row where the fourth component pays for itself outright.

Every timed loop here obeys the rules Module 7 has accumulated the hard way: consume the whole result, never overwrite the accumulator (7.4 §F.1 measured 0.000 ns that way), vary the input with the repetition counter (7.2), best of three after a warm-up, and check the answer is physically possible before believing it.

8. The storage swap

math/transform.hpp has carried a promise since Lesson 2.8: the mat3 in the rotation field is honest but not final, and a quaternion replaces it. That promise has moved three times — first to 7.1, then to 7.4, then to here — and the comment that made it said, until this lesson, that the swap "touches one line of parent_from_local() and nothing else".

That sentence was written in Module 2 and was wrong by Module 5. It is wrong for a reason worth the whole section.

8.1 The change itself

struct transform
{
    vec3 position{0.0f, 0.0f, 0.0f};
    quat rotation = quat::identity();     // was: mat3 rotation = mat3::identity();
    vec3 scale{1.0f, 1.0f, 1.0f};
};

And parent_from_local gains exactly one line — the Module 2 comment was right about that much: 

const mat3 basis = mat3_from_quat(t.rotation);

const mat3 axes{basis.c0 * t.scale.x,
                basis.c1 * t.scale.y,
                basis.c2 * t.scale.z};

return affine(axes, t.position);

That one line is also engine policy arriving as a statement. Lesson 7.4 §10.3 measured the crossover: a quaternion is 1.57× dearer than a matrix at rotating a vector, and converting costs 4.619 ns, so building the matrix first pays for itself after about eight vectors. A mesh has thousands. So the engine stores quaternions, converts exactly once per object per frame, right here, and multiplies every vertex by a matrix. "Quaternions are faster than matrices" is true of storing and composing and false of the thing a frame spends its time on.

8.2 Twenty-five call sites, and what the compiler said about three of them

Narrowing a type is a compile error at every site that was relying on the width. Most of the twenty-five are a rename with a better function underneath — rotation_y(a) becomes quat_y(a), and composing two of them drops from 27 multiplies to 16. Several are a basis the geometry genuinely speaks in, kept as a mat3 and narrowed at the assignment: 

// demos/common/demo_scene.cpp — a plank's frame is "along the rail, across it, up"
t.rotation = engine::quat_from_rotation(engine::mat3{axis_x, axis_y, axis_z});

That pattern is safe precisely because of Lesson 7.4 §9: Shepperd extraction has no bad case anywhere — four candidates that sum to exactly 4, so the pivot is never below 1 — which means there is no angle at which "construct as a matrix, store as a quaternion" is less accurate than another.

Two sites are stepped rather than rebuilt, and those gained a repair: 

// demos/collector/main.cpp — the carousel, one line per frame, for ever
t.rotation = engine::renormalised_fast(t.rotation * engine::quat_y(c.rate * h));

This is the first place in the engine where drift is a real problem rather than a measurement. The mat3 it replaced had the same disease and a dearer cure — Gram-Schmidt, three normalisations and two projections. renormalised_fast is one subtract and five multiplies, no sqrt and no divide, and 7.4 §F.4 measured it holding a million compositions at 6e−08 where the unrepaired walk reached 1e−03. The site next to it, ecs_swarm's spin system, gets no repair and should not: it rebuilds from an angle every frame, so the previous frame's answer is never an input. The difference is not "rotation" versus "rotation" — it is whether the field is an accumulator.

And then there were three sites that did not compile for a more interesting reason.

8.3 The field called rotation with no rotation in it

Lesson 7.4 §11 found the first one and declined to fix it. gfx/renderable.cpp turns an ECS world_transform — a composed 4×4 — into a scene_object, and it did so like this: 

.xform = {.position = translation_of(w.matrix),
          .rotation = linear_of(w.matrix),      // the WHOLE upper-left 3x3
          .scale    = {1.0f, 1.0f, 1.0f}},

Its comment said, correctly, that the recomposition "reproduces the original affine matrix to the bit". It did. It did so because a mat3 will hold anything — including the scale that came down the hierarchy from a parent, sitting in a field named rotation, since Lesson 5.11.

A quaternion will not hold a scale, so that line became a compile error, which is the type doing exactly the job it is here to do. And once you go looking, the same trick is in demos/ecs_swarm — and a third variant, which is the one worth the paper.

8.4 collector's camera boom, sheared since 5.12

Lesson 5.12 built a checkpoint game with a rover and a chase camera, and §6 of that lesson made a good argument: a camera parented to the player inherits the player's 22° of cosmetic roll, the horizon see-saws, and the fix is not a special case in the renderer but one more link in the chain — a boom that applies the inverse roll. It also has to undo the rover's non-uniform scale, or "6.4 units back" means 3.97 metres.

The algebra it derived is right, and the demo's comment spells it out because getting it backwards cost that file its first crash:

\[ \text{boom local} \;=\; \big(H \cdot R_z(\text{bank}) \cdot S\big)^{-1} H \;=\; S^{-1} \cdot R_z(-\text{bank}) \]

Scale first, rotation second. And a transform builds T · R · S — scale innermost. So a single node can say "rotate, then scale" and cannot say "scale, then rotate", and those are different matrices whenever the scale is non-uniform. The difference is a shear: the product S⁻¹R has columns that are not perpendicular.

So that line had been storing a sheared basis in a field called rotation for thirteen lessons, and nothing complained, because a mat3 will hold anything. Push it through a quaternion and the error is 0.5863 of a matrix entry — not a precision loss, a different basis.

The repair is not a wider type. It is one more link: 

// rover  world       = H · Rz(bank) · S
// unscale node local =                   S⁻¹        -> H · Rz(bank)
// pivot   node local =                        Rz(−bank) -> H

Two entities, each holding something a transform can genuinely express, and the hierarchy multiplies them parent-first — which is the order the derivation asked for in the first place. The harness checks that the chain reproduces the old basis: worst entry difference 0.000e+00, and the camera is where it always was.

The general statement, which is worth carrying into Module 8

T · R · S per node is a restriction, not a representation. The set of matrices a scene graph can express is not the set of affine matrices — and when you need one that is outside it, the answer is usually another node rather than a wider field. Real engines do exactly this, and this is why.

8.5 One decomposition, derived once, instead of three

Three places in this repository were pulling an affine matrix apart. Two of them were doing it with the mat3 trick and one — demos/gltf_view — had been doing it properly since Lesson 6.6. So the compile error did not just need fixing; it had found a function the engine was missing, written out three times with three different sets of defects. It is now in math/transform.hpp: 

struct transform_extraction
{
    transform value;
    float out_of_square = 0.0f;   ///< largest |cos| between two basis directions
    bool mirrored = false;        ///< the matrix had negative determinant
};

[[nodiscard]] inline transform_extraction transform_from_affine(const mat4& m);

The shape — a value plus what the recovery could not represent — is the one axis_angle_extraction (7.2) and euler_extraction (7.1) already use, and for the same reason: a recovery that can lose information should hand back the information about what it lost, in the same breath.

Three steps, and the second is the one nobody writes down.

  1. The scale is the column lengths. parent_from_local builds the matrix by scaling each basis column, and scaling a unit vector by s makes its length s. Reading the lengths back is the inverse of the construction, not an estimate of it.
  2. A negative determinant is a mirror, and no quaternion is one. Every rotation has determinant +1; an object scaled by (−1, 1, 1) has determinant −1 and is a reflection, which is not in the rotation group at all. Column lengths are non-negative by construction, so the sign has to be handed back deliberately. Skip this and a mirrored object silently becomes an unmirrored one that is rotated — the kind of bug that gets blamed on the artist.
  3. Divide the lengths out and extract.

8.6 A zero column, and an inherited defect

A degenerate object — scale 0 on an axis, which is how a sheet of paper or a collapsed node is authored — has no direction to read off that column. The version inherited from gltf_view substituted the parent's axis and said, correctly, that this "keeps the matrix finite instead of producing NaNs".

It does. It also produces a basis whose three columns are not perpendicular, and quat_from_rotation of a non-orthonormal basis is wrong in every column, not just the bad one. The harness measured it at 0.399 of absolute entry error on a flattened object whose other two axes were perfectly recoverable. The zero column is the one that does not matter — its scale multiplies it away. The two good ones are the ones that got broken.

A cross product of the surviving pair is the right answer and costs six multiplies: perpendicular to both by construction, unit because they are, and correctly handed because the order is cyclic. With the repair, the probe table is exact: 

   probe          worst entry   out of square  mirrored
   uniform 1      0.000e+00     1.892e-08      no
   uniform 2      0.000e+00     1.892e-08      no
   non-uniform    0.000e+00     1.892e-08      no
   mirrored x     0.000e+00     1.892e-08      yes
   flat z         0.000e+00     1.892e-08      no
"Finite" is not "right"

That comment had been in a published lesson's listing since 6.6, it was accurate about what it claimed, and the claim was the wrong one. Guarding against NaN is necessary and is not sufficient: a guard that returns a plausible wrong answer is harder to find than a NaN, because a NaN spreads and announces itself and a wrong basis just looks like the artist scaled something oddly.

8.7 What stays outside the struct, and is counted

One case genuinely cannot be decomposed: shear. A non-uniformly scaled parent with a rotated child produces a world matrix whose columns are not perpendicular, and that is not (rotation × axis-aligned scale) under any decomposition. Column lengths cannot even see it — a shear changes the angles between the columns and not their lengths — which is why out_of_square measures the angles instead.

collect_renderables counts those objects in renderable_report::skewed and says so in its warning line. Nothing is dropped and nothing goes NaN: the object draws in the nearest placement a transform can express, and the count turns "the crate looks subtly wrong and I cannot see why" into a number. Measured on a deliberate example — a parent scaled (2, 0.5, 1) with a child yawed 40° — the basis is 0.5941 out of square and the best available repair still leaves 0.3922 of entry error.

The honest fix is unchanged and unmade: scene_object should hold a mat4, which is what Module 6's GPU path already does with gpu_draw_item::world_from_model and what Lesson 5.9's hierarchy already computes. Two functions in this repository now take a matrix apart only to hand the pieces to parent_from_local, which puts it back together. That round trip is the argument. It is a change to a published struct with a dozen callers, so this lesson names it and does not smuggle it in — the same discipline 5.12 applied to view<const T>.

8.8 Did the picture move?

Every object in the software renderer now goes mat3 → quat → mat3 where it used to go nowhere. That is a real round trip with a sqrt and three divides in the middle, and the honest question is whether it moved a pixel.

Measured over 20,000 random poses, the worst matrix entry to survive the round trip differs by 5.960e−07. Put that in the units that decide whether a picture moves: a vertex 3 units from the origin, in a 960-pixel frame about 12 units wide, displaces by 0.00014 pixels.

And then check it rather than trusting the arithmetic. scratch/closure_75.py walks the include graph and reports that math/transform.hpp, math/quat.hpp, gfx/scene.hpp and demos/common/demo_scene.cpp are all in the golden render's closure — so the structural argument does not apply and the run is the only evidence there is: 

golden_75: rc=0  ours=1382416 bytes  reference=1382416 bytes
golden_75: identical=YES

Eight frames, 1,382,400 bytes, hash E917C06C — byte-identical, and identical to the reference Lesson 5.2 recorded. The engine changed how it stores every orientation in the scene and not one pixel moved.

11. Common pitfalls & debugging

SymptomCauseFix
A character spins nearly all the way round between two adjacent keyframes, once, and then carries on normally. The two keyframes are stored with opposite signs, and the blend has no shortest-arc comparison. q and −q are the same pose and opposite points. nearest(a, b) before the difference is formed. §6.3. Half of all pairs need it, so if you have hit it once you will hit it again.
A blend looks fine in the unit tests and lurches on screen. The tests sample t = 0, 0.5 and 1, which are precisely the three values at which nlerp and slerp are the same function. Sample t = 0.229 and 0.771, where the gap is maximal. §7.1.
An entire character vanishes for one frame. A NaN pose propagating down the skeleton. The textbook slerp divides by sin Ω, and Ω comes from an acos that returns exactly 0 below an arc of 0.0280°. Use the a·(a⁻¹b)ᵗ form, whose ratio sin(tθ/2)/sin(θ/2) tends to t and needs no guard. §7.4.
A path-length or error measurement reports a number that is impossible (a geodesic shorter than the straight line between its ends). An acos-based metric being asked for small angles. Its floor is 2√(2·eps) = 0.0560° and its error is biased, so it accumulates. Measure with atan2(|v|, |w|), or with a chord. §7.3. This is the fourth appearance of this trap in Module 7.
A test table shows a broken function scoring a perfect 0.0000e+00. The worst error is accumulated with std::max, and every comparison against NaN is false, so std::max(x, NaN) == x. Count non-finite results separately; never let one into a maximum. §7.4.
After the swap, an object is inside-out. A negative scale. The decomposition read the column lengths, which are non-negative, and threw the determinant's sign away. Check determinant(linear) < 0 and put the sign back on one scale component. §8.5, step 2.
A flattened object (scale 0 on one axis) comes back rotated as well as flat. The zero column was replaced by the parent's axis, which is not perpendicular to the other two, so the extraction is wrong in all three columns. Rebuild the missing column as the cross product of the surviving pair. §8.6.
A child object is subtly the wrong shape, and only when its parent is scaled. Shear. A non-uniformly scaled parent with a rotated child produces a world matrix that is not (rotation × axis-aligned scale), and no transform can hold it. Read renderable_report::skewed. Then either make the parent's scale uniform, or split the node in two (§8.4), or bake the scale into the mesh.
An orientation stepped every frame slowly stops being a rotation. Accumulated rounding. The field is an accumulator, so its own previous value is an input. renormalised_fast once per step — one subtract, five multiplies, no sqrt. Only for accumulators; a field rebuilt from an angle each frame needs nothing. §8.2.
The blend takes the right path but arrives early or late. nlerp on too large an arc. Its schedule error peaks at t ≈ 0.23 and 0.77. Under 73.50° of arc it stays within half a degree — use it. Over that, use slerp, or subdivide the arc. §5.4.

12. Exercises

  1. Take the long way on purpose. A turntable that has to spin 350° the long way cannot use quat_slerp, because nearest will turn it into 10° the short way. Write quat_slerp_long(a, b, t), which takes the other arc.

    Hint: it is quat_slerp with nearest(a, b) replaced by its opposite. Sketch: const quat away = -nearest(a, b); then the same three steps. Check with travelled() that the two versions sum to 360°, and think about what happens when the poses are identical. Lesson 7.3's Exercise 4 asked for the same function in the plane; compare the two and note that the plane's version had no sign to remove.

  2. Find the crossover for your own budget. §5.4 reports 73.50° for a half-degree budget. Write a loop that finds the arc for budgets of 0.1°, 0.25°, 1° and 2°, and plot arc against budget.

    Hint: the harness's H.3 does the 0.5° case; generalise it. What to notice: the relationship is not linear, and it is not quite a square root either. Derive why from §5.3's φ(t) and check your derivation against the table.

  3. Break the metric deliberately. Rebuild the harness with angle_between swapped for angle_between_by_cosine throughout, and record which checks fail and by how much.

    What to notice: §C's path integral fails loudly and §D's table barely moves, because D measures a separation and C measures a sum of steps. That is the whole of §7.3's lesson, arrived at from the other end: the formula is fine for the question 7.4 was asking and wrong for the question 7.5 asks.

  4. Interpolate a whole transform. Write transform_slerp(const transform& a, const transform& b, float t): lerp the position, quat_slerp the rotation, lerp the scale.

    Why it is not in the engine yet: nothing has asked for it. Lesson 7.7 will, when it blends two animation clips, and it will want to know whether scale should be lerped or geometrically interpolated (a·(b/a)ᵗ) — try both on a scale going from 1 to 8 and say which looks like a smooth growth.

  5. Make the shear visible. Build a scene with a parent scaled (2, 0.5, 1) and a child yawed 40°, and put a cube on the child. Read renderable_report::skewed, then render it twice — once through the software renderer (which goes through transform) and once through Module 6's GPU path (which keeps the mat4).

    What to notice: the two pictures differ, and the GPU one is the correct one. That is §8.7's argument as a pair of screenshots rather than a paragraph — and it is the strongest case for scene_object holding a matrix.

  6. Engine challenge: detect the sign problem at load time. Write a pass over an animation clip's quaternion keyframes that makes their signs consistent — for each keyframe after the first, negate it if its dot product with the previous one is negative.

    Why this is the real fix: nearest inside quat_slerp repairs the symptom at every call, for ever. One pass at load time repairs the cause once. Then ask the harder question: is the pass always correct? Construct a clip where it is not — one that genuinely rotates more than 180° between two keyframes — and say what an exporter should have done instead.

13. Recap & next

The engine can blend two orientations. That sentence is the whole of Module 7's purpose, and everything before it was construction: 7.1 showed why three angles cannot do it, 7.2 found the single turn and the geodesic, 7.3 proved in the plane that four components are forced, and 7.4 built them. This lesson spent three lines cashing that in, and the other five hours on the two things three lines do not cover.

What the engine gained:

  • quat_slerp and quat_nlerp, with nearest and quat_pow_unit underneath — the shortest arc, at constant speed, with no singularity and no small-angle special case.
  • A metric that works at the scale a path integral asks questions at, and the old one kept beside it as an instrument rather than deleted.
  • transform::rotation as four floats, which is what Lesson 7.6's skeleton will hold a hundred of per character.
  • transform_from_affine, and a runtime count of the objects whose placement the struct cannot express.

What was learned that nobody planned:

  • The double cover bounds the error. nearest forces the sphere arc under 90°, so sec²(Ω/2) ≤ 2 exactly and the positional gap ≤ 8.1491°. The plane, with no double cover, could reach 13,131 and 26.34°. The cost and the guarantee are the same fact.
  • An instrument has a scale. 2·acos|a·b| is the right formula for a separation and a broken one for a step, and the difference is not visible in the name, the type, or the doc comment. It failed slerp's own geodesic by 41%.
  • A NaN never wins a maximum, so a table of worst-case errors can certify a function that is failing in every sample.
  • "Finite" is not "right." A guard that returns a plausible wrong answer is harder to find than the NaN it prevented.
  • A narrower type finds existing bugs, not just future ones — three of them this time, one shipping in the engine since 5.11 and one in a published demo since 5.12.

Next: Lesson 7.6, skeletal animation and the skinning maths. A skeleton is a transform hierarchy — the one Lesson 5.9 built — with a hundred joints instead of six, and a pose is a hundred of the four-float rotations this lesson just made the engine store. The two things 7.6 needs from here are exactly the two things it now has: somewhere cheap to keep a pose, and a way to be between two of them. What it adds is the bind pose and the inverse bind matrix, which are the only genuinely confusing part of skinning and are confusing for a reason we can now name precisely: they are a change of basis, and Lesson 2.8's naming convention makes them read correctly the moment you write the space names down.

14. Further reading

  • Ken Shoemake, "Animating Rotation with Quaternion Curves", SIGGRAPH 1985. The paper that introduced slerp to graphics, and still the clearest statement of why the interpolation has to happen on the sphere. It is also where the trig form of §7.4 comes from, and reading it with this lesson's §7.4 in hand is instructive: the numerical objection is not the paper's fault, it is what happens when a formula written for exposition is typed into a float.
  • Jonathan Blow, "Understanding Slerp, Then Not Using It", Game Developer, 2004. The classic argument for nlerp, and worth reading against §5 and §6.4: it is right about the path, right about the cost, and predates the framing this lesson gives the schedule error — which now has a closed form and a hard bound.
  • David Eberly, "Quaternion Algebra and Calculus" (Geometric Tools). Careful, complete treatment of q^t, the logarithm and the exponential, including the small-angle analysis §3.2 waves at. The right next step if you want squad and higher-order curves.
  • Ken Shoemake, "Uniform Random Rotations", Graphics Gems III. The sampling used by §D and §E of this lesson's harness. Worth knowing because a "random rotation" generated from three uniform Euler angles is not uniform, and a worst-case measurement taken over a clustered sample understates the worst case.
  • Real-Time Rendering (4th ed.), §4.3. Quaternions, slerp, and the matrix conversions, with the standard formulas laid out for reference. Section 4.3.2 covers the q/−q choice in two sentences; this lesson's §6 is those two sentences with the measurements attached.
  • Ken Shoemake and Tom Duff, "Matrix Animation and Polar Decomposition", Graphics Interface 1992. What to do when §8.7's shear is not acceptable: polar decomposition splits any linear map into a rotation and a symmetric stretch, which is strictly more general than column lengths and costs an iteration. The right reading if Exercise 5 leaves you unsatisfied.
  • The SDL3 wiki on SDL_GetBasePath. Unrelated to rotation, and relevant every time the golden harness is run from the wrong directory (§10.4).
