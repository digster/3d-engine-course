Module 6 — Advanced Rendering · Lesson 6.1

Linear and sRGB: The Gamma Lesson

The lesson every graphics programmer needs exactly once and then has for ever — and it opens Module 6 for a reason that is not ceremonial: every lighting result in this module is wrong until the colour space is settled, so there is no point deriving a BRDF first. Lesson 1.6 gave you the idea. This lesson turns it into a pipeline, audits every conversion the engine performs, and finds that one of them has been missing since Module 4. The engine you have been looking at for nine lessons is too dark, by up to thirteen times in shadow, and the fix is one call.

1The Problem

Here is a claim, and it is about the code you have been running since Lesson 4.8:

The engine is displaying the wrong picture

Every pixel the GPU path has drawn since Module 4 has been too dark. At mid grey it is wrong by a factor of 2.3; in shadow, by thirteen. The software renderer, meanwhile, has been correct the whole time.

That is not a rhetorical opening. It is measured, in verify_61 §E, by rendering one quad of known brightness twice and reading back what was stored:

Sixty codes out of 256, on every pixel, for four modules. And nobody noticed — not while writing Module 4, not while porting the scene in 4.8, not while comparing the two renderers pixel by pixel in the same lesson. §6 explains exactly how a comparison that measured 87% byte-identical agreement between the two renderers managed to sit directly on top of this and report success.

The reason to open Module 6 here is that this is not a cosmetic defect you can come back to. Everything the module is about — a BRDF, an energy-conserving material, an image-based light, a bloom threshold — is arithmetic on quantities of light. If the number leaving your renderer is not a quantity of light, then a physically-based shading model is a physically-based calculation of the wrong thing, and it will be tuned by hand until it looks acceptable, which is how "PBR that never quite looks right" happens.

Lesson 1.6 introduced all of this and was honest about what it was leaving:

This lesson pays the first two clauses — decode once in, encode once out — and hands the other three to the HDR lesson later in this module, for reasons §8 gives. Along the way it does the thing that turns a rule into an engineering practice: it audits. Not "be careful about colour spaces" but "here are the eleven places this engine converts, here is what each one is for, and here is the one that is missing."

2Two Different Things Called Colour

2.1 Light adds; codes do not

Put two identical lamps in a room and turn them both on. The room is twice as bright. That sentence is true in the strongest possible sense: photons from one lamp and photons from the other simply coexist, and a sensor pointed at the wall receives the sum. Nothing in physics negotiates.

Everything a renderer computes lives in that world. A surface's albedo is a reflectance — the fraction of arriving light that leaves again — so it multiplies. Two lights add. A pixel covered half by a red triangle and half by a green one emits the average of their light. Lambert's cosine law is a multiplication; a specular lobe is a multiplication; an accumulation buffer is a sum. All of it is ordinary arithmetic, and it is only meaningful because the numbers are quantities of light.

Now look at the byte in your framebuffer. It is not a quantity of light. It is an index into a scale — a code that a display converts into light by a rule, and the rule is not the identity. That is the entire subject, and Figure 1 is it in one picture.

The two readings marked on that curve are worth memorising, because they turn a vague unease into two integers you can check against:

Two readings of the sRGB curve
QuestionIntuition saysThe truth
How much light does code 128 emit?half 0.2159 — about a fifth
What code emits half the light?128 188

Sixty codes apart. And notice the shape of the disagreement — the curve sags below the diagonal everywhere, and most in the darks. Any mistake made here will therefore be an error that is small near white and enormous in shadow, which is a shape worth carrying around because it is exactly the shape that hides.

2.2 Why an encoding exists at all

A reasonable objection: if light is the honest quantity, why is anything stored any other way? Why not just store light and be done?

The answer is a budget, and it is worth deriving rather than asserting, because the usual explanation — "CRTs had a nonlinear response" — is historically true and completely useless. CRTs are gone; the encoding is not, and it would have been invented anyway.

Start from how eyes work. Your ability to tell two intensities apart depends roughly on their ratio, not their difference. The step from 1% of white to 2% is obvious — it is a doubling. The step from 90% to 91% is invisible, and it is the same 1% of light. A useful encoding therefore spends its codes so that each step is a similar ratio, which means packing them close together in the darks and letting them spread out near white.

Count them, which verify_61 §B does rather than taking anybody's word for it. Of 256 codes, spent evenly in light:

How 256 codes are spent under two schemes
Codes falling in…evenly spaced in lightspaced by sRGB
the darkest tenth of the range2690
the brightest half of the range12868

The even scheme spends half its budget — 128 codes — on the top half of the range, where the eye can barely distinguish two neighbours, and leaves 26 for the darkest tenth, where it can distinguish dozens. The result is visible banding in every shadow and every gradient. Storing linear light in 8 bits needs roughly 12 bits to look as smooth as sRGB does in 8.

So the encoding is a compression scheme

It is perceptual compression, in the same family as everything else in a codec: spend bits where a person can tell, save them where nobody can. That framing is more useful than the CRT story because it tells you the encoding will still be here when displays change again — and it explains why HDR formats, which have bits to spare, use a different curve rather than none at all.

Which leaves the working rule, and it is a rule about where rather than about care:

The rule

Storage is encoded. Arithmetic is linear. Convert on the way in, convert on the way out, and do everything in between on real quantities of light.

3The Transfer Function

3.1 The definition, and its four constants

Here is the sRGB encoding function, from linear light to a stored value, both in [0, 1]. It is a definition rather than a derivation — a standards committee chose these numbers — so this course states it plainly and derives only the part that has a reason (§3.2), rather than pretending the constants fall out of something.

\[ \mathrm{encode}(x) = \begin{cases} 12.92\,x & x \le 0.0031308 \\[4pt] 1.055\,x^{1/2.4} - 0.055 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \]

In prose, so this survives the CDN being unreachable: below a linear value of 0.0031308 the encoded value is 12.92 times the linear one; above it, the encoded value is 1.055 times the linear value raised to the power 1/2.4, minus 0.055.

And the inverse, which is what a decode does:

\[ \mathrm{decode}(c) = \begin{cases} c / 12.92 & c \le 0.04045 \\[4pt] \left(\dfrac{c + 0.055}{1.055}\right)^{2.4} & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \]

In prose: below an encoded value of 0.04045 the linear value is the encoded one divided by 12.92; above it, the linear value is (c + 0.055) / 1.055, all raised to the power 2.4.

Four constants — 12.92, 0.0031308, 1.055 and 0.055 — and all four matter. They are not independent: 0.04045 is 12.92 × 0.0031308, and 1.055 and 0.055 are chosen so that the two pieces meet. verify_61 §A checks that they do, and reports the step at the join as 8.02 × 10⁻⁵, which is a fiftieth of a code — as close to continuous as a piecewise definition with rounded published constants can be.

3.2 Why there has to be a straight piece

The piecewise definition looks like a committee compromise. It is not: the straight piece is load-bearing, and the reason is one line of calculus.

Take the derivative of the power part near zero. For f(x) = x1/2.4,

\[ f'(x) = \tfrac{1}{2.4}\,x^{1/2.4 - 1} = \tfrac{1}{2.4}\,x^{-0.583} \]

In prose: the derivative is (1/2.4) times x to the power −0.583.

A negative exponent, so as x → 0 the derivative goes to infinity. A pure power curve has infinite gain at black. Three practical consequences follow immediately:

  • Noise is amplified without limit. A camera sensor's darkest readings are mostly noise, and an encoding with unbounded gain there turns that noise into visible, structured banding.
  • The inverse is numerically unstable. Decoding near zero divides by something approaching infinity; small errors in the encoded value become large errors in light.
  • Fixed-point arithmetic falls apart. An 8-bit encode must map many distinct linear values to code 0 or code 1, and with infinite slope the boundary between them is arbitrarily sensitive.

Replacing the bottom with a straight line of finite slope fixes all three at once. The slope, 12.92, is chosen so that the line and the curve meet smoothly at 0.0031308 — verify_61 §A measures it back out of the shipped function as 12.9200 to four decimals.

That slope has a consequence worth keeping, because it explains where every disagreement about this subject ends up: near black, one step of linear light crosses codes about twelve times faster than it does elsewhere. Lesson 4.8 met exactly this when it compared our encoder against the GPU's and found the floor was one code, worst in the darks. It is not that the darks are mysterious; it is that the function is steep there, so two implementations that round slightly differently part company sooner.

3.3 Numbers through it, by hand

Worked example — half the light, and half the code

What code stores half the light? Linear x = 0.5, which is well above the join, so use the power branch:

1.055 × 0.51/2.4 − 0.055 = 1.055 × 0.7402 − 0.055 = 0.7354

Multiply by 255 and round: 188. Not 128.

And what does code 128 emit? Encoded c = 128/255 = 0.5020, above 0.04045, so the power branch of the decode:

((0.5020 + 0.055) / 1.055)2.4 = (0.5280)2.4 = 0.2159

So the code everyone reaches for when they want "half" emits 21.6% of white's light. This is the single most useful pair of numbers in the whole subject: if a 50% grey looks too dark in your renderer, you have just found this bug.

Worked example — the toe, where the other branch lives

Linear x = 0.002, which is below 0.0031308, so the straight branch:

12.92 × 0.002 = 0.02584 → × 255 = 6.6 → code 7

Had we used the power branch anyway, we would get 1.055 × 0.0021/2.4 − 0.055 = 1.055 × 0.0705 − 0.055 = 0.0194, i.e. code 5. Two codes apart at a brightness where two codes is a 40% error in light. The toe is not a rounding detail.

3.4 Why not pow(x, 1/2.2)

You will see x1/2.2 everywhere — in tutorials, in shader snippets, in engine code. It is a different curve. It has no toe, its exponent is not 2.4, and it lacks the 1.055/0.055 scaling, so it does not even agree with sRGB in the middle where it is supposed to be a good approximation.

verify_61 §A sweeps a thousand linear values through both and reports the worst disagreement: 8 codes, near linear 0.0010 — down in the toe, exactly where §3.2 said the trouble would be, and exactly where the eye has the most codes to notice it with.

When the approximation is fine, stated honestly

If you are writing a colour grade for a stylised game and you want a rough brightness knob, x1/2.2 is perfectly serviceable. What it must never be is the output transfer function — because that one has to agree, to the code, with what every texture decode and every hardware sampler in the pipeline is doing. Two curves in one pipeline is a bug that appears only as a diff.

One more property, and it is the one that makes the whole scheme usable: decode-then-encode is lossless for all 256 codes. verify_61 §A checks every one and reports a worst error of zero. That matters because the engine converts in both directions constantly, and an encoding that lost a code per round trip would grind an image down every time it passed through.

4Two Conversions, at the Edges

Lesson 1.6 established the rule and then, being Module 1, applied it the only way it could: per operation. Blending two colours? Decode both, blend, encode. Fading? Decode, scale, encode. It said so at the time and called it what it was — slow, and lossy at 8 bits, because every intermediate result gets rounded back into a byte.

A pipeline does it differently, and the difference is not "more careful". It is structural: convert at the edges, and make everything in between a region where ordinary arithmetic is valid.

Three reasons this is better, and only one of them is speed:

  • It cannot be forgotten. Per-operation conversion is a rule every author of every new operation has to remember. Edge conversion is a property of the pipeline's shape — a new operation in the middle is automatically working on light, because there is nothing else there.
  • It does not round. Converting per operation means quantising to 8 bits after every step; a chain of five operations quantises five times. Converting once means one quantisation, at the end.
  • It is faster, but say this last and quietly, because it is the least important of the three and the easiest to over-claim.

So the question this lesson actually asks is narrow and answerable: where are this engine's edges, and does every one of them convert?

6The Bug, Measured

Reasoning is how you find a bug like this; measurement is how you are allowed to claim it. verify_61 §E renders one quad whose emitted light is exactly a value we choose, into two different target formats, and reads back what was stored.

Getting the light to be exactly a chosen number takes a small piece of experiment design worth naming: the key light is set to black, the albedo to white, and the specular to none, so the whole shading equation collapses to lit = base × (0 + ambient) = ambient. The question under test is the transfer function, and any shading arithmetic left in the path would be a second variable in a one-variable experiment.

Worked example — reading the ramp

Take the fourth column. The shader emits linear 0.20.

Before: written raw into a UNORM target, the stored code is round(0.20 × 255) = 51. A display treats 51 as an sRGB code, so it emits decode(51/255) = 0.0331 of white.

Asked for 0.20, got 0.0331. The picture is 6.0× too dark at that brightness.

After: the same shader into an _SRGB target stores 124, which is linear_to_srgb_u8(0.20) exactly — the number the CPU renderer has computed for the same light since Lesson 1.6.

First, the shape of the error. It is 13× at linear 0.02 and 1.1× at 0.95. It is a ratio, not an offset, so it is savage in shadow and nearly absent near white. Three implementations of one curve, in three languages, on two processors. A tighter result on an easier test is not a better result.)

6.1 How 4.8's comparison missed it

This deserves its own answer, because Lesson 4.8 spent a whole section comparing the two renderers pixel by pixel and reported 87% byte-identical. How does a comparison sit on top of a 60-code error and report agreement?

The harness rendered into an _SRGB target. So inside the test the encode happened — correctly, in hardware — and the two renderers genuinely did agree. The measurement was sound. It simply measured a configuration the shipped program did not use, because the shipped program renders to the swapchain and nobody had compared the two formats.

The lesson inside the lesson

A test that constructs its own environment is testing the environment it constructed. This one differed from production in a single enum, and that enum was the entire subject. When a harness sets up a resource the program also sets up, the two ways of setting it up are a thing to check — or better, the harness should ask the engine for it rather than writing its own literal.

Not a criticism of 4.8, which could not have known. It is the reason gpu_report now carries the answer as a field: a value the program computes and a harness can read is one that cannot silently differ.

7The Fix

7.1 Asking SDL for a linear swapchain

The right fix does not touch the shading at all. The shader is correct: it computes light and returns light. What is missing is the output edge, and SDL will provide it — the hardware has a transfer function in its write path, and using it costs nothing.

Four things about those eleven lines are worth saying out loud, because each is a habit rather than a detail.

Asked, never assumed. SDL guarantees only SDR; SDR_LINEAR may be refused. So we query support, attempt the change, and then read the format back rather than believing our own request took effect. On this machine the log now reads:

The answer becomes a field, not a comment. gpu_report::output_encodes_in_hardware is what a renderer reads to decide what to do. A comment saying "the swapchain is sRGB" would have been true on this machine and false on the next one; a field is true on both.

A predicate that two files need is not a private detail. is_srgb_format lived in an anonymous namespace in gpu_present.cpp while exactly one caller asked the question. There are two now — the present target, deciding whether its mirror texture must cancel the swapchain's decode, and the device, answering the field above — so it moved into the public header. Nothing about it changed except who can see it.

And a two-parameter setter must pass through the parameter it is not changing. set_present_mode() called SDL_SetGPUSwapchainParameters with a literal SDR, because when it was written that was the only composition there had ever been. 7.2 The fallback, and why it is second best

If SDR_LINEAR is refused, the swapchain's values are codes and somebody must encode. The only remaining somebody is the fragment shader.

Note that it is the exact piecewise curve, with the same four constants as engine::linear_to_srgb — not pow(x, 1/2.2). Two implementations of "the sRGB curve" that disagree produce a bug visible only in a diff, and §6's table is what proves these two do not.

The flag lives in the per-frame light uniform, and it cost nothing to add:

That slot was already there. It was pad2, and it existed because HLSL packs a float3 and a float into one 16-byte register — Lesson 4.6's packing rules. The padding was never wasted space; it was an unused field. The struct is still 64 bytes, every offset is unchanged, and 4.6's static_asserts did not move. A flag that costs zero bytes and zero repacking is one you can afford to read on every fragment.

The two right answers are not equally right

Hardware blending happens after the fragment shader. If the shader returns an encoded value, the blend unit is adding and interpolating codes — which is the exact mistake §2 is about, moved one stage later. It is correct for opaque geometry and wrong the moment anything is transparent.

The _SRGB target has no such problem: the blend happens in linear and the encode happens on the write, after it. So prefer the swapchain, keep the fallback, and know which one you are on — which is what the field is for.

The software path needed no change at all, and that is worth a sentence rather than silence. Its output edge — to_encoded in raster.cpp — has been correct since Lesson 1.6. The framebuffer's mirror texture does change format, because gpu_present.cpp matches it to the swapchain so that a decode on read cancels an encode on write; that logic was written in Lesson 4.2 and anticipated exactly this, so the fix cost it nothing. One small thing did improve for free: when the framebuffer is scaled up with SDL_GPU_FILTER_LINEAR, the interpolation now happens in linear light rather than between codes.

8What This Lesson Does Not Do

Lesson 1.6's note listed five things a real pipeline needs. This lesson delivered two — decode once in, encode once out — and deliberately left three. Naming them is part of the deliverable, because a reader who thinks colour is now "done" will be surprised twice.

8.1 Headroom above 1.0, and a float framebuffer

The output of the shading equation is clamped to [0, 1] because that is all an 8-bit target can hold. But real light does not stop at 1: a lamp seen directly, a specular highlight on chrome, the sun through a window are all far above it. Clamping them throws that information away before anything can use it, which is why the picture cannot yet have a bloom that means anything.

Fixing that means a floating-point render target and a whole extra stage, and the stage has a name — tonemapping — and its own lesson later in this module. Adding half of it here would mean building a float target with nothing to do with the extra range.

8.2 The software renderer's per-operation conversions

§5's three ⚠ rows are still per-operation. They are correct, and making them a pipeline means giving the software renderer a linear float framebuffer — the same change as §8.1, on the other path. It buys accuracy that nothing currently measures, and it is a large change to raster.cpp. The debt is real and it is smaller than it was; it is not urgent.

8.3 Colour spaces, as opposed to transfer functions

Everything above is about the transfer function — the curve between light and code. sRGB also specifies primaries: which physical red, green and blue the three channels mean. Two images can share a transfer function and still disagree about what "pure red" is, which is what a wide-gamut display exposes.

This course stays in sRGB primaries throughout and says so here rather than leaving you to discover the distinction from a bug report. If you go on to HDR displays or film work, the chapter you want next is about primaries and chromatic adaptation, and it is a genuinely separate subject from this one.

A prediction that was wrong, and why that is the finding

The plan for this lesson, written at the end of Module 5, said the golden was “probably over” — a lesson that settles colour would surely change the reference render, and the instruction was to re-baseline deliberately rather than quietly.

It did not change, and the reason is precisely the result: the software renderer was already right. A prediction that fails for a reason you can state is worth more than one that succeeds.

11Common Pitfalls & Debugging

Everything is too dark, worst in the shadows

Symptom. The image looks moody and underexposed. Bright surfaces are nearly right; dark ones are crushed. Turning the light up makes the bright parts blow out before the dark parts arrive.

Cause. Linear light is reaching an sRGB-encoded destination without being encoded. This lesson's bug.

Fix. Find the output edge. On SDL_GPU, print the swapchain format: no _SRGB means the shader must encode. Confirm with the arithmetic before changing anything — should mid grey be 128 or 188?

Everything is washed out and milky

Symptom. Blacks are grey, the image looks foggy, contrast is gone.

Cause. The same mistake with the sign flipped: encoding twice. An _SRGB target and a shader that encodes; or a texture loaded as _SRGB whose contents were already linear.

Fix. Count the conversions along one path and make it exactly one in each direction. verify_61 §C reports the double-encode of 0.5 as 0.7354 → 0.8732, so the fingerprint is quantifiable rather than a matter of taste.

A normal map or roughness texture produces nonsense

Symptom. Lighting is subtly wrong in a way that changes with the texture rather than the light. Normals point oddly; a roughness map behaves as if it had been contrast-boosted.

Cause. The texture was loaded as _SRGB. A normal map is not a colour — it is three numbers — and decoding it applies a curve to data that was never encoded.

Fix. Lesson 4.7's rule, and it is one of the commonest material-system mistakes: _SRGB for colours, _UNORM for numbers. Albedo and emissive are colours; normal, roughness, metalness, occlusion and masks are not.

Fades and gradients look muddy in the middle

Symptom. A black-to-white fade spends too long dark and then rushes; a red-to-green blend passes through a dark olive rather than a bright yellow.

Cause. Interpolating codes instead of light. This is the per-operation failure, and it is why mix_linear exists.

Fix. Decode, interpolate, encode. And note the honest exception Lesson 1.6 recorded: a UI gradient an artist tuned by eye may genuinely mean the encoded blend. Ask what the numbers measure.

Red and blue are swapped and you suspect gamma

Symptom. The image is wrong in colour, and the green channel looks fine.

Cause. Channel order, not gamma. A transfer-function mistake affects all three channels identically, because it is a curve applied per channel.

Fix. Green being right is the tell. Go and look at your pixel format (Lesson 1.6's fingerprint list, and Lesson 4.2's ARGB8888 byte order).

Toggling vsync makes the picture go dark again

Symptom. Correct at start-up, wrong after a settings change.

Cause. SDL_SetGPUSwapchainParameters sets composition and present mode together. A function that changes one of them and passes a literal for the other silently resets it.

Fix. Carry the value through — report_.composition, not SDL_GPU_SWAPCHAINCOMPOSITION_SDR. This engine had exactly this bug latent in set_present_mode() until this lesson.

Your test passes and the program is still wrong

Symptom. A harness compares two renderers and reports agreement, while the shipped program looks wrong.

Cause. The harness built its own render target and chose a different format from the one production uses. Lesson 4.8, exactly.

Fix. Have the harness ask the engine for the configuration rather than writing its own literal — and where it cannot, make the production value a readable field so the difference can be asserted on.

12Exercises

  1. 12.1 — Put the bug back, on purpose. Add a runtime toggle that forces SDR and leaves encode_output at 0, and flip it while looking at the scene. Then write down, before you look, which parts of the image you expect to change most.

    Hint

    You cannot change a swapchain's composition per frame cheaply, but you can leave the composition alone and lie about the flag — set encode_output to 0 on an _SRGB target and you get the same too-dark result, because the shader hands over a value the hardware then encodes a second time. Predict that too.

    Solution sketch

    The prediction that matters is where: shadowed sides of objects and the dark background change enormously, specular highlights barely at all. If your intuition said "everything gets darker by the same amount", Figure 5 is the correction — and it is worth noticing that this is the same reason the bug survived four modules.

  2. 12.2 — Audit something else. Run §5's grep against a codebase you did not write — an open-source renderer, or your own older project — and build the same table. Every conversion gets a job: input edge, output edge, or per-operation.

    Hint

    Search for the words rather than the functions: srgb, gamma, 2.2, 2.4, 0.0031308, SRGB in format enums, and pow( near a colour. The constant 2.2 in a shader is almost always worth reading twice.

    Solution sketch

    The interesting outcome is not "found a bug" — it is discovering how many codebases have three conversions on one path and stay correct by cancellation. Those are the ones that break when somebody adds a post-processing pass, because the third conversion was load-bearing and nobody knew.

  3. 12.3 — Measure the code budget yourself. Render a black-to-white gradient across 256 pixels twice: once with the code stepping evenly, once with the light stepping evenly and the codes computed by linear_to_srgb_u8. Look at both on a real display.

    Hint

    Use the software renderer and --shot, so you can look at the file as well as the screen. Count the distinct codes in the darkest tenth of each.

    Solution sketch

    The evenly-stepping-light ramp bands visibly in the darks — 26 codes across a range where the eye wants dozens — while the evenly-stepping-code ramp looks smooth. That is §2.2's argument as a picture, and it is the most direct demonstration that the encoding is a perceptual compression scheme rather than a historical accident.

  4. 12.4 — Break the blend. Add a transparent surface to the GPU scene and render it twice: once on an _SRGB swapchain, once with the shader encoding into a UNORM target. Compare the blended region.

    Hint

    You need alpha blending enabled in the pipeline — Lesson 4.4's SDL_GPUColorTargetBlendState. Make the transparent surface 50% over a dark background, where the disagreement is largest.

    Solution sketch

    The shader-encoded version blends codes, so a 50% blend over black gives roughly 0.5 × 188 = 94, which decodes to about 0.11 of white rather than the 0.25 that half of half the light should be. This is §7.2's warning as a measurement, and it is why "encode in the shader" is the fallback rather than the plan.

  5. 12.5 — The float target, early. Render the scene into an R16G16B16A16_FLOAT target instead of the swapchain, then copy it to the screen with a trivial pass that just encodes. Nothing should change visually — and that is the point.

    Hint

    Two pipelines and one extra pass. The scene pipeline's colour target format becomes the float one; the copy pass samples it and writes to the swapchain.

    Solution sketch

    You have just built the skeleton of the HDR pipeline, and the interesting part is that values above 1.0 now survive to the copy pass instead of being clamped at the shader's return. Print the maximum: on a scene with a strong highlight it is well above 1. That number is what tonemapping is for, and it is the lesson this one hands off to.

13Recap & Next

The engine's output stage is now correct on both surfaces, and the reason it was not is a better lesson than the fix.

  • A stored channel is a code, not a quantity of light, and the curve between them is not the identity: code 128 emits 0.2159 of white, and half the light is code 188.
  • The encoding is a budget, not a historical accident. 90 codes in the darkest tenth against 26 — perceptual compression that would be invented today if it did not exist.
  • The transfer function is piecewise for a reason: a pure power curve has infinite slope at zero, which amplifies noise without limit and cannot be inverted stably. The toe is 12.92·x, and skipping it costs 8 codes where the eye is sharpest.
  • Convert twice, at the edges. Not "carefully" — twice. Everything between the two conversions is light, and ordinary arithmetic is valid there because of where the conversions are, not because anybody remembered.
  • The audit found a hole: the GPU path had an input edge and no output edge. SDL claims windows with a swapchain whose "pixel values are in sRGB encoding", and our shader returned light.
  • The error is a ratio — 13× in shadow, 1.1× near white — which is why four modules of looking at the picture did not find it, and why a comparison in Lesson 4.8 sat on top of it and reported agreement.
  • The fix is one call, asked and not assumed, with the answer recorded as a field so a renderer and a harness cannot disagree about it.

34 checks green in debug and release; verify_45 through verify_511 still green; and the reference shot byte-identical for the twelfth lesson — which this lesson's own plan predicted it would not be, for a reason that turned out to be exactly backwards.

Next: 6.2 — What a BRDF Is. Now that the numbers leaving the renderer are quantities of light, it is worth asking what quantity, precisely. Lambert has been standing in since Lesson 3.6 with a comment admitting it is a convenient guess; the next lessons replace the guess with radiometry — what a unit of light actually is, what a surface does to it, and what it means for a material to conserve energy. Cook–Torrance follows, derived rather than quoted, with its three terms visualised separately.

None of that would have meant anything an hour ago. A BRDF is a ratio of two quantities of light, and until this lesson the quantity leaving this engine was not one.
