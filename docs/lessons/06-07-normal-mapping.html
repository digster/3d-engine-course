Module 6 — Advanced Rendering · Lesson 6.7

Normal Mapping and the TBN Derivation

Every surface this engine has ever drawn is exactly as flat as its triangles. The fix is not more geometry — it is to lie about the normal, per pixel, from an image. But a stored direction is meaningless without a frame to express it in, and finding that frame is the whole lesson: T and B are whatever two vectors make the triangle's edges and its uv deltas agree, which is two equations in two unknowns and nothing more mysterious. Along the way the engine finally answers a question Lesson 6.6 had to walk away from — is this image colour, or is it data? — and the answer turns out to have been sitting in the GPU path since Lesson 4.7, with no counterpart on the CPU side at all.

1The Problem: Flat Is All We Have

Run gltf_view --model torus.obj as Lesson 6.6 left it. The torus is smooth, lit, and — if you look at it for a moment — completely characterless. It is a shape, not a surface. There is no grain, no pitting, no weave, nothing that says what it is made of.

The engine has exactly one lever for that, and it is the wrong one: add triangles. Lesson 3.6 established that shading is a function of the normal, Lesson 3.8 made it per-pixel, and Lessons 6.2 through 6.4 built a physically-based BRDF on top — but every one of those consumes a normal that came from the geometry. To make a surface look bumpy, the geometry has to be bumpy.

Price that. The torus is 2,304 triangles and reads as smooth at arm's length. To give it visible surface detail — a fabric weave, say, at a millimetre scale — you would need triangles at a millimetre scale, which on an object this size is tens of millions. For one prop. The vertex buffer alone would be hundreds of megabytes, and the rasterizer would spend all of it on triangles smaller than a pixel, which is the worst case for every renderer ever built.

So: do not add the geometry. Add the normal. Shading only ever consults the normal, so a surface that reports a bumpy normal will shade exactly as though it were bumpy — and reporting takes one texture lookup instead of ten million triangles.

What this trick cannot do, said up front

A normal map lies to the shading and to nothing else. The silhouette stays smooth, because the silhouette is geometry. Bumps do not occlude one another, because occlusion is geometry. Nothing casts a shadow onto anything else on the same surface, and at a grazing angle the illusion collapses entirely — you are looking along a flat plane that claims to be rough.

Those are real limits with real names, and each has a technique that addresses it: parallax mapping moves the sample point to fake the occlusion, and displacement mapping gives up and moves the vertices. Both are out of scope here. This is the ninety-percent picture, and the missing ten percent is the silhouette.

2The Idea, and Why It Needs a Frame

Storing a normal per texel sounds like the whole design, and it nearly is. The problem is which normal.

The obvious version — store the world-space normal directly — fails immediately, and it fails in a way worth understanding rather than just avoiding. A world-space normal map is correct for exactly one orientation of the object. Rotate the torus and every normal in the image is now wrong; the lighting slides around the surface as if the bumps were painted on the world rather than on the object. And the same map could never be shared between two objects, or tiled, or mirrored, because it encodes where the surface is rather than what it is like.

The insight the whole technique rests on

Store the normal relative to the surface, not relative to the world.

"Tilted a bit toward increasing u, and a bit toward increasing v" is a statement that stays true when the object rotates, that means the same thing on the left half of a model and the right, and that can be tiled across a wall. It is a statement about the material, which is exactly what a texture ought to be.

And now the whole difficulty of this lesson appears in one sentence. If the map says "tilted toward increasing u", the renderer has to know which way in world space u increases — and that is not something the geometry has ever been asked before. A mesh knows where its vertices are and which way its normals point. Nowhere does it record the direction, in metres, that you would walk to make u go up.

That direction is the tangent, conventionally T. Its partner, the direction of increasing v, is the bitangent, B. Together with the normal N they form an orthonormal frame — the TBN basis — and a normal map's three numbers are coordinates in it.

A naming annoyance worth clearing up once

You will see B called the binormal in older material, including some very good older material. It is a misnomer borrowed from differential geometry, where a curve's Frenet frame genuinely has a binormal. A surface does not have one; what it has is a second tangent. Bitangent is the correct word and the one glTF uses, and the only reason to know "binormal" is so that you recognise it in somebody else's code.

So the plan for the rest of this lesson has three parts, and they are genuinely independent: work out what the numbers in the image mean (§3), work out what the frame is (§4–§6), and get the frame from the vertex to the fragment through two renderers (§8–§9).

3Colour, or Data? The Question 6.6 Deferred

Lesson 6.6 read a glTF material's normalTexture slot, counted it, and did not load it. The reason it gave was one sentence, and it is time to pay for it:

Every texture in this engine before now has been an albedo — an authored colour, sRGB-encoded like every stored colour since Lesson 1.6 — so sample could decode unconditionally and be right every single time. A normal map breaks that. Its three channels are a direction, packed into bytes. Running a direction through the sRGB transfer function is arithmetic on numbers that were never a colour.

How wrong? One number settles it, and it is the number the whole section turns on.

The byte 128 is what a flat normal map stores for x and y — "no tilt in either direction". Read as data it means 0.50196, which maps to a tilt of essentially zero. Read as a colour it means 0.21586, which maps to −0.568 — so the "flat" direction becomes (−0.568, −0.568, 1), and every surface in the scene is tilted 38.8°, in the same direction, everywhere.

And it does not look like a bug

38.8° of uniform tilt does not produce a black screen or a crash. It produces lighting that is subtly, consistently wrong — every surface behaving as though the light were somewhere slightly different from where it is.

The usual diagnosis is "this normal map was authored too strong", and the usual fix is to reduce its strength, which makes the picture less wrong without making it right. That is why this is worth a type rather than a comment.

Where the colour space belongs

Three places it could live, and this decision has to be made once, because both normal maps and the still-deferred metallic-roughness maps read through it. A per-call answer is two answers.

Two halves of one idea, and no path between them

The GPU has had a colour space since Lesson 4.7 and the software renderer has never had the concept at all. In SDL_GPU the decode is declared by the texture's format — _UNORM against _UNORM_SRGB — and performed by the sampler. One image cannot be sRGB in one binding and linear in another, because the format is a property of the memory.

This is the same shape of gap Lesson 6.6 found between load_image and sample: one half complete, the other absent, and no code path crossing between them until a feature needed both. It is worth writing down as a habit — look for pairs of subsystems that ought to compose and have never been asked to.

And it settles the design without a preference: Lesson 3.9 built these types to mirror SDL_GPU's "so that when Module 4 replaces this file with a GPU sampler object, the change is a rename and not a re-education". Mirroring includes mirroring where a decision lives. It goes on the texture.

The whole cost of it is one branch, in the one function every read already goes through — which is the argument for putting it there rather than at the call site. There is exactly one fetch, so there is exactly one place the question can be asked, and no caller can forget to ask it:

The branch is on a value that is constant for an entire draw, so it predicts perfectly. And the default is srgb, which keeps every texture written before this lesson meaning exactly what it meant — a default that changed the answer would have made this lesson a re-baseline instead of an addition.

The space is part of the asset's identity

One consequence that falls straight out of Lesson 5.5. The same PNG read as colour and read as data are two different textures with two different texel meanings; they cannot share a cache entry, and a store keyed on the filename alone would hand the second requester the first one's decode and be quietly wrong.

That is exactly what mesh_import established for geometry — "these are part of an asset's IDENTITY, not arguments to a function" — arriving for a second type. So load_texture takes the space, the key carries it, and 5.5's rule that the default serialises to nothing applies unchanged:

4Deriving the Tangent Frame

Here is the part that usually arrives as a formula to be copied. It is four lines of algebra and it follows from one observation, so there is no reason to copy anything.

Take a triangle. Its three corners have positions p0, p1, p2 in metres, and texture coordinates w0, w1, w2 in texture units. Two edges from the first corner describe the triangle in space:

\[ e_1 = p_1 - p_0, \qquad e_2 = p_2 - p_0 \]

…and the corresponding uv differences describe the same two directions in the chart:

\[ (\Delta u_1, \Delta v_1) = w_1 - w_0, \qquad (\Delta u_2, \Delta v_2) = w_2 - w_0 \]

That correspondence is the entire content of the derivation, so it is worth saying slowly. e1 and (Δu1, Δv1) are two descriptions of one walk across the surface — one measured in metres, one in texture units. If T is the world direction you move when u increases by one, and B is the direction for v, then walking Δu1 in u and Δv1 in v must land you exactly where e1 lands you:

\[ \begin{aligned} e_1 &= \Delta u_1\,T \;+\; \Delta v_1\,B \\ e_2 &= \Delta u_2\,T \;+\; \Delta v_2\,B \end{aligned} \]

Two equations, two unknowns. That is all a tangent frame is — and the whole reputation of this subject for being fiddly comes from being shown the answer rather than the question.

Solving is inverting a 2×2. Write the system as a matrix equation with T and B as the unknowns:

\[ \begin{bmatrix} e_1 \\ e_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \Delta u_1 & \Delta v_1 \\ \Delta u_2 & \Delta v_2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} T \\ B \end{bmatrix} \]

and multiply through by the inverse. With det = Δu1Δv2 − Δu2Δv1:

\[ \begin{aligned} T &= \tfrac{1}{\det}\big(\Delta v_2\,e_1 - \Delta v_1\,e_2\big) \\ B &= \tfrac{1}{\det}\big(\Delta u_1\,e_2 - \Delta u_2\,e_1\big) \end{aligned} \]
Worked example — the triangle verify_67 §B uses

p0 = (0, 0, 0), p1 = (2, 0, 0), p2 = (0, 0, −2), lying flat in the xz plane so N = (0, 1, 0). Texture coordinates (0, 0), (1, 0) and (0, 1).

e1 = (2, 0, 0) and e2 = (0, 0, −2). The uv deltas are (1, 0) and (0, 1), so the 2×2 is the identity and det = 1.

T = 1·(2,0,0) − 0·(0,0,−2) = (2, 0, 0), which normalises to (1, 0, 0).
B = 1·(0,0,−2) − 0·(2,0,0) = (0, 0, −2), which normalises to (0, 0, −1).

Sanity-check it against the picture: the triangle's u runs along +x, so the direction of increasing u is +x. It does. And v runs toward −z, so B points that way. It does. verify_67 §B gets exactly these to six decimals.

What the determinant is, and why zero is not a bug

det is twice the signed area the triangle occupies in the uv chart — which is the same signed-area quantity Lesson 2.4 derived barycentric coordinates from, applied to a different pair of axes.

So det = 0 means the triangle has no area in texture space: three corners on one line of the chart, or all three at the same point. That is not a degenerate input to be guarded against — it is an untextured face, or a collapsed unwrap, and both are ordinary. There is genuinely no tangent frame to compute, so the face contributes nothing and its vertices fall back to whatever their other faces say:

Dividing anyway would put an infinity into every vertex the face touches and spread it through the average — so a single untextured face would poison a region of an otherwise fine model. verify_67 §B feeds a triangle whose three uvs are collinear and asserts the output is finite.

And it is why cube.gltf looks odd under a normal map

Lesson 6.6 gave assets/cube.gltf one uv per box corner, so that the round-trip test in §A could compare index-for-index against cube_mesh(). That was the right call for that test and it has a consequence nobody could see until now: on four of the cube's six faces, u is constant — so those faces have zero uv area and no tangent frame at all.

Normal-map the cube and the result is banded rather than dimpled, because only two faces have a chart. That is not a bug in this lesson; it is a property of that asset, which normal mapping is simply the first thing to make visible. Use --model torus.obj for a mesh with a real chart on every triangle.

From per-face to per-vertex

The solve gives a frame per triangle, and a vertex shared by several triangles needs one answer. The same treatment with_normals gives normals applies without a word of new argument: accumulate, then normalise.

And the weighting is free, for the reason Lesson 3.6 gave about area-weighted normals. The solved T is not normalised before accumulation, and its magnitude scales with the triangle's own size — so summing the raw solutions weights each face by its area without a single extra multiply.

Then one step that has no counterpart in the normal case:

The accumulated tangent is an average of what several faces wanted, and the accumulated normal is an average of what several faces wanted, and there is no reason the two averages should be perpendicular. Subtracting the component along N leaves what lies in the tangent plane.

Note which vector is held fixed. The normal is what we refuse to move, because it is what the lighting is actually about; the tangent only has to span the plane, so it is the one that gets adjusted. Doing it the other way round would tilt the shading normal to accommodate a texture coordinate, which is precisely backwards.

5The Fourth Component

mesh::tangents is a span of vec4, not vec3, and the fourth component is not padding. It is the frame's handedness: +1 or −1.

Once N and T are known, the bitangent is almost determined — it is perpendicular to both, so it is cross(N, T) up to a sign. Which sign is not determined by the geometry at all. It is determined by the uv chart, and a chart can be mirrored.

Every symmetric model has a mirrored chart

An artist unwrapping a character does not unwrap both arms. They unwrap one, reflect it, and lay both halves on the same region of texture — which halves the texture memory and means a detail painted once appears on both sides.

On the reflected half, u increases in the opposite direction, so the frame comes out left-handed where the other half is right-handed. That is not a defect in the model; it is the point of mirroring.

So the sign has to be recorded, and it is recorded by testing the solved B against the one cross(N, T) would produce:

Rerun §4's worked example with u negated — texture coordinates (0,0), (−1,0), (0,1) — and the arithmetic falls out: det = −1, so T = (−1, 0, 0) and B = (0, 0, −1). But cross(N, T) is now (0, 0, +1), which disagrees with B. Handedness −1. verify_67 §C asserts exactly that.

Why store a sign instead of the bitangent

Twelve bytes a vertex to save one cross product a fragment is a poor trade on its own. The decisive argument is different: a stored bitangent can disagree with the N and T beside it, and a recomputed one cannot.

After interpolation across a triangle, three separately-interpolated vectors are no longer mutually perpendicular and no longer unit length. Recomputing B from the N and T you actually have guarantees a frame that is consistent with itself. This is the same argument Lesson 6.5 made for deriving material::textured(): a value you compute cannot contradict the value it came from.

glTF stores it this way too (§3.7.2.1: "the w component […] is 1 or −1 and indicates the handedness of the tangent basis"), which is a useful independent vote.

Get the sign wrong and the failure is specific and recognisable: the lighting on the mirrored half of a model is the mirror image of the lighting on the other half. Bumps lit from the left on one arm are lit from the right on the other. verify_67 §C measures it — the two handednesses give normals whose bitangent components are exact negatives — and it reads as a modelling error rather than a shading one, which is why it survives.

6The Basis Change, and the Lavender

With the frame in hand the fragment's job is four lines. Sample, decode, build, multiply.

The decode is the step worth pausing on, because it explains something you have already seen a hundred times without being told why.

A direction has negative components. A byte does not. So the encoding is an offset — the stored value is (c + 1) / 2 and the decode is c = 2·value − 1. Which means the direction (0, 0, 1) — "no change at all" — is stored as (0.5, 0.5, 1.0), or (128, 128, 255) in bytes.

That is why every normal map is lavender

The pale blue-violet you see in every normal map ever authored is not a convention somebody chose, or a tint applied for recognisability. It is arithmetic. It is what "this surface is flat here" looks like when a signed direction is packed into unsigned bytes, and the more nearly flat a region is the more nearly that exact colour it becomes.

Which also tells you how to read one at a glance: reddish means tilted toward +u, greenish means tilted toward +v, and the strength of the tint is the steepness.

Then the basis change itself, and it is a matrix multiply written as its own definition. Lesson 2.5 established that a matrix is where the basis vectors land — and here T, B and N are the basis vectors, so the multiply is:

\[ n_{\text{world}} \;=\; T\,x \;+\; B\,y \;+\; N\,z \]

Read the z term and the round trip is obvious: a flat map has x = y = 0 and z = 1, so the result is N exactly. Nothing changes. That single property is the most valuable test in this lesson, because every mistake it is possible to make here breaks it — a wrong colour space, a wrong decode range, a frame that is not orthonormal, a bitangent with the wrong sign, a basis change written in the wrong order, a tangent that was never re-orthogonalised. One assertion, six bugs.

And the round trip is not exact, which is the finding

verify_67 §D asserted worst < 1e-6 on its first run and measured 5.5460 × 10−3. The renderer was right and the assertion was wrong, for a reason worth considerably more than the check:

0.5 is not an 8-bit code

The codes either side are 127 and 128, giving 0.498 and 0.502. Take 128 — the conventional choice — and decode it: 128/255 × 2 − 1 is 1/255, not zero.

So the flattest normal map that can be stored encodes the direction (0.0039, 0.0039, 1), and every flat normal map in existence tilts its surface by 0.318°. Not the ones authored badly. All of them.

That is why 16-bit normal maps exist, and why you will find pipelines that argue about 127 against 128 — neither is exactly right, and the argument is about which direction to be wrong in.

The fix in the harness is not to loosen the tolerance — Lesson 6.3's rule is that a tolerance widened to admit a failure is a test deleted slowly. It is to predict the floor from the encoding and assert the measurement equals it:

Which is a strictly stronger test than the one that failed: it now catches an implementation that is better than the quantisation floor as well as one that is worse, and either would mean the encoding is not what we think it is.

7Which Matrix Carries a Tangent

The frame is computed in model space and used in world space, so something has to carry it across. Lesson 3.6 established that a normal is not carried by the model matrix — it needs the inverse transpose, because a non-uniform scale does not preserve perpendicularity. The obvious move is to use that matrix for the tangent too.

It is wrong, and the reason is the same reason, arriving on the other side of it.

The distinction in one sentence each

A normal is defined by being perpendicular to the surface. Perpendicularity is the property that a non-uniform scale destroys, and the inverse transpose is exactly the correction that restores it.

A tangent lies in the surface. It is the direction the surface goes as u increases — which makes it a difference of positions, and a difference of positions transforms precisely the way positions do.

verify_67 §F establishes that rather than asserting it, by transporting two nearby points through the model matrix and subtracting — which is what "difference of positions" means operationally. Under a scale of (2, 1, 1) applied to a tangent at 45°:

The two candidate transforms for a tangent under a non-uniform scale, against the ground truth
RouteResultAgainst the truth
the model matrix(0.894, 0.447, 0) 0.0 — exact
the normal matrix(0.447, 0.894, 0) 0.632
…which is 36.9° apart, on one object.

Note what makes the wrong answer dangerous rather than obvious: both results still lie in the surface. The frame is not broken, it is skewed — the normal map's bumps are rotated within the plane — and a skewed normal map reads as an asset that was authored at the wrong angle.

And, exactly as with the normal-matrix bug that Lesson 3.6 put on a key: under a uniform scale the two agree to 8.4 × 10−8. An engine that gets this wrong looks perfect on every object nobody stretched, which is most of them.

8Implementation

8.1 — A fourth varying, through four places

"What a vertex carries" is written down in three separate places in this engine, and a tangent has to be threaded through all of them plus the clipper:

The clipper is the one people forget. A triangle cut by the near plane gets new corners that were never in the mesh, and there is nowhere to get their tangent from except interpolation — so it is one more line beside the normal's, lerped with the same crossing parameter (Lesson 3.3).

And w is lerped too, which is the interesting case

Across an ordinary triangle both corners carry the same handedness and the interpolation is the identity. Across one that spans a mirroring seam they carry +1 and −1, the interpolated value passes through zero, and the frame collapses in the middle of the triangle.

That is a real artefact and it is not the clipper's to fix: an exporter splits the vertices at such a seam, exactly as it does at a uv seam. Clamping the sign here would hide it while leaving the tangent itself interpolated through the same degeneracy.

8.3 — The fragment

The Gram-Schmidt appears again, here at the fragment, having already been done at the vertex — and that is not redundancy. The vertex pass makes the frame orthonormal at the vertices; interpolation across the triangle destroys both properties again, exactly as it does for the normal (Lesson 3.8 measured that). The per-vertex pass is what makes the inputs sane; the per-fragment pass is what makes the frame sane.

And the guard on the whole branch is worth reading, because it needed no new flag:

Geometry with no tangents cannot be normal mapped, because tangent space is the uv parameterisation. The zero xyz that tangent_at returns for a mesh without them is what makes that checkable here rather than needing a flag alongside — the same trick normal_at has used since Lesson 3.6.

8.4 — A normal map with an analytic answer

The test image is generated rather than painted, for the reason Lesson 3.9 generated its checkerboard and 6.6 generated its glTF: the course ships no third-party assets, and every number a lesson quotes has to be reproducible from the repository alone.

It is an egg-carton — a height field whose normal has a closed form, so verify_67 §E can compute the right answer and compare rather than looking at it:

\[ k = 2\pi c, \qquad h(u,v) = \tfrac{s}{k}\cos(ku)\cos(kv), \qquad \mathbf{n} \propto (-\partial_u h,\; -\partial_v h,\; 1) \]
The 1/k, and the build where it was not there

The first version used s as the amplitude directly. A·cos(ku)cos(kv) has a peak gradient of A·k, and k grows with the cell count — so an amplitude of 1 at six cells is a slope of 37.7, a surface tilted 88° everywhere. Every normal points sideways, n·l collapses, and the render goes dark.

It took a minute to realise that was a parameterisation bug pointing at the renderer. Dividing the amplitude by k makes strength mean the maximum slope: 1.0 is a 45° tilt at any cell count. A parameter whose effect changes when you change an unrelated parameter is one nobody can author against — and its failures accuse the wrong subsystem.

§E compares 1,369 samples against the closed form and agrees to 5.18 × 10−3, against a bound of √3/255 = 6.79 × 10−3 — and that bound is derived, not chosen: rounding to the nearest of 256 codes is a half-code error, a half code in the stored [0,1] range is 0.5/255, the [−1,1] decode doubles it to 1/255, and three channels of that in the worst direction is √3/255.

(The first draft of that line said 1/510 and forgot the doubling, which produced a bound below the true floor and failed a correct implementation. A tolerance that is wrong rather than merely loose is the more expensive of the two mistakes.)

9The GPU Port, and a Layout That Widened

Both renderers get this, in one commit, for the reason Lesson 6.4 gave when it changed the BRDF: a material field that one renderer honours and the other ignores is exactly the pair of opinions that can disagree. A material with a normal_map handle would be silently flat on the GPU, and nothing anywhere would say so.

The fragment shader is line-for-line the software one — same Gram-Schmidt, same decode, same basis change — so the interesting part of the port is everything around it.

9.1 — The flag that cost nothing

Lesson 6.4 left a `pad0` in material_uniforms, added purely to fill the second 16-byte register. This lesson spends it:

No binding code moved and no shader's register allocation changed, and the static_assert(sizeof(material_uniforms) == 32) is what says so rather than a comment. It is derived from material::normal_mapped() at the push, like textured and for the same reason (6.5 §5): a flag beside a handle is two opinions, and a flag computed from the handle is one.

9.2 — Two fallback texels, two identity elements

A declared sampler slot with nothing bound draws nothing at all — no pixels, no error message — which Lesson 4.7 hit once and solved with a 1×1 white texture. The normal map needs the same treatment with a different constant:

The two 1x1 fallback textures and what makes each the identity for its operation
SlotFallbacksRGB?Why it is the identity
t0 albedo(255, 255, 255)yes white is the identity for a multiply
t1 normal(128, 128, 255)no the lavender decodes to (0,0,1), and T·0 + B·0 + N·1 is N

That false in the second row is this lesson's entire subject on the GPU side. Pass true and the hardware asks for an _SRGB format, 128 decodes to 0.2159 rather than 0.5020, and every unmapped surface in the scene tilts by 38.8°.

9.3 — A shared vertex layout is an interface

gpu_vertex_pnu gained four floats, 32 bytes to 48. The static_assert Lesson 4.5 wrote caught it before a pixel was drawn:

That is the whole value of asserting a number you also wrote in a diagram: the alternative is a lesson page that says 32 bytes forever while the code says 48.

Then the interesting failure. gpu_mesh::describe now wants to emit a fourth attribute at location 3 — and vertex attribute locations are numbered across the whole pipeline, not per buffer. Lesson 4.6's instancing pipeline already puts its per-instance placement at location 3.

Caught by 4.5's check_layout, in verify_46

The harness compares the declared attributes against the shader's reflected inputs and reports extra and duplicate. It failed the moment describe widened — before any rendering, with a name.

Without it the symptom would have been placement silently fetching a tangent's bytes: seven instances stacked at nonsense positions, in a demo three modules old, with nothing pointing at the lesson that broke it.

Two fixes were available and the choice is instructive. Renumber every downstream consumer to 4, 5, 6 — which teaches that a shared layout is an interface, and is real churn. Or make the attribute optional:

The second is better engineering, and not because it is smaller. A vertex layout is per-pipeline state. A shader that never reads a tangent should not have one declared: it is a fetch paid for nothing, and the location it occupies is a resource other buffers want. The vertex buffer still carries the tangent either way — the pitch is sizeof(gpu_vertex_pnu) regardless — so a pipeline that opts out pays the memory and not the bandwidth, which is the honest description of the trade.

11Build & Run, and What You Should See

Run the two commands back to back. Same 2,304 triangles, same light, same material, same silhouette — and a surface that has gone from smooth to quilted. Nothing about the geometry changed; only what it claims its normal is.

Three things worth noticing in that picture

The tangents were derived, not loaded. OBJ has no tangent attribute at all, so every frame in that render came out of with_tangents and the uv chart. The log line says 1225 tangents (OBJ carries none, so every one was DERIVED).

The silhouette is unchanged. Look at the outer edge of the torus in both: perfectly smooth, because the outline is geometry and geometry is what a normal map does not touch. That is the technique's honest limit, visible in its own demo.

The overall brightness is about the same. If it were not — if the bumpy one were much darker — that would be the parameterisation bug §8.4 describes, and it is worth running --bumps 24 once to see what "too steep" looks like.

And the cube, which looks wrong on purpose

--model cube.gltf comes out banded rather than dimpled, and §4 explains why: Lesson 6.6 gave that asset one uv per box corner so its round-trip test could compare index-for-index, and four of its six faces therefore have zero uv area and no frame at all. Normal mapping is simply the first thing that makes an existing property of the asset visible.

12Common Pitfalls & Debugging

Symptom, cause and fix for the failures this lesson's code can produce
SymptomCauseFix
Everything is lit as though the light moved, and reducing the map's strength helps but never fixes it. The normal map was read as sRGB. 128 decodes to 0.216 instead of 0.502. texel_space::linear, and create_sampled(…, false) on the GPU. The fingerprint is a uniform 38.8° tilt.
Bumps read as dents. The green channel's sense is inverted — a map baked for the other convention. Negate y after the decode. glTF fixes the convention (green is +v, which is down the image), so this is an asset problem, not a code one — but you will meet it.
One half of a symmetric model is lit as the mirror image of the other. The handedness was dropped — a vec3 tangent, or a hard-coded cross(N, T). Carry w and multiply the bitangent by it. verify_67 §C is the test.
The normal map looks rotated within the surface, but only on stretched objects. The tangent was transformed by the normal matrix. The model matrix. Both answers stay in the plane, which is why this is a skew rather than an obvious break — and why it looks fine on everything uniformly scaled.
Black pixels, or NaNs spreading through the shading. normalize of a zero tangent — geometry with no uvs, or a vertex all of whose faces had zero uv area. Guard the length before normalising, and fall back to the geometric normal. Both renderers do; the GPU one costs a dot product per fragment.
The render is very dark and reads as a lighting bug. The normal map is far too steep — an amplitude used where a slope was meant. Check what your strength parameter actually scales. A·cos(ku)cos(kv) has peak gradient A·k, so a fixed amplitude gets steeper as the cell count rises.
A cube (or any box-projected asset) comes out banded rather than bumpy. Faces with zero uv area have no tangent frame. Nothing to fix in the renderer — the asset's chart is degenerate. Check mesh_data::tangents for zero entries.
Instanced geometry draws at nonsense positions after adding a vertex attribute. Attribute locations collided: they are numbered across the whole pipeline, not per buffer. check_layout reports duplicate and extra. Either renumber, or declare the attribute only on pipelines that read it.
A frame appears to work but a test asserting an exact round trip fails at ~5.5e−03. Not a bug. 0.5 is not an 8-bit code, so a flat map tilts by 0.318°. Predict the floor from the encoding and assert equality with it, rather than widening the tolerance until it passes.

13Exercises

13.1 — Break each step on purpose guided

Five one-line edits, each producing a distinct and recognisable artefact. Make each, look at torus.obj, then run verify_67 and note which section catches it: read the map as srgb; skip the *2-1 decode; skip the fragment's Gram-Schmidt; force handed = 1; use normal_matrix for the tangent.

Hint. Two of the five leave verify_67 entirely green on the torus, and that is the point of the exercise rather than a flaw in the harness — the torus has no mirrored chart and is uniformly scaled. Work out which two, then write the asset that would catch them. That is how a test suite actually grows.

13.2 — Remove the duplicated frame guided

The TBN construction now exists three times: raster.cpp, scene.frag.hlsl, and verify_67's own perturb. The C++ two can be one. Lift the frame into a small function in microfacet.hpp or a new header, and have both the fragment and the harness call it.

Hint. Note what the duplication currently costs: if the fragment changes and the harness does not, verify_67 keeps passing while the renderer is wrong. That is the real argument, not tidiness. The HLSL copy cannot be shared — and thinking about why not is worth more than the refactor: what would it take, and what does Module 8's shader work need to exist first?

13.3 — Honour normalTexture.scale engine challenge

glTF's normal texture carries a scale that multiplies the sampled x and y before normalisation — so 2.0 doubles the apparent steepness and 0 flattens the surface. gltf_material_desc::normal_scale reads it and nothing applies it. Make it work.

Hint. It needs a per-material float in the fragment, and material_uniforms is exactly full at 32 bytes — so the interesting part is the decision, not the arithmetic. Growing to 48 bytes costs a third register on every draw; packing it into an existing field costs precision. Measure the push cost with verify_46's uniform benchmark before choosing, and write down which you picked and why.

13.4 — Two-channel normal maps open

§E notices that z is almost always near 1 and gets a full byte for the privilege, while x and y use only half their range either side of 128. Store two channels and reconstruct the third as z = sqrt(1 - x² - y²).

Hint. This is what BC5 compression assumes, which is why it is the standard format for normal maps. Two things to measure rather than assume: how much precision the two surviving channels gain if you rescale them to the full range, and what happens at the one place the reconstruction is ill-conditioned — x² + y² slightly above 1 after filtering, which is a negative square root in a shader.

13.5 — Compress the tangent open

§9.4 prices the vertex growth at 50%. A unit vector plus a sign does not need sixteen bytes: octahedral encoding maps a direction to two numbers with excellent uniformity, so a tangent can be two 16-bit values with the handedness in a spare bit — four bytes.

Hint. Implement it on the CPU first and measure the worst angular error over a few million random directions before touching the vertex format; the answer for 16 bits is around a tenth of a degree, which you should confirm rather than take. Then note what the same trick would do to the normal, which has the same problem and twice the history.

14Recap & Next

The engine can now describe a surface finer than its triangles. Concretely, it gained:

  • A tangent frame, derived from the uv chart rather than copied — with_tangents, with the handedness recorded, the degenerate chart handled as a case rather than a guard, and the whole thing checkable against a triangle solved on paper.
  • A texture that knows what it holds. texel_space closes a gap that had been open since Lesson 4.7 gave the GPU an srgb flag the software renderer never had a counterpart for — and it is part of the asset's identity, so the same PNG read two ways is two textures over one decode.
  • Per-pixel normals in both renderers, from the same four lines, with glTF's normalTexture finally loaded.
  • An analytic test image, whose closed form lets verify_67 check the sampled normal against the right answer rather than against a picture.
  • A layout that widened safely, because Lesson 4.5's check_layout turned an attribute-location collision into a named failure before anything rendered.
The through-line, if you take one thing from this lesson

Three of the hardest bugs available here — the colour space, the handedness, the transform matrix — share a shape: they produce a picture that is plausible. A uniform 38.8° tilt reads as an over-strong map. A dropped handedness reads as a modelling error. The wrong matrix reads as an asset authored at the wrong angle. None of them looks like the thing it is.

Which is why every one of them is a type or an assertion in this lesson rather than a comment: a vec4 that cannot lose its sign, an enum on the texture that no caller can forget, and a round trip whose error floor is predicted from the encoding.

Two things deferred, both named with reasons. Metallic-roughness textures are now unblocked — they are linear data and texel_space is what they were waiting for — but reading them changes the shading equation's inputs rather than its normal, and that belongs with the material work rather than here. And normalTexture.scale is read and not applied, because material_uniforms is exactly full; that is Exercise 3, and the exercise is the decision rather than the arithmetic.

Next: 6.8 — Shadow Mapping: Bias, Acne, and PCF. Every surface in this engine is lit as though nothing were between it and the light, which is why the torus's inner ring is as bright as its outer one. Shadow mapping fixes that by rendering the scene from the light and asking, per pixel, whether what the light can see is what the camera can see — and the whole lesson is that the comparison is between two floating-point depths that were computed differently, which produces the most famous artefact in real-time graphics. This lesson leaves you well placed for it: a normal map is the first time the engine has had a shading normal that disagrees with the geometry, and shadow bias is the first time that disagreement will matter.

15Further Reading

  • Eric Lengyel — Mathematics for 3D Game Programming and Computer Graphics, §7.8 "Bump Mapping". The 2×2 inversion in §4 is Lengyel's derivation, and his is the account most later ones are downstream of. Worth reading for the way it arrives at the same two equations from the chain rule instead.
  • Morten Mikkelsen — Simulation of Wrinkled Surfaces Revisited (2008). The MikkTSpace thesis. The reason it matters is not a better formula but a standard: if the baker and the renderer compute tangents differently, a normal map is subtly wrong at every seam, and MikkTSpace exists so that both can agree bit-for-bit. glTF's spec recommends it by name. Ours does not implement it, and §8 says so.
  • Christian Schüler — "Followup: Normal Mapping Without Precomputed Tangents". The frame can be reconstructed in the fragment shader from screen-space derivatives, with no vertex attribute at all — which trades §9.4's 16 bytes a vertex for arithmetic per pixel. Read it for the trade rather than the technique.
  • Khronos — glTF 2.0 Specification, §3.7.2.1 (the TANGENT attribute and its w) and §5.28 (normalTextureInfo.scale). Short, and the source for two decisions in this lesson.
  • Real-Time Rendering, 4th ed., §6.7 "Bump Mapping", and §16.6 for the vertex-compression material Exercise 5 needs.
  • Cigolle, Donow, Evangelakos, Mara, McGuire, Meyer — A Survey of Efficient Representations for Independent Unit Vectors (JCGT, 2014). Octahedral encoding, measured against every alternative. The reference for Exercise 5, and unusually good at reporting error rather than asserting quality.
  • Scratchapixel — Normal Mapping. The gentler first pass, with the tangent-space picture drawn out at length.
