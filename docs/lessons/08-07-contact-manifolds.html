Contact Manifolds and Persistence

8.6 finished the narrow phase's arithmetic. epa_penetration returns how deep two convex shapes overlap, which way to push them apart, and a pair of witness points on the two surfaces — everything a solver asks for — and a crate resting on a floor still will not sit still. A contact force acts at a point, so the torque it exerts about that point is zero, and a body held up by one point is a body balanced on a pin. This lesson measures that on the engine's own rigid_body with the same gravity, the same impulses and the same normal in both arms: four contact points settle at 1.16 mm of penetration and 5.2 °/s of residual rotation, and the same contact reduced to its deepest single point sinks 21.27 mm and rocks at 27.99 °/s, for ever. The moment a support polygon can carry is derivable before it is measured — m·g·(w/2), 49.05 N·m — and a bisection lands on 47.87. Then the part that needed a new interface. 8.5's claim that a convex set is determined by its support function is true, and support(d) still returns one point when the argmax is a whole square: 4,000 directions drawn from a cone a thousandth of a degree wide about a face normal come back with all four corners, deterministically and arbitrarily, which are not the same thing. So the shapes learn to return a whole feature, and the two features are clipped against each other — Sutherland and Hodgman, which 3.3 wrote for the near plane, doing a different job. The second half is the word persistence, and the reason for it is 8.10's solver: warm starting needs to know which of this frame's contacts is which of last frame's, and the tempting answer is wrong. Positions are floats computed by a different route through the same code; over a crate's landing a ten-micrometre position match finds 0.00% of them while an id match finds 82%, rising to 100% of 1,999 consecutive frames once it settles. And a control that expected zero matches after a two-metre teleport got four of four — because the manifold was right and the control was wrong. Ships manifold.hpp, support_face for every primitive, a frame-to-frame cache, a demo that applies one clip plane per keystroke, nine measured sections and 92 checks, and a knob that was written, measured at zero over 208,000 pairs, and taken back out.

1. The problem: a crate balanced on a pin

Here is where 8.6 left the engine. Two convex shapes overlap; epa_penetration returns the minimum translation that separates them, the direction of it, and a pair of witness points — one on each surface — that the depth is measured between. That is a complete answer to the question “how are these two intersecting”, and it is the answer every paper on penetration depth sets out to compute.

Now build the simplest thing in the world out of it. A floor. A crate. Gravity. Each step, ask the narrow phase where they touch, push the crate out along the normal with an impulse, and integrate. Run it for two seconds.

The crate does not sit still. It rocks, gently and for ever, and it sits a couple of centimetres inside the floor while it does it. Turn up the solver's iteration count and it rocks with a smaller amplitude and does not stop. Turn up the position correction and it rocks faster. Drop it from higher and it rocks harder. Nothing about the tuning fixes it, because nothing about the tuning is wrong.

A contact force acts at a point, so the torque it exerts about that point is zero. Put the point anywhere but directly beneath the centre of mass and the force and the weight form a couple, so the body turns. As it turns, the contact point moves to the other corner, and the couple reverses. There is no equilibrium unless the single point happens to sit exactly under the centre of mass — which is a set of measure zero, and which the crate leaves on the first frame it is disturbed.

That is not a numerical problem and it is not an accuracy problem. A one-point contact does not approximate a resting crate badly; it describes a different physical situation — a crate balanced on a pin — and simulates that one correctly. Figure 1 is the whole argument in a picture and a table.

Read the middle two columns rather than the first. The tilt after two seconds is small in both arms, and it is small in the one-point arm for a reason that is worth being uncomfortable about: a single point does hold a crate up, by chattering. It pushes the lowest corner, which turns the other corner down, which becomes the lowest corner, which gets pushed in turn. Averaged over a few frames the contact point sweeps the whole face and the average force can act anywhere on it, which is exactly what a support polygon does — at 27.99 °/s of permanent rocking and 21 mm of sink, where the four-point contact rocks at 5.2 and sinks 1.16.

This is why single-point contact looks nearly fine in a demo and is unusable in a game. Drop one crate on a floor and it lands, wobbles for a moment and looks settled. Stack six and the stack breathes; put a character on top and the character jitters; ask a physics-driven door to stay shut and it buzzes. Every one of those is the same fact wearing a different hat, and none of them is fixed downstream.

The number you can write down first

The good news about a statement this clean is that it comes with arithmetic. A support polygon w metres wide, under a body of weight m·g, can resist a moment of at most m·g·(w/2): the largest moment arm available is half the polygon's width, because that is as far from the centre as the resultant can be pushed before it leaves the polygon and the body tips.

For a 10 kg crate one metre wide, m·g·(w/2) = 10 × 9.81 × 0.5 = 49.05 N·m.

§A.2 of the harness applies a steady torque about z and bisects for the value at which the crate stops resting. Fourteen bisection steps land on 47.869 N·m, 2.41% below the prediction — and the 2.41% is not error. It is the penetration the solver allows before it pushes back, which puts the crate's effective lip a hair inside its geometric one. A prediction that survives three digits is a different kind of statement from one that survives a bracket.

And the manifold was never told where anything was. §A.3 slides the same crate off the edge of a ledge and asks, at each overhang, whether the centre of mass projects inside the contact set the manifold returned. It does at 0.00, 0.20, 0.40 and 0.45 m of overhang, and the crate rests to within a tenth of a degree. It does not at 0.55 and 0.70, and the crate topples 156° and 108°. Statics predicts the crossing at exactly half a metre, where the centre of mass passes over the lip. Nothing in the simulation knows there is a ledge.

The other thing 8.6 left behind

8.6 §13 named a second problem and did not solve it, because it is not solvable: the contact normal is discontinuous. Slide a box along a wall toward the wall's end. Until it passes the corner, the shortest way out is through the wall's face; past the corner, the shortest way out is past the end. The minimum translation switches from one to the other in a single frame, and the direction swings ninety degrees while the depth — the thing the two escapes are being compared on — changes by nothing at all.

§B.1 walks a 0.4 m cube past that crossing in ten-micrometre steps. Between two adjacent steps the normal changes by 90.00° and the depth changes by 0.01001 mm, which is the step size times the slope. Both numbers are correct. They are correct because min of two smooth functions is not smooth where the argmin changes, and penetration depth is a minimum: every implementation of it has this, and no amount of care in EPA removes it.

Nothing in this lesson removes it either. What this lesson does is make the contact set stable within a feature pair, so that the swing becomes a single discrete event affecting a handful of identities rather than four points re-derived from scratch on every frame. §B.2 measures the difference on the mildest motion there is — a crate sliding two millimetres across a floor, one micrometre at a time: 

     samples                          : 2000
     the crate travelled              : 1.999 mm
     EPA's witness point travelled    : 502.749 mm
     its worst single step            : 500.863 mm
     steps where it moved over 10 um  : 1
     manifold contact ids changed     : 0 times

The crate moved two millimetres. EPA's witness point moved half a metre, essentially all of it in one step, because the face it sits on is a metre across and which corner of it the support function returns is decided by a tie-break. The manifold's four identities did not change at all, and the control says that is not because they cannot: slide the same crate off the floor's edge, where the contact genuinely becomes a different one, and they change three times.

Two problems, then, and they have one answer between them. A resting body needs a set of contact points rather than a point, and a solver needs to recognise this frame's set as last frame's. The rest of this lesson is that answer, and the four steps it takes.

2. Building intuition: the support polygon

Before any code, the statics — because it is three sentences long and it explains everything §1 measured.

A body resting on a set of contact points is held up by a set of normal forces, one per point. Each of them pushes and none of them pulls: a floor can hold a crate up and cannot hold it down. So each force fᵢ is a non-negative number times the shared normal n, and the total force on the body is

F = n · Σ fᵢ, with every fᵢ ≥ 0.

That is the easy half. The interesting half is the torque. Taking moments about the centre of mass, with rᵢ the vector from the centre to contact point pᵢ:

τ = Σ rᵢ × (n fᵢ) = (Σ fᵢ rᵢ) × n

and the thing being crossed with n is Σ fᵢ rᵢ. Divide through by Σ fᵢ and it is a weighted average of the contact points, with non-negative weights summing to one. That is exactly the definition of a point in the convex hull of the pᵢ.

The resultant of a set of normal contact forces acts at a point of their convex hull, and can act at any point of it. That hull — projected onto the contact plane — is what statics calls the support polygon. A body rests when gravity's line of action passes through the support polygon, because then there is a choice of non-negative fᵢ that cancels both the force and the torque. It tips when gravity's line passes outside, because then there is not.

The worked example, with real numbers

Take the crate from §1: 10 kg, one metre on a side, resting on four contact points at the corners of its base. Put the origin under its centre of mass and let the contacts be at x = ±0.5, z = ±0.5, all at y = 0, with n = (0, 1, 0).

Gravity is (0, −98.1, 0) N through the centre. For equilibrium, Σ fᵢ = 98.1 N, and the four weights must average to a point directly under the centre — which, with the centre at x = 0, means Σ fᵢ xᵢ = 0. One solution is fᵢ = 24.525 N each. There are infinitely many others (this is the rank deficiency §3 comes back to), and every one of them works.

Now push the crate sideways until its centre of mass is at x = 0.4. The weights must now average to x = 0.4. The most any average of four numbers drawn from {−0.5, +0.5} can be is +0.5, so 0.4 is achievable: put f = 88.29 N on the two contacts at +0.5 and 9.81 N on the two at −0.5, and check that (88.29 × 0.5 + 9.81 × −0.5) × 2 / 98.1 = 0.4. ✓

Push it to x = 0.6 and there is no solution, because no average of numbers that are all at most 0.5 reaches 0.6. The crate must tip. The boundary is at x = 0.5, which is where §A.3 measured it — between 0.45 and 0.55.

And the moment form of the same statement: the largest moment the polygon can resist about the centre is m·g times the farthest the resultant can be pushed, which is half the polygon's width. 10 × 9.81 × 0.5 = 49.05 N·m, measured at 47.869.

Why one point is a special case rather than a small one

Run the same argument with a single contact point. The hull of one point is that point, so Σ fᵢ rᵢ / Σ fᵢ = r₁ always, whatever f₁ is. There is exactly one line of action available, and unless gravity happens to lie on it there is no equilibrium at any force.

Two points are better and still not enough: their hull is a segment, so the body is held against rotation about every axis except the one along that segment, about which it is free. A crate resting on two contact points is a crate on a hinge. §6 measures exactly this happening to a lightly tilted crate, and it is why the parallelism tolerance there is worth spending a section on.

3. What a manifold is, and why four

A contact manifold is a set of contact points that share one normal. Not a set of independent contacts — a set of points on one contact, each with its own position and its own depth, all resolved against the same direction.

The shared normal is the part that makes it a manifold rather than a list. Two convex shapes touching along a face touch along a planar region, and every point of that region has the same outward direction; the depths differ from point to point, which is precisely what a four-point manifold knows about a slightly tilted crate and a single averaged contact does not. Handing a solver four contacts with four different normals would be describing four different collisions, and the solver would fight itself.

The contact region is a polygon, and that is a theorem

Where exactly do two convex shapes touch? The set of points they have in common. Both shapes are convex; the intersection of two convex sets is convex; so the contact region is convex. When the two shapes meet face to face, that region is the intersection of two convex polygons lying in the same plane, which is a convex polygon — and a polygon with at most m + n vertices when the two have m and n, because each edge of one can cut each boundary of the other at most twice and each cut adds at most one vertex net.

That bound is why k_max_clip_points is 2 × k_max_face_vertices and not a guess, and §7 measures what a real mix actually produces: over 99,995 face contacts between random boxes, the clip returns four points 36.96% of the time, five 33.06%, six 20.97%, and seven or eight the rest.

Four

A convex polygon can have any number of vertices; a manifold holds four. That is not a budget.

Go back to §2's arithmetic. The manifold's job is to let the resultant act anywhere in the support polygon, and that needs enough independent non-negative weights to reach every point of a convex region in the plane. Three points span a triangle. Four span a quadrilateral, which is the general shape of a planar convex region's extremes in two independent directions. Five add a weight that is a linear combination of the other four as far as the resultant is concerned: the system of equations the solver is trying to satisfy — total force, two components of torque — has three rows, and four unknowns already over-determine the position of the resultant.

A fifth contact point makes the solution non-unique rather than better. This is the same statement as “a four-legged table wobbles”: three legs determine a plane and the fourth is a redundant constraint that the table resolves by rocking. A physical four-point manifold on a rigid body has the same redundancy, and a solver resolves it by distributing the impulse arbitrarily among the points, which is fine — the resultant is what matters. Adding more points adds more arbitrariness and no more resultant.

§8 measures what the fifth point would buy on this engine's own geometry, and the answer has two halves. Against the whole clipped polygon, four points span 88.87% of the area. Against the best four points there are — brute-forced over every four-subset, seventy combinations for an eight-point clip — they span 96.32%, with a median of 1.00000. And the depth lost by the reduction, over 61,455 manifolds, is exactly zero, because the reduction picks the deepest point first.

Box2D, Bullet's persistent manifold and Havok all land on four. They land on it for this reason and not by coincidence.

4. The feature a support function cannot name

Now the part that needed a new interface, and the part that is genuinely surprising if you have been reading 8.5 carefully.

8.5's header makes a strong claim and the claim is true: a convex set is completely determined by its support function. Not described by, not approximated by — determined. A convex set is the intersection of all the half-spaces that contain it, support names the boundary of each one, and knowing every half-space is knowing the set. Two convex shapes with the same support function are the same shape.

So a manifold generator ought to be writable in terms of support alone. It is not, and the reason is one word: argmax.

support_X(d) = the point of X that is farthest along d

Read it again. The point. When d is a face normal, the set of points farthest along d is the whole face — four corners for a box, and every point between them — and the function has to return one of them. Which one is decided by the implementation: 

// engine/src/phys/shape.cpp, from 8.5, unchanged
const float sx = (dot(box.axes.c0, d) >= 0.0f) ? box.half_extents.x : -box.half_extents.x;
const float sy = (dot(box.axes.c1, d) >= 0.0f) ? box.half_extents.y : -box.half_extents.y;
const float sz = (dot(box.axes.c2, d) >= 0.0f) ? box.half_extents.z : -box.half_extents.z;
return box.axes.c0 * sx + box.axes.c1 * sy + box.axes.c2 * sz;

Three sign tests. When d is the box's +y axis exactly, the first and third dot products are zero, the >= 0 sends both to the positive side, and the function returns the (+, +, +) corner. That is deterministic, and it is reasonable, and it is arbitrary — which is not the same thing. Perturb d by a millionth of a degree and a different corner wins.

§C.1 measures it directly. Four thousand directions drawn from a cone one thousandth of a degree wide about a unit cube's +y face normal — a cone every direction in which has exactly the same four-corner argmax set: 

     distinct corners `support` returned  : 4 of 4
     `support_face` count == 4            : 4000 of 4000
     distinct corners it named            : 4

All four. Which is to say that the point support returns is a discontinuous function of a direction that is everywhere inside the same argmax set — and that discontinuity is not a defect, because every one of the four answers is correct. The question simply has four answers and the function can only give one.

The control says the ambiguity belongs to the query and not to the function. §C.2 runs the same jitter about a direction pointed at a corner, where the argmax really is a single point, and gets 1 corner of 8. So the arbitrariness appears exactly where the feature is bigger than a point — which is exactly where a manifold is needed, and nowhere else.

The prediction that turned out to be wrong

There is an obvious-looking way round this, and it is worth saying why it does not work, because it was this lesson's plan until it was tried.

EPA finishes with a face of the Minkowski difference — the triangle whose plane is closest to the origin. A face of A ⊖ B is always one of three things: a face of A against a vertex of B, a vertex of A against a face of B, or an edge of each. So the contacting features are, in principle, recoverable from EPA's winning triangle, and this lesson was expected to widen epa_result to carry it.

Two things are wrong with that. The first is that the triangle's three vertices are support points — differences of two support calls — so they carry the tie-break problem above intact, and recovering “which face of A” from them means undoing the very ambiguity that made the problem. The second is more basic: EPA's triangle is a piece of the difference set's face, not the whole of it. The face is a polygon; EPA covered it with triangles and stopped at the first one that proved the bound. Recovering the whole face from one triangle means walking the polytope's neighbours and merging coplanar faces, which is more code than asking the shape.

So the interface widens instead, by exactly one query, on the shapes themselves: 

[[nodiscard]] contact_face support_face(const obb& box, vec3 d);
[[nodiscard]] contact_face support_face(const engine::sphere& s, vec3 d);
[[nodiscard]] contact_face support_face(const capsule& c, vec3 d);
[[nodiscard]] contact_face support_face(const hull& h, vec3 d,
                                        float gather_sin = k_face_gather_sin);

epa.hpp is untouched by this lesson. That is a nicer outcome than the one that was planned, and it is worth noticing the shape of it: the prediction was made by looking at where the information was, and the right answer came from asking where the information was exact.

5. Asking a shape for its face

support_face returns the whole feature a shape presents in a direction: a point, a segment, or a polygon. The counts are not a limitation of the implementation — they are the actual dimensions of the contact features these shapes have.

The box: an argmax over three numbers

A box has six faces and the one to return is the one whose outward normal leans into d hardest. That is an argmax over three absolute dot products, and it is a strictly better-behaved question than “is this vertex on the supporting plane”: the three candidates are generally well separated, and a tie between two of them means the two faces are equally good answers, so either is correct and only reproducibility is at stake. 

    int best = 0;
    float best_abs = -1.0f;
    float sign = 1.0f;
    for (int i = 0; i < 3; ++i)
    {
        const float p = dot(box.axis(i), d);
        const float a = std::fabs(p);
        if (a > best_abs)
        {
            best_abs = a;
            best = i;
            sign = (p >= 0.0f) ? 1.0f : -1.0f;
        }
    }

Note the > rather than >=: a direction exactly between two faces keeps the lower axis index, deterministically. This is support_local's >= 0 argument one dimension up.

The winding, which is load-bearing

The four corners have to come back in order around the face, and in a specific order: counter-clockwise about the face's own outward normal. That is conventions.html §7, the same winding the rasteriser has used since 3.5 and the GPU since 4.4, and here it is not decoration. §7's clipper builds each side plane as cross(edge, normal), which points out of the polygon only if the winding is counter-clockwise. Reverse it and every side plane faces inward, every incident point is classified outside, and the manifold comes back empty on exactly the contacts that matter most.

Getting it right is one observation. If (i, j, k) is a cyclic permutation of a right-handed frame then axis(j) × axis(k) = axis(i), so walking −e₀−e₁ → +e₀−e₁ → +e₀+e₁ → −e₀+e₁ turns counter-clockwise about +axis(i): the first two edges are 2e₀ then 2e₁, whose cross product is 4·hj·hk·axis(i). On the negative face the outward normal is −axis(i), so the two in-plane vectors swap and the same walk turns counter-clockwise about the normal that is actually outward. 

    const vec3 e0 = (sign >= 0.0f) ? uj : uk;
    const vec3 e1 = (sign >= 0.0f) ? uk : uj;

    f.normal = n;
    f.count = 4;
    f.v[0] = centre - e0 - e1;
    f.v[1] = centre + e0 - e1;
    f.v[2] = centre + e0 + e1;
    f.v[3] = centre - e0 + e1;

§5's control checks it by hand: reverse a face's winding and count how many of the incident face's four vertices the side planes accept. The answer is 0 of 4, on a contact that is plainly there.

The sphere and the capsule: the honest answers

A ball has no flat feature. support_face(sphere, d) returns one vertex, and that is the truth rather than a shortfall. It is tempting to fabricate a small disc around the support point so that a ball “stacks properly”, and the lie shows up immediately as a ball that will not roll — the disc resists rotation that nothing is resisting.

A capsule has exactly one flat feature, and it is one-dimensional: the line where the cylindrical side meets the supporting plane, which exists only when the query direction is perpendicular to the spine. Everything else — the two caps, and every shoulder between them — is spherical. So the capsule is the first shape with a tolerance, and it is a tolerance on how perpendicular counts as perpendicular: 

    if (along * along <= k_face_gather_sin * k_face_gather_sin && p2 > 0.0f)
    {
        // the side: a two-vertex segment along the spine
    }
    else
    {
        // one point: a cap, or a shoulder
    }

§5 measures 1,041 side hits and 18,959 cap hits over 20,000 random directions and shapes, and every returned vertex is exactly radius from the spine segment — worst case 1.19e−07 m, which is float's own resolution and needs no second opinion at all, because “is this point on the capsule” is a one-line exact test.

The hull: the one that has no answer

And then a hull, which is where it gets interesting. 8.5 made hull a point cloud on purpose — a span of vertices with no topology — because that is what an artist's convex collider is when it arrives, and because support needs nothing more. support_face does. A face of a point cloud is the set of vertices on its supporting plane, and deciding whether a vertex is on a plane is 8.6 §7's problem again: a comparison whose two sides are equal in exact arithmetic.

The gather tolerance has to be an angle rather than a distance, and the derivation is one line. A vertex w metres across a face sits w·sin θ below the supporting plane when the query direction is θ off the face normal. So the tolerance that admits a whole face is sin θ times the shape's own extent: 

    const float eps = gather_sin * std::sqrt(extent2);

A constant in metres here would gather half the hull on a doorknob and one vertex on a car — 8.5 §11's argument, in a new place.

And there is no value of gather_sin that is right, because the question is topological and a point cloud has no topology. §5 sweeps it on a 24-sided prism, querying the true normal of one of its side faces: 

     gather_sin   side face    bulge      end cap
     0.002         4 vertices  0.0000 m   8 vertices
     0.010         4 vertices  0.0000 m   8 vertices
     0.050         8 vertices  0.0338 m   8 vertices
     0.200         8 vertices  0.0990 m   8 vertices

Too tight and a cylinder's side contact drops to a bare edge — two points, no support polygon, and the thing rolls when it should not. Too loose and three flat faces are merged into one polygon that bulges 34 mm. On a hand-authored collider, with genuinely flat faces whose normals are well apart, any value in a wide range gives the same answer; on a tessellated curve there is no value that does. The only real fix is to give hull its faces, and §12 says so plainly rather than pretending the tolerance is a tuning matter.

Two details in the hull's implementation that are easy to get wrong

The gathered vertices arrive in index order, which is not an order. They are in whatever order the exporter wrote them, and a polygon whose vertices are not in order around its own boundary is not a polygon: its edges cross, cross(edge, normal) points in scattered directions, and the clipper rejects everything. So they are sorted by angle about the face's centroid — and the frame that angle is measured in starts at the lowest-indexed gathered vertex, which is what makes local index 0 the same vertex on every frame. Local indices are half of every contact id.

The face's normal is not the query direction. Up to the sort it has to be, because a point and a segment have no plane of their own. A polygon does, and it is a different vector: the gather admits a face up to k_face_gather_sin off the query, so the two can differ by nearly three degrees, and §7 clips against the reference face's plane. Using the query direction there would tilt every side plane by that angle and mis-measure every depth. 

    vec3 newell{};
    for (int i = 0; i < count; ++i)
    {
        newell += cross(f.v[i], f.v[(i + 1) % count]);
    }

Newell's method rather than one cross product of two edges, because the gathered set is only approximately planar — it is defined by a tolerance, after all — and a cross product of the first two edges reads whatever noise those particular three vertices carry. Newell's sum is Σ vᵢ × vᵢ₊₁, which for a planar polygon is exactly twice its area times its normal and for a nearly planar one is the area-weighted average of every triple. It is also translation invariant, which is why it can be computed on centre-relative vertices without a correction.

Decimation, and where it goes

A contact_face holds eight vertices. A tessellated cylinder's end cap has as many as the artist drew, and the honest thing is to inscribe a polygon in it rather than to refuse. The order of operations is the whole of it: decimate after the angular sort, keeping every ⌈n/8⌉-th vertex, so what is kept is a polygon inscribed in the true face and every contact point it produces is still on the surface. Decimate before the sort and the result is eight vertices in exporter order, which is not a shape.

The cost is predictable before it is measured. The kept octagon's edges cut the chord of the true rim, so the deepest shortfall is at each edge's midpoint: r(1 − cos(π/8)). For r = 0.5 that is 0.03806 m, and §5 measures 0.03806.

6. Reference, incident, and how parallel is parallel

Two shapes, two features. Now they have to be clipped against each other, and clipping is not symmetric: one polygon supplies the planes and the other gets cut. Which is which is a decision, and it changes the answer.

The reference face supplies the side planes and the plane every depth is measured from. The incident face is the one cut. The reference is the more face-like of the two: the one whose own plane normal agrees best with the contact normal.

A feature counts as a face at all only when it has three or more vertices and its normal is within face_cos of the contact normal. The second condition is the one that matters, and it is easy to leave out: support_face always returns a box's dominant face, even when the contact is really on one of its edges, and clipping against a face thirty degrees off the contact normal produces four points none of which describes the contact. 

    const float cos_a = dot(fa.normal, n);
    const float cos_b = dot(fb.normal, -n);
    const bool a_is_face = (fa.count >= 3) && (cos_a >= cfg.face_cos);
    const bool b_is_face = (fb.count >= 3) && (cos_b >= cfg.face_cos);

Note the sign on the second: normal points from a toward b, so a's surface faces along +n and b's faces back along −n. Getting one of these backwards returns the far side of the shape and produces a manifold whose points are a whole diameter away — which looks, on screen, exactly like a body being flung across the level by a solver that is working perfectly.

What the tolerance actually decides

Whose normal the manifold reports, and why it is not EPA's

A design decision worth defending. contact_manifold::normal is the reference face's own normal, oriented from a toward b — not the normal EPA returned. The two are close and they are not the same, and the difference is deliberate.

A face normal is a cross product of two edges of a real shape. EPA's normal is the normal of a triangle approximating a face of the difference set, converged to a tolerance. On a box-on-box contact both are exact, because the difference set has a genuinely flat face and EPA terminates on it — which makes box-on-box the one fixture that cannot show the difference. §6 uses a ball instead, which 8.6 §9 measured as EPA's worst case:

An aside on measuring angles, because this section's first numbers were the instrument. acos(dot(a, b)) is the obvious spelling and it has a resolution floor: for a small angle t, dot is 1 − t²/2, so all the information about t lives in the last bits of a number near 1. Two unit float vectors give a dot product good to about 1e−7, which recovers t only to √(2e−7) radians — 0.036°. The first version of this section reported the reference-face normal as “0.0198° off the exact MTV”, and that number was the instrument rather than the normal. The chord |a − b| is the angle to first order and loses nothing: 2·asin(|a−b|/2) is exact at every angle, and near zero it is a subtraction of two nearly equal vectors, which carries full relative precision. Sterbenz's lemma again, 8.5 §11's argument in a measuring instrument rather than in the engine.

7. The clip

Two polygons in nearly the same plane, one of them nominated as the reference. The contact set is their intersection, and computing it is an algorithm this course has already written.

3.3 clipped a triangle against the near plane by walking its edges and asking, for each one, whether it crosses. This is the same walk against ref.count planes instead of one, and the planes are built from the polygon rather than given.

Where a side plane comes from

For edge v[i] → v[i+1] of a polygon wound counter-clockwise about its own normal n, the outward in-plane direction is cross(edge, n). Check it on a square in the xy plane with n = +z, wound (0,0) → (1,0) → (1,1) → (0,1): the first edge is (1,0,0), and (1,0,0) × (0,0,1) = (0,−1,0), which points away from the square at its bottom edge. ✓ 

            const vec3 a0 = rv[e];
            const vec3 a1 = rv[(e + 1) % ref.count];
            const vec3 side = cross(a1 - a0, rn);
            const float offset = dot(side, a0);

side is deliberately not normalised. Sutherland–Hodgman needs only the sign of each offset and the ratio of two of them, and both are invariant under a positive scale, so a square root per plane per pair would buy exactly nothing.

The walk

One pass per plane. For each edge of the polygon being clipped, classify both endpoints against the plane; keep the first if it is inside; and if the two are on opposite sides, insert the crossing point. 

            for (int i = 0; i < count; ++i)
            {
                const clip_vertex& c = buf[cur][i];
                const clip_vertex& nx = buf[cur][(i + 1) % count];
                const float dc = dot(side, c.p) - offset;
                const float dn = dot(side, nx.p) - offset;

                if (dc <= 0.0f && out < k_max_clip_points) { buf[dst][out++] = c; }

                if ((dc < 0.0f) != (dn < 0.0f) && out < k_max_clip_points)
                {
                    const float t = dc / (dc - dn);
                    /* ... insert c + (nx − c)·t, and name it ... */
                }
            }

Each plane adds at most two vertices and removes at least one, which is where §3's m + n bound comes from, and why two faces of eight vertices need a buffer of sixteen.

The part that is not in the textbook: naming what comes out

A clipped vertex is a position, and a position is exactly what §9 is going to refuse to match on. So every vertex the clipper produces has to remember what made it, and this is where a careful implementation differs from a quick one.

There are two different questions to answer about each vertex, and the temptation is to answer them with one field:

  • What made this point? An incident-face vertex that survived; an incident edge cut by a reference side plane; or a corner of the reference face itself. This is fixed the moment the vertex is created and becomes its contact_id.
  • Which feature does the segment leaving this point lie along? This is rewritten by every subsequent pass, and it is what the next plane needs in order to name the vertex it creates.
struct clip_vertex
{
    vec3 p;                          ///< Relative to `a.origin`.

    std::uint8_t inc_vertex = 0xff;  ///< This IS incident polygon vertex n.
    std::uint8_t cut_inc_edge = 0xff;///< Made by cutting incident edge n.
    std::uint8_t cut_ref_edge = 0xff;///< ...with reference side plane n.
    std::uint8_t ref_vertex = 0xff;  ///< This IS reference polygon vertex n.

    std::uint8_t out_inc_edge = 0xff;///< The outgoing segment lies on incident edge n.
    std::uint8_t out_ref_edge = 0xff;///< The outgoing segment lies on reference plane n.
};

Conflate them and a point cut by the second side plane is attributed to the first. The ids come out stable, plausible and wrong — the worst combination there is, because nothing downstream can tell. A warm start would land on the wrong contact every time a face is cut on two sides at once, which §7 measures at about a third of all the contact points a real mix produces.

The third kind of contact point, which is easy to miss

Two of the three outcomes are obvious once drawn: an incident vertex that survived every plane, and an incident edge cut by a plane. The third is not, and it happens whenever the reference face is the smaller of the two — a small crate under a big one, with the small one chosen as reference.

Mechanically, it appears when a segment that already lies along one reference plane is cut by another. That intersection is exactly a corner of the reference polygon, and it can be named as one: side plane e carries edge e, which runs from vertex e to vertex e+1, so two planes share a vertex only when their indices are adjacent, and the shared vertex is the later one. 

std::uint8_t shared_vertex(std::uint8_t p, std::uint8_t q, int n)
{
    if (p == 0xff || q == 0xff) { return 0xff; }
    const int pi = static_cast<int>(p);
    const int qi = static_cast<int>(q);
    if (qi == (pi + 1) % n) { return static_cast<std::uint8_t>(qi); }
    if (pi == (qi + 1) % n) { return static_cast<std::uint8_t>(pi); }
    return 0xff;
}

§7's histogram says it is not rare: 31,661 reference-vertex contacts out of 481,847 points, against 211,615 incident vertices and 162,966 crossings.

The filter, which is the whole difference between a contact and a coincidence

The clip produces the intersection of the two faces in projection. Some of those points are penetrating and some are not — a tilted crate's far corners are above the floor's plane even though their shadows land squarely on it — and only the penetrating ones are contacts. 

            const float sep = dot(rn, v.p - rv[0]);
            if (sep > keep) { continue; }

            contact_point& cp = kept[kept_count++];
            cp.depth = -sep;
            cp.position = a.origin + v.p - rn * (sep * 0.5f);

keep is cfg.keep_slop × scale and defaults to zero, so this lesson's manifolds contain only real overlaps. 8.9 will turn it up a little, because a point a tenth of a millimetre off the surface this frame is a contact next frame and having it already in the manifold with a warm impulse is what stops a stack breathing.

§7's control widens it to a whole shape-width and measures what that costs: of 90,783 points kept, 10,707 — 11.79% — are not touching at all, with a median gap of 0.19 m and a worst of 1.60. A solver handed those applies a correction where nothing is touching; with a non-negative impulse clamp it mostly does nothing and merely wastes the iteration, which is why a small positive slop is a good idea and a shape-width is not.

The contact point is a midpoint, and the instrument has to know that

A contact is really a pair of points, one on each surface, separated by depth along the normal. A solver wants a single lever arm per body, and the midpoint splits the error evenly rather than biasing every torque toward one of them. Either surface point is recoverable exactly:

A's surface point is position + normal·depth/2; B's is position − normal·depth/2, whichever shape supplied the reference face.

This mattered for the harness before it mattered for anything else. §7's first version checked the midpoint against both boxes and reported a worst case of 0.63 m — a number that looked like a catastrophic clipping bug and was a fact about the convention. On a pair overlapping by more than its own size, the midpoint is legitimately half a metre from either surface. Checking the two recovered points instead gives 2.52e−06 m worst, over 410,075 points, measured with a signed distance field so that a point pushed inside a shape counts as wrong too.

An edge contact has no reference plane, so it has no such recovery. Its point is the midpoint of the closest points of two crossed edges, and only when they nearly touch is that midpoint near either surface. At the depths a solver maintains it is within a few millimetres of both; on a deep overlap it is half a metre from one, and no convention does better, because there is no single point where two deeply crossed boxes touch. §12 names it as a limitation rather than hiding it.

8. Four, and why not five

§3 argued for four from statics. This section measures the argument, and answers the practical question it leaves open: which four.

First, how often the question arises at all. Over 99,995 face contacts between random boxes the clip returns: 

        2 :      8  ( 0.01%)
        3 :   1573  ( 1.57%)
        4 :  36959  (36.96%)
        5 :  33055  (33.06%)
        6 :  20972  (20.97%)
        7 :   6349  ( 6.35%)
        8 :   1079  ( 1.08%)

A third of face contacts clip to more than four, so the reduction is not an edge case — it runs on one contact in three.

The heuristic, and what each step is for

The manifold's job is to span the largest support polygon it can, because §2 showed that polygon is what resists rotation. So:

  1. Keep the deepest point. It is the one the solver most has to fix, and starting here is why the reduction never loses depth.
  2. Keep the point farthest from it. That fixes the longest axis of the set.
  3. and 4. Keep the points maximising triangle area on each side of that axis, which is what stops the four collapsing onto a line.

Every tie is broken by the lower index, so the result is a deterministic function of the clip output. It has to be: a manifold that reorders itself when nothing moved produces four new contact_ids and throws away every warm start.

The output is left in cyclic order — 0, 2, 1, 3 — rather than selection order, so the four points trace the polygon rather than a bowtie. That costs nothing and makes support_area and every debug drawing correct by construction.

One side empty is not a reason to return three points

A detail worth four lines. Steps 3 and 4 can find nothing on one side, which happens when the deepest point and the one farthest from it are adjacent on the contact polygon rather than opposite. The first version returned three points there, and the three points it kept spanned half the polygon. 

    if (i2 < 0 || i3 < 0)
    {
        int& empty = (i2 < 0) ? i2 : i3;
        float best = 0.0f;
        for (int i = 0; i < n; ++i)
        {
            if (i == i0 || i == i1 || i == i2 || i == i3) { continue; }
            const float area = std::fabs(
                signed_area2(pts[i0].position, pts[i1].position, pts[i].position, normal));
            if (area > best) { best = area; empty = i; }
        }
    }

The measured gain is small — mean area kept over 61,455 manifolds rose from 88.27% of the true contact polygon to 88.87% — and the size of the gain is not the reason to keep it. The reason is that a three-point manifold where four points exist resists rotation about one axis for no reason at all, which is a shape of failure rather than a magnitude of one.

Measuring the heuristic against the optimum

Here is the honest comparison, and it needed thinking about before it could be made.

Four points can never span an octagon: the largest quadrilateral inscribed in a regular octagon is 70.7% of it. So a ratio against the whole clipped polygon measures the shape of the contact as much as the heuristic. The question the heuristic is actually asking is “are these the best four?”, and with at most eight points that is seventy combinations — cheap enough to brute-force:

9. Identity, and what survives a frame

The manifold is built. Now the second half of the lesson's title, and the reason for it is 8.10's solver rather than 8.7's geometry.

A sequential-impulse solver converges over many iterations, and sixty times a second it gets to run a handful. Warm starting — beginning each contact from the impulse that held it last frame — is what turns “not converged yet” into “converged four frames ago and still is”, and it is the single largest difference between a stack that settles and a stack that sinks. 8.10 builds it. This lesson builds the thing it needs: a way to say that this contact is that contact, one frame later.

Why not position

The obvious answer is to match contacts by where they are. It fails, and it fails in the way that is hardest to catch: it works perfectly right up until the scene gets interesting.

§9 runs a crate landing on a floor for 600 frames at 60 Hz and asks, on each frame, how many of that frame's contact points can be matched to the previous frame's: 

     frames with a contact on both sides : 591
     contact points to match             : 1969
     matched by ID                       : 1620  (82.28%)
     matched by position, 10 microns     : 0  (0.00%)
     matched by position, 10 millimetres : 1618  (82.17%)

Zero at ten micrometres. A crate falling at walking pace travels centimetres per frame, so no tolerance that is tight enough to distinguish two corners of the same face is loose enough to follow one corner across a frame. Widen it to ten millimetres and it works — on this fixture, at this speed, on a crate one metre wide. Halve the crate or double the speed and it stops.

And the version of the experiment that looks like a success is the one to be suspicious of. Hold the same crate at rest and jitter it by a hundred nanometres a frame, which is what a settled body in a real solver does: 

     frames                              : 1999
     matched by ID                       : 100.00%
     matched by position, 10 microns     : 100.00%

A position matcher works perfectly on a body that is not moving. That is exactly why it is a trap: it passes the test you would write for it, and the failure arrives with the motion.

The deeper reason is 8.6 §7's, and this is its third appearance in the module. A contact point's position is a float computed by a different route through the same code on each of the two frames — different clip parameters, a different t, a different reference vertex to subtract from. Asking whether two such numbers are equal is asking a question that has no correct answer in floating point.

What an id is made of

Every field of a contact_id is a discrete index into geometry that does not move. A face's own identity — 0–5 for a box, a hash of the coplanar vertex set for a hull. A vertex's own index on its shape — 0–7 in obb::corners' order. None of them is computed from a position, so none of them can change because a body moved by a micron. They change when the contact changes, which is the definition of what an id is for. 

struct contact_id
{
    std::uint16_t reference_face = 0;
    std::uint16_t incident_face = 0;
    std::uint8_t reference_index = 0xff;
    std::uint8_t incident_index = 0xff;
    contact_feature kind = contact_feature::point;
    std::uint8_t flipped = 0;
};

Six bytes of named fields rather than a packed integer. Box2D famously folds the equivalent into four, and it is right to: it stores one per contact point in a structure it copies constantly. Here the id sits inside a 40-byte contact_point that already carries three impulses and a position, so the two bytes buy nothing and cost every reader a shift and a mask. Pack when the packing pays; name the fields when it does not.

The 8.4 connection is worth noticing. obb::corners' doc comment, written a lesson before any of this existed, says: “8.7's manifolds identify contact features by index, and two conventions for corner 3 would be a bug that only appears when a box changes type.” It is honoured literally — corner 3 of a box's face is corner 3 of the box — which is why contact_face carries a per-vertex id array at all rather than relying on the local index within the face.

Matching, and why a linear scan

int carry_impulses(contact_manifold& fresh, const contact_manifold& previous)
{
    int matched = 0;
    for (int i = 0; i < fresh.count; ++i)
    {
        for (int j = 0; j < previous.count; ++j)
        {
            if (!(fresh.points[i].id == previous.points[j].id)) { continue; }
            fresh.points[i].normal_impulse = previous.points[j].normal_impulse;
            fresh.points[i].tangent_impulse[0] = previous.points[j].tangent_impulse[0];
            fresh.points[i].tangent_impulse[1] = previous.points[j].tangent_impulse[1];
            fresh.points[i].warm = true;
            ++matched;
            break;
        }
    }
    fresh.warm_points = matched;
    return matched;
}

Four against four, in two arrays that are already in cache because the caller just wrote one of them. Sixteen comparisons of six bytes. A map keyed on the id would be correct, slower, and would allocate.

The control that was wrong, and what it taught

§9's first control teleported the crate two metres sideways between two frames, expected zero id matches, and got four out of four. The manifold was right and the control was wrong.

A contact_id names features: the floor's top face, the crate's bottom face, corners 0 to 3. Move the crate anywhere on that floor and the same corner really is on the same face, so the same id is the correct answer to the question an id asks. Ids cannot detect a teleport and should not be asked to — a body that was moved rather than simulated is a discontinuity, and invalidating its cached manifolds is the cache's job.

The control that does work is the one that changes the feature: 

     slid 2 m along the same face : 4 of 4 matched
     rolled onto its side         : 0 of 4 matched

A control that convicts the code of the test's own mistake is the most expensive kind there is, because it looks like a finding. 8.6 §A.2 had the happy version of this — a control that fired and was right — and 7.8 §G had the other one, a starvation counter that fired on the healthy case. The tell here was that the “failure” was too clean: four of four is not what a broken matcher looks like.

The tie that does not fire, and the knob that came back out

Every 2D physics engine carries a bias on the reference choice, and the reasoning is sound: two equally parallel faces make the choice a coin toss, a bare > decides it on rounding, and a flip renames all four contacts at once because flipped is part of the identity. This lesson shipped that knob in draft, and §9 measured it: 

     population        pairs   cos tie   flips   ids kept
     free boxes        67955    0.00%      0   100.00%
     crates on crates 120000  100.00%      0   100.00%
     hex prisms        20000  100.00%      0   100.00%

     207955 pairs, 0 flips.

Zero. And the third column says why: on a face contact both cosines are exactly 1.0f, because each face really is perpendicular to the contact normal. The tie is exact, and an exact tie is resolved deterministically — a wins, this frame and every frame. A quantity that merely ought to be equal would be a coin toss; one that is equal is not. The hazard belongs to the quantity, not to the comparison.

A measurement of zero needs a control more than any other kind, because “it never happened” and “I was not measuring anything” produce the same number. The flip cannot be provoked by a nudge — that is the result — so §9's control forces one the only way it can, by swapping the two arguments: 

     cos_a = 1.000000000   cos_b = 1.000000000
     bit-identical                   : yes
     ids that survive the swap       : 0 of 4

So the flip is not harmless — it costs every warm start on the pair — and it does not fire. The knob came out, under 8.6 §12's rule that a change buying nothing still costs a reader.

That control doubles as a warning about argument order, and it is the one thing in this file most likely to bite. pair_key(a, b) is deliberately order-independent, because a broadphase has no reason to report a pair the same way round twice. The manifold is not: hand the same pair to collide_manifold the other way round on one frame and the normal reverses, the reference moves to the other shape, and every id changes. Generate with the same shape as a every frame, and 8.8's broadphase adopts “lower index first” for exactly this reason.

Where the manifolds live

carry_impulses knows how to match two manifolds. Something has to remember which manifold belonged to which pair, across a frame in which the set of pairs changed completely.

manifold_cache is an open-addressed table with linear probing and no tombstones: end_frame rebuilds from the entries that were touched. That is O(capacity) once a frame, against O(pairs) of work the caller already spent walking them, and it buys a table that never rots. A deleted-marker scheme degrades until a rehash, and a physics cache deletes on most frames, because pairs are born and die as things move. 

    cache.begin_frame();
    for (pair : pairs) {
        manifold m = collide_manifold(a, b);
        if (const contact_manifold* old = cache.find(key)) { carry_impulses(m, *old); }
        solve(m);
        cache.store(key, m);
    }
    cache.end_frame();

Three calls, and skipping the last one is a leak that presents as a slow frame every few seconds. §9 runs 400 frames of a 60-body scene where pairs are born and die every frame: 

     stores / hits / drops : 22807 / 990 / 21766
     peak live pairs       : 79
     slots at the end      : 128
     worst load factor     : 0.500
     allocations           : 749, over 400 frames

The table grows at half full, which is where linear probing stops being cheap: the expected probe length is (1 + 1/(1−load)²)/2, which is 2 at half full and 13 at ninety per cent. A physics cache is walked once per pair per frame, so the probe length is multiplied by everything touching everything.

And the hash is SplitMix64's finaliser rather than the key itself, which matters here more than it usually does. A pair key is two small integers side by side, so its low bits are one body's index; a table indexed by key & mask would put every pair involving the same body into the same handful of slots. Every body in a stack collides with its neighbour, so that is not a hypothetical — it is the arrangement the cache exists for.

10. Watching it, and breaking it on purpose

demos/manifold draws two panels. The left one is the world: the two shapes, the contact points as filled dots, and the support polygon they span drawn as a closed loop, with its area printed. The right one is the same contact seen face on, in the reference face's own plane — and S applies one Sutherland–Hodgman side plane at a time, with the plane about to be applied drawn dashed and its outward normal as a short stub.

As with gjk and epa, it runs its own clipper. A single-stepping visualiser needs the iteration, not the answer — and it uses only support_face and collide_manifold, both public, so 5.1's boundary is intact. The engine's own answer is printed beside the picture, so the two can be seen to agree.

Four things to do with it, in order:

  1. Press 1 and look at the left panel. Four points, a square support polygon, an area of 1.0 m². Then 4: a ball on the same floor gets one point and an area of zero, and that is not a shortfall.
  2. Press 2 and hold Z. The crate tilts, and somewhere around a degree the manifold drops from four points to two — the two corners still below the floor's plane. The support polygon collapses to a line and its area goes to zero. §2's “a crate on two points is a crate on a hinge”, happening.
  3. Press A to stop the auto-run, then S four times. The right panel applies one side plane per press. On preset 1 nothing is cut, because the crate's face is wholly inside the floor's; drag it to the floor's edge with the arrow keys and watch the crossings appear.
  4. Press W, then drive the crate around with the arrows. The panel reports how many of this frame's points matched last frame's by id. It stays at full while the crate slides, because the ids name features. Tip it far enough with Z and it drops to zero for exactly one frame, which is correct.

Preset 5 is the one that shows §5's truncation: a twelve-sided prism on a floor clips to eight points, and the four the reduction keeps are spread around the octagon rather than bunched. Preset 3 is two crates crossed at 45°, which is an edge contact — the panel says so, and deliberately does not draw a clip that did not happen.

11. The budget, and a knob that came back out

Twenty thousand overlapping box pairs, on a release library: 

     GJK distance only            :    87.3 ns/pair
     GJK + EPA                    :   252.0 ns/pair
     build_manifold alone         :   142.9 ns/pair
     the whole narrow phase       :   395.1 ns/pair
     one support_face             :     5.2 ns

The manifold is 36% of the narrow phase and the penetration depth is still what costs. That ordering is worth internalising, because the instinct is the other way round: clipping feels like the expensive part, and it is four passes over at most eight vertices with no square roots, while EPA is eight to sixteen expansions each rebuilding a chunk of a surface.

Nothing allocates. Measured by replacing the global operator new and counting — 6.17 §9's method, for the third lesson running — twenty thousand whole narrow-phase queries make zero allocations. Both contact_faces, the clip buffers and the manifold live on the stack.

8.6 §12 deleted four default member initialisers and bought 20.5%. The same question here buys nothing, and the reason is the one 8.6 named: the tell was the ARRAY, not the struct. polytope held 128 faces, so one initialiser on one member made polytope p; write four kilobytes that the next line overwrote, and expand rebuilt a three-kilobyte horizon array once per pass. A contact_face is 124 bytes, a query builds exactly two of them once, and there is no array of them anywhere. So vec3's own initialisers are left alone and v is documented as written-before-read.

And the other thing that came out. §9's reference_bias was written for a real hazard, measured at zero over 208,000 pairs across three shape mixes, and removed — leaving a > and a comment explaining why it is safe here and would not be in a formulation that compared two separations. That is 8.6's discipline applied to a feature rather than to a micro-optimisation, and it is the harder half of it: deleting something that works because it does not do anything.

12. What this does not do

Five honest gaps, each named rather than discovered later.

Nothing consumes the manifold yet. normal_impulse and tangent_impulse are declared, carried across frames, and written by nobody. 8.9 resolves a contact with an impulse and 8.10 accumulates and warm-starts. They are declared here rather than later because the whole point of the id is to make them survive, and retrofitting them would mean rewriting the matching.

There is no speculative margin. A pair a micron short of touching produces nothing at all — collide_manifold returns a manifold with status == none. That is the honest answer to “are these in contact” and the wrong answer for a solver, which would rather see the contact one frame early so that it arrives with a warm impulse already on it. keep_slop is the hook; 8.9 turns it up, in the one place that should own the decision.

A hull still has no faces. §5 measured both ends of the gather tolerance and there is no value that is right, because the question is topological. A real fix is a face list on the collider — computed offline by a hull builder, which 8.5 §13 already said was out of scope and named the algorithm for. Until then, hand-authored colliders with genuinely flat faces work well and tessellated curved ones are approximated.

An edge contact's point is not on either surface at depth. §7 measured it: the midpoint of two crossed edges is within a few millimetres of both at the depths a solver maintains, and half a metre from one on a deep overlap. There is no convention that does better, because there is no single point where two deeply crossed boxes touch — but a solver that lets overlaps get deep will see it, and that is one more reason not to.

And the normal still jumps. §1 measured the ninety-degree swing and this lesson does not remove it, because it cannot: it is a property of a minimum. What has changed is that the swing is now a discrete event that renames a handful of ids, rather than four points re-derived from scratch every frame. 8.10's solver will need to survive it, and knowing that it is a feature change rather than noise is what makes that possible.

15. Common pitfalls & debugging

Symptom: the manifold is empty on a contact that plainly exists.
Cause: the reference polygon's winding is clockwise about its normal rather than counter-clockwise, so every side plane built as cross(edge, normal) faces inward and classifies everything as outside.
Fix: check one face by hand. Take three consecutive vertices a, b, c and confirm dot(cross(b − a, c − b), normal) > 0. §5's control does exactly this and reports 0 of 4 incident vertices accepted when the winding is reversed — which is the quietest way there is to lose a floor, because nothing errors.

Symptom: contact points are on the far side of one of the shapes.
Cause: one of the two face_of calls has the wrong sign. a looks along +n and b looks back along −n, because the normal points from a toward b.
Fix: assert that dot(fa.normal, n) > 0 and dot(fb.normal, −n) > 0. The failure looks, on screen, exactly like a body being flung across the level by a solver that is working perfectly, which is why it is worth an assertion rather than a comment.

Symptom: a crate rocks on a floor even with four contact points.
Cause: probably not the manifold. Print support_area(): if it is near zero the manifold has four points on a line, which resists nothing about that line. If it is the size of the face, the rocking is the solver's, and 8.9 and 8.10 are where it goes.
Fix: if the area really is collapsing, check face_cos — §6's table shows the mean contact count falling as it tightens, and a crate one hundredth of a degree off a face normal dropping to an edge contact is exactly this.

Symptom: warm starting does nothing; warm_points is always zero.
Cause: the two manifolds being matched were generated with the shapes the other way round. pair_key is order-independent and the manifold is not.
Fix: fix the order at the broadphase — lower body index first — and check by printing reference_on_b on consecutive frames. If it alternates, the arguments are alternating.

Symptom: a tessellated cylinder's side contact is two points, not four.
Cause: the gather tolerance is tighter than the angle between the cylinder's consecutive face normals, so the “face” recovered from the supporting plane is the single edge between two of them.
Fix: raise gather_sin for that asset, or — properly — give the hull a face list. §5 sweeps both ends and says plainly that there is no value that is right.

Symptom: contact depths are slightly wrong on hull contacts.
Cause: the face's normal is being taken as the query direction rather than computed from the gathered polygon. The two can differ by asin(k_face_gather_sin) — nearly three degrees — and the reference plane is what every depth is measured from.
Fix: Newell's method over the sorted polygon, oriented to agree with the query. And note that it has to be after the sort: Newell's sum over vertices in exporter order is not the face's normal.

Symptom: a contact point's position is nowhere near either surface.
Cause: you are measuring the midpoint. It is defined as halfway between the two surfaces, so on a deep overlap it is legitimately depth/2 from each.
Fix: recover the surface points — A's is position + normal·depth/2, B's is position − normal·depth/2 — and measure those. §7's first instrument made exactly this mistake and reported a 0.63 m “bug” that was a fact about the convention.

16. Exercises

  1. Do the statics by hand, then check it. A 20 kg crate 0.8 m wide rests on four contact points at its base corners. Write down the largest moment about its centre of mass that the contact set can resist, then set up the same crate in a simulate_rest-style loop and bisect for the value at which it tips. Explain the sign of the discrepancy.

    Hint. m·g·(w/2) = 20 × 9.81 × 0.4 = 78.48 N·m. The measurement should come out a couple of per cent low, for the reason §1 gives: the crate's effective lip is inside its geometric one by however much penetration the solver allows.

  2. Break the winding, and watch where it does not error. In support_face(const obb&, vec3), swap f.v[1] and f.v[3]. Rebuild and run the demo. What does preset 1 show? What does the harness say? Then swap them back and instead reverse the sign of side in the clipper, and compare the two failures.

    Hint. One of them empties the manifold; the other keeps everything, including points outside the reference face. Both are silent, and only one of them is caught by a test that checks the points are on the surfaces.

  3. Measure the fifth point yourself. Extend §8's brute-force comparison to ask what the best five points span, as a fraction of the whole clipped polygon. Then argue — from §2's arithmetic, not from the number — why the answer does not change what a solver can do.

    Hint. Five points span more area. The question a solver asks is whether the resultant can act anywhere in the support polygon, and it already could.

  4. Give hull its faces. Add an optional std::span<const face_range> to hull, where each entry names a run of indices into a shared index array. Build one by hand for a hexagonal prism, make support_face use it when present and fall back to the gather when it is not, and re-run §5's tolerance sweep. Report what the sweep now looks like.

    Hint. With real faces the sweep should be flat: the tolerance has nothing to decide, because the face is named rather than recovered. That flatness is the whole argument for doing it.

  5. An engine challenge: make the swing survivable. §1 measured a 90° normal swing between two consecutive frames. Write an experiment that runs a box sliding past a wall's end at a realistic speed, prints the manifold's ids and normal on every frame, and reports how many frames the ids survive on either side of the crossing. Then propose — and measure — a policy for what a solver should do on the frame the normal flips.

    Hint. Two candidate policies worth comparing: drop the warm impulses on a flip (safe, and the stack loses a frame of convergence), or rotate them into the new normal's basis (keeps magnitude, and is wrong if the flip is genuine). Measure which one settles a stack faster, and note that you now have a reason to prefer one rather than a preference.

17. Recap & next

The engine can now say where two shapes touch, not just how far into each other they are. A contact_manifold is up to four points sharing one normal, each with its own depth and its own identity, and the identity is what lets 8.10 begin each contact from the impulse that held it last frame.

The four things worth carrying forward:

  • A manifold is a support polygon, and a support polygon is a statics problem. A body rests when its centre of mass projects inside the convex hull of its contact points, and the moment that hull can carry is m·g·(w/2) — predicted at 49.05 N·m and bisected at 47.87. One contact point spans a hull of zero area and holds a crate up only by chattering, at 27.99 °/s of permanent rocking and 21 mm of sink.
  • A support function determines a shape and names no feature. Four thousand directions from a cone a thousandth of a degree wide return all four corners of the same face, deterministically and arbitrarily. The fix is one more query on the shapes, not a cleverer reading of EPA's output — and epa.hpp was not touched.
  • Clip, filter, reduce. Sutherland–Hodgman against the reference face's side planes, keep what is actually penetrating, and take the deepest point plus the three that span the most area. That reduction reaches 96.3% of the best four there are and loses no depth at all.
  • Match on identity, never on position. A crate's landing matches 0.00% of contacts at a ten-micrometre position tolerance and 82% by id, rising to 100% of 1,999 consecutive frames once it settles. And a position matcher passes every test you would write for it, because it only fails once things move.

Two smaller things that are really the same thing: a control that convicts the code of the test's own mistake looks exactly like a finding, and a knob written for a real hazard can measure zero and should then come out.
