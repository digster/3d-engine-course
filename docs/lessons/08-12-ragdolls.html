Ragdolls: Joints on a Skeleton

Module 7 gave the engine a character that moves because a clip says so; Module 8 gave it bodies that move because Newton says so. A ragdoll is the one object that has to be both, one after the other. This lesson turns 7.7's 23-joint humanoid into eleven capsules — Dempster's segment masses, four hinges, six sockets with a swing cone and a twist range — and hands a jogging character to the solver in mid-stride and back. The angles come first, and they are a swing–twist split, not Euler angles: Euler's sensitivity reaches 609.9 at an arm raised forward, where swing–twist reads 1.55. The swing's row is exact; the obvious twist row is not. The twist's rate is the half-way axis over cos(φ/2), and a row about the bone is wrong by the rate at which the swing's plane turns — which, integrated round a loop, is the loop's solid angle: Codman's paradox, reproduced to three decimals. Then the character. The handoff keeps its momentum exactly — 204.40 kg·m/s — because a steered body's velocity is the chord its integrator inverts; the return blends from the pose read off the bodies, realigned over the pelvis, and moves no bone by more than 0.06 mm. And the ragdoll found two bugs the module had shipped: a violated hinge limit was never repaired under split impulse, because 8.11's position pass let the far stop cancel the near one; and a moving kinematic body never woke anything it touched, so an animated character ran straight through a crate that had fallen asleep. Both are fixed, and 8.10's and 8.11's harnesses still agree with their own pages everywhere but one table.

1. The problem: a character that cannot fall

Put 7.7's walking character on a ledge and walk it off. Nothing happens. It walks on air, at a constant height, until the clip loops — because nothing in the animation system knows what a ledge is, or gravity, or that a foot has to be on something. A clip is a function from time to pose (7.7's sentence), and that is its strength: it plays back exactly what an animator made. It is also why a game character hit by a truck plays a canned "hit" animation that ends with it lying in the same position on a staircase as on a flat floor.

Module 8 has built the opposite kind of object. A body under 8.11's joints falls, swings, hangs and piles up, because the solver computes where it goes; nobody authored the pose it lands in. What a ragdoll wants is both, in sequence: the clip while the character is in control, the solver the moment it is not — tripped, shot, knocked off the ledge — and the clip again when it gets up. That turns out to be three problems, and only the first is the one the lesson title promises:

  1. Joints that behave like a body's. 8.11's ball-socket lets a limb go anywhere — a shoulder that swings the arm through the chest, or twists it round a full turn. A hinge is right for an elbow and a knee; a shoulder, a hip, the neck and the waist need a ball-socket with two limits: how far the limb may swing away from a rest direction, and how far it may twist about itself. Writing those limits needs two angles, and choosing the angles is most of §3 to §5.
  2. A handoff that does not pop. At the instant the clip lets go, the bodies have to be where the animation put them and moving the way it was moving them. Get the position wrong and the character teleports; get the velocity wrong and it stops dead in mid-stride and then crumples like a puppet with its strings cut. §9 measures both.
  3. A return the physics does not fight. Getting up means the clip owns the bodies again, starting from wherever they landed. The tempting implementation leaves the bodies dynamic and writes the animated pose into them every frame — 8.11 §1's teleport, now as a real design — and §12 measures what the solver does to a body whose velocity no longer describes its motion.

And, as every lesson in this module has found, the first real use of the solver on a new kind of object measures things nobody planned to. This one found two bugs that earlier lessons shipped, and five claims its own sections were written to make that the measurements refused. Each is told where it happened.

2. Building intuition: a skeleton with mass, and one owner at a time

Start from what a ragdoll is. 7.6's skeleton is a tree of frames: each joint has a parent and a local transform, and composing down the tree gives every joint's place in the character's model space. A ragdoll is that tree with mass. Some of the joints — not all of them — get a rigid body: a capsule, a mass and an inertia tensor, bolted to the joint's frame. Those are the parts. Each part is joined to its nearest ancestor part by one of 8.11's joints, anchored at the skeleton joint the part follows, so a shoulder joint sits at the shoulder.

Why not every joint? Because 7.7's humanoid has a clavicle, a hand, a foot and a toe on each side, three spine joints and a head, and giving each of those a body would make a hand a 0.48 kg body hinged to a 1.3 kg forearm — ratios the solver would pay for (§8) for motion nobody would see. So eleven parts, the classic ragdoll: pelvis, torso, head, two upper arms, two forearms (with the hand's mass folded in), two thighs and two shins (with the foot's). The other twelve joints are passengers: each rides on the nearest part above it, at whatever local transform the clip last gave it, so a ragdoll's hand stays at the end of its forearm and its toes stay on its foot while the solver owns the limb.

Figure 1 also shows the second idea, which is the one that keeps the code small: every body has exactly one owner at a time, and the owner is who writes its velocity. While the character is animated the clip owns the bodies. They are kinematic — no mass, moved only by their own velocity, 8.2's kind of body for a lift — and each step the ragdoll sets that velocity to whatever lands the body exactly on the animated pose at the end of the step. They still collide, so a jogging character pushes crates, at the speed its limbs really move. While the character is simulated the solver owns them: they are dynamic, their joints are in the solver, and the pose the skinning code draws is read back off them.

With that rule the two handoffs are nearly nothing. Animation to physics is simulate(): give each body its mass back. Its velocity is already right, because the ragdoll has been steering it — and §9 shows that for this engine's integrator "already right" is exact, not an approximation. Physics to animation is animate(): take the mass away and start steering again, toward a pose that begins where the bodies lie and blends to the clip. The alternative — two systems writing the same body — is what §12 is about.

Worked, with real numbers. The masses come from Dempster's cadaver measurements as tabulated by Winter (Table 4.1): pelvis 0.142 of body mass, thorax and abdomen 0.355, head and neck 0.081, upper arm 0.028, forearm and hand 0.022, thigh 0.100, leg and foot 0.061. For an 80 kg person that is 11.36 kg of pelvis, 28.40 of torso, 6.48 of head, 2.24 per upper arm, 1.76 per forearm, 8.00 per thigh and 4.88 per shin — and the fractions sum to exactly 1.000 (0.142 + 0.355 + 0.081 + 2 × (0.028 + 0.022 + 0.100 + 0.061)). The heaviest part is the torso and the lightest the forearm, 16.1 : 1. Hold on to that number: 8.11 §14 measured what a 10:1 chain does to eight sweeps.

3. Swing and twist

A shoulder joint is a ball-socket, and 8.11's ball-socket already holds the arm's top to the torso. What it cannot say is "not like that". To limit a rotation you need numbers that describe it — angles — and a limit is only as good as the angles it is a limit on. The first choice most engines make is the one this course made for a camera in 7.2: three Euler angles, each with a range. 7.2 also measured why that fails: when the middle angle reaches 90° the first and third rotate about the same axis, the split into three stops being unique, and the angles jump.

For a camera, 90° of pitch means looking straight up, and a game can simply forbid it. For a shoulder in the engine's YXZ convention, the middle angle is the rotation about x — and an arm raised straight forward is exactly 90° of it. That is not an edge of the shoulder's range. It is the middle of it: reaching for a door handle.

Two angles that describe a limb

Look at what a limb actually does, instead of at three axes. The arm has a bone — a direction, t at rest. Any rotation q sends it somewhere, to c = q t. The angle between t and c is how far the arm has swung away from rest, and that is a single, perfectly well-defined number however the arm got there. What is left, once the bone's direction is accounted for, is how far the arm has turned about itself. So split the rotation in two:

\[ q \;=\; \underbrace{s}_{\text{swing}}\;\underbrace{\tau}_{\text{twist}}, \qquad \tau\ \text{turns about}\ t, \qquad s\ \text{turns about an axis}\ \perp t \] q = swing * twist: the twist turns about the bone axis t, the swing about an axis perpendicular to it

Read right to left, 7.4's convention: first twist the arm about its own bone, which leaves the bone where it was, then swing the bone to its new direction. Because the twist leaves t alone, q t = s t: the swing alone decides where the bone points. And because the swing's axis is perpendicular to t, it is the smallest rotation that carries t to c — any other would add a turn about the bone, which is the twist's job.

7.4 already built the smallest rotation between two directions, without knowing it. A reflection in the plane perpendicular to a unit vector n is n v n, and two reflections make a rotation by twice the angle between the mirrors, about the line they share. Reflect first in the plane perpendicular to t, then in the plane perpendicular to the half-way vector h = (t + c)/|t + c|: the mirrors are ∠(t, h) = φ/2 apart, so the rotation is by φ, about an axis perpendicular to both — and it carries t to c. That is the swing, as a product of two pure quaternions:

\[ s \;=\; \pm\, h\,t, \qquad h \;=\; \frac{t + c}{\lvert t + c\rvert} \] swing = +/- (h t): the rotor of two mirrors, first perpendicular to t, then to the half-way vector h

— the sign is 7.4's double cover, and the engine keeps the w ≥ 0 representative. §5 is going to differentiate this form, and it is the reason the twist's rate comes out the way it does. For computing, there is a shorter route. The twist's quaternion has its imaginary part along t, so take q, keep only the part of q.v along t, and renormalise; the swing is whatever turns that into q:

\[ \tau \;=\; \frac{\bigl(q_w,\;(q_v\cdot t)\,t\bigr)}{\sqrt{q_w^2 + (q_v\cdot t)^2}}, \qquad s \;=\; q\,\tau^{*} \] twist = normalise(q.w, (q.v . t) t), swing = q * conjugate(twist)

Worked, with real numbers. An upper arm with its bone along +x, twisted 30° about itself and then raised 60° about +z. The two rotations are (cos 15°, sin 15°, 0, 0) = (0.9659, 0.2588, 0, 0) and (cos 30°, 0, 0, sin 30°) = (0.8660, 0, 0, 0.5000), and 7.4's product gives q = (0.8365, 0.2241, 0.1294, 0.4830) — every component mixed. Now split it about x: the part of q.v along x is 0.2241, so the twist is (0.8365, 0.2241, 0, 0) divided by √(0.8365² + 0.2241²) = √0.7500 = 0.8660, which is (0.9659, 0.2588, 0, 0) — 30° about x, recovered. And q τ* is (0.8660, 0, 0, 0.5000), 60° about z. The harness prints both, to four decimals.

Over twenty thousand random rotations and axes the harness finds s τ rebuilding q to 4.956 × 10⁻⁷, the swing's axis perpendicular to the bone to 1.461 × 10⁻⁷, the swing sending the bone where q does to 8.663 × 10⁻⁷, and the two-mirror rotor agreeing with the projected swing to 1.272 × 10⁻⁵ — the last number larger than the others for a reason worth a sentence: the mirror form divides by |t + c| = 2 cos(φ/2), and the harness includes swings up to 179°, where that is 0.017.

Where each split breaks

That division is the swing–twist split's one weakness. When c = −t — the bone swung a full 180° — there is no half-way vector, every axis perpendicular to t is equally "smallest", and the twist is undefined. So the honest comparison with Euler angles is: where does each one lose its conditioning? The harness perturbs a rotation by a milliradian in 400 random directions and reports the largest change in any extracted angle, per radian of nudge, for a hanging arm raised forward by increasing amounts: 

     raised    Euler (yaw/pitch/roll)   swing-twist
        0.0               1.0              1.00      1/cos(pitch)      1.0   1/cos(swing/2)    1.00
       45.0               1.4              1.10      1/cos(pitch)      1.4   1/cos(swing/2)    1.08
       80.0               5.8              1.41      1/cos(pitch)      5.8   1/cos(swing/2)    1.31
       89.0              57.3              1.53      1/cos(pitch)     57.3   1/cos(swing/2)    1.40
       89.9             609.9              1.55      1/cos(pitch)    573.0   1/cos(swing/2)    1.41
      135.0               1.4              3.28      1/cos(pitch)        -   1/cos(swing/2)    2.61
      170.0               1.0             16.81      1/cos(pitch)        -   1/cos(swing/2)   11.47
      179.0               1.0            176.33      1/cos(pitch)        -   1/cos(swing/2)  114.59

Euler angles track 1/cos(pitch) to the digit until 89°, and at 89.9° a milliradian of nudge moves an angle by 0.61 radians — 35° of output from a sixteenth of a degree of input. A limit tested on those angles does not fail at the lock; it fails in a band around it, by jittering the arm between two poses that are both legal. Swing–twist reads 1.55 at the same pose. It does blow up, as the control rows show — 16.8 at 170°, 176 at 179° — and it tracks the 1/cos(φ/2) that §5 is about to derive, with a factor of about 1.5 on top that comes from reporting the swing as a rotation vector, whose direction is itself poorly defined near 180°.

So the useful statement is about where the singularity is, and figure 2 draws it: Euler's sits in the middle of the range an arm uses; swing–twist's sits at the antipode of the axis the swing is measured from. That last clause decides a design choice. Measure the swing from the bind pose's bone and an arm raised straight overhead from a hanging rest is near its antipode — the 170° row. Measure it from the middle of the cone the arm is allowed to move in, and the antipode is by construction outside the cone: a limit never has to evaluate its angles anywhere near the singularity, because it stops the limb long before. That is why the engine's cone-twist joint stores a cone axis separately from the bone, and §6's shoulder tilts it to point between "out" and "down".

4. The swing cone is a limit like any other

The swing angle is the angle between two unit vectors: the cone's axis as the parent body carries it, a₁, and the bone as the child carries it, b₁. So cos φ = a₁ · b₁, and a limit is C = φ_max − φ ≥ 0 — a one-sided constraint of exactly 8.11's shape. What it needs is a Jacobian row: the direction in the relative angular velocity along which φ changes. Differentiate the cosine. The parent's axis turns at ω_a × a₁ and the child's at ω_b × b₁, so

\[ \frac{d}{dt}(a_1\cdot b_1) \;=\; (\omega_a\times a_1)\cdot b_1 + a_1\cdot(\omega_b\times b_1) \;=\; (\omega_a - \omega_b)\cdot(a_1\times b_1) \] d(a1 . b1)/dt = (w_a - w_b) . (a1 x b1), by the scalar triple product twice

and the left side is −sin φ · φ̇, while |a₁ × b₁| = sin φ. Divide:

\[ \dot\varphi \;=\; (\omega_b - \omega_a)\cdot \hat n, \qquad \hat n \;=\; \frac{a_1\times b_1}{\lvert a_1\times b_1\rvert} \] dphi/dt = (w_b - w_a) . n, with n = a1 x b1 normalised

Exactly — no approximation, no correction factor. So Ċ = −(ω_b − ω_a) · n̂, which is 8.11's angular_row(−n̂): the hinge's upper stop, with n̂ for its axis. Bounds [0, ∞); speculative when C > 0, with target −C/h; bias when violated. Everything 8.11 built for a stop applies unchanged. The only new case is on the cone's axis itself, where n̂ does not exist — and that is the one place the limb is as far from its limit as it can be, so the row is simply left out. Speculation then misses only a cone the limb could cross from dead centre in a single step, which at 60 Hz is a spin faster than φ_max/h: 31 rad/s for a 30° cone.

The harness checks the row against a central difference of swing_angle over two thousand random joints, and they agree to 1.097 × 10⁻³ of the rate (a float difference's resolution); the same row with its sign flipped is off by 2.001, which is the control. Then it puts the row in the solver.

Speculation works on a cone exactly as on a hinge. A rod socketed through its centre of mass — 8.11's turnstile, where an angular row has no pin to converge against — spun at 3 rad/s into a 40° cone, over two hundred arrival phases (8.11's lesson: sample the phase, not the parameter): the speculative cone's worst overshoot is 1.19 × 10⁻⁷ rad, and the reactive one overshoots by a fraction of ω·h with mean 0.4977 — uniform on [0, 1], 8.10's arrival depth once again.

And it rebounds like a hinge stop. Socket the rod at its end, as a limb is, and the cone row — which sees only the rod's inertia about its own centre — converges against the pin at 8.11's ρ = m·d²/I_pivot per sweep, 0.7614 for this rod. With restitution zero, the rod comes back off the cone at:

        1    0.7614    1.0693

        4    0.3360    0.1850
        8    0.1129    0.0834

— below ρⁿ and closing on it: 0.55 of it at four sweeps, 0.74 at eight, 0.93 at sixteen, the same shape 8.11 found on a door, where eight sweeps gave 0.0753 against ρ⁸ = 0.0981. At the shipped eight sweeps a ragdoll's arm hitting its shoulder cone comes back at 8% of its speed. That is visible, and it is the handover's second limit, confirmed on the new row: a limb striking its swing limit bounces at about ρ⁸, and the fix that would remove it at any sweep count is still the limit-and-pin block 8.11 named. The one-sweep row is the odd one out — 1.07, faster than it arrived — because with one sweep the position pass's push out of the overshoot is most of what the rod feels.

5. The twist row, and Codman's paradox

The twist is the other limit. The engine already measures it: 8.11's hinge_angle projects the relative rotation onto the hinge axis, which is exactly the twist of a swing–twist split — a hinge's angle is a twist whose swing the alignment rows hold at zero. So a twist range is two one-sided rows, lower and upper, and the only question is their axis. The obvious answer is the bone: the twist is a turn about the bone, so the row should be angular_row(b₁). For a hinge that is right, because the swing is zero. For a shoulder it is wrong, and the reason is worth doing properly.

The twist's rate, derived

Work in the parent's frame, where the cone's axis is a constant t and the relative rotation is r = s τ. The relative angular velocity Ω drives it by 8.3's rule, ṙ = ½ Ω r. Differentiate the product:

\[ \tfrac12\,\Omega\,s\,\tau \;=\; \dot s\,\tau + s\,\dot\tau \;=\; \dot s\,\tau + \tfrac12\,\dot\theta\, s\,t\,\tau \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \Omega \;=\; \underbrace{2\,\dot s\, s^{*}}_{\omega_s} \;+\; \dot\theta\,\underbrace{s\,t\,s^{*}}_{c} \] Omega = w_s + theta_dot * c, where w_s = 2 s_dot conj(s) is the swing's own angular velocity and c = s t s* is the bone

The relative spin is the swing's own angular velocity plus the twist rate about the bone. So Ω · c = ω_s · c + θ̇, and the bone row would be right if the swing's angular velocity had no component along the bone. It does. Here is where the two-mirror form pays: the swing is s = h t with t constant, so ṡ = ḣ t and s* = t h, and

\[ \omega_s \;=\; 2\,\dot h\,t\,t\,h \;=\; -2\,\dot h\,h \;=\; 2\,h\times\dot h \] w_s = 2 h_dot t t h = -2 h_dot h = 2 h x h_dot, because t t = -1 and h_dot is perpendicular to the unit h

— which is perpendicular to h, and therefore to t + c. That is the direction along which the swing contributes nothing, so take the component of Ω along it, and use c · (t + c) = 1 + t · c:

\[ \dot\theta \;=\; \frac{\Omega\cdot(t + c)}{1 + t\cdot c} \;=\; \frac{\Omega\cdot h}{\cos(\varphi/2)} \] theta_dot = Omega . (t + c) / (1 + t . c) = Omega . h / cos(phi/2): the half-way axis, over cos(phi/2)

The twist row is the half-way axis divided by cos(φ/2). At zero swing h = t = c and it is the bone, which is why 8.11's hinge limit was right. As the swing grows the row tilts back toward the parent and lengthens — √2 at 90° — and at 180° it divides by zero, which is the singularity §3 found in the angles now found in their rate. And the bone row's error, ω_s · c, comes out in closed form: it is −(Ω⊥ · t)/(1 + cos φ), at most |Ω⊥| tan(φ/2), with Ω⊥ the relative spin across the bone.

Worked, with real numbers. A limb at a swing of 90° from a cone axis t = (0, −1, 0), its bone c = (1, 0, 0). The twist row's axis is (t + c)/(1 + t·c) = (1, −1, 0)/1, of length √2 = 1/cos 45°. Spin the limb about the cone's axis only — Ω = (0, −2, 0), the arm sweeping round the rim of its cone at 2 rad/s with no spin about itself in the parent's frame. The bone row reads Ω · c = 0: no twist. The correct row reads Ω · (1, −1, 0) = 2 rad/s of twist, and the formula agrees: Ω⊥ = Ω here, so the bone row's error is −(Ω⊥ · t)/(1 + cos 90°) = −2/1, and 0 − 2 = −2. An arm circling its shoulder cone is twisting, relative to the swing–twist frame, while turning about nothing — which is the paradox this section is named for, one derivative early.

The harness checks the three candidate rows against a central difference of the twist over forty thousand random joints, binned by swing. The half-way row agrees everywhere to the difference's own resolution — 6.3 × 10⁻⁴ near zero swing, growing to 5.8 × 10⁻³ near 175° as the 1/cos(φ/2) amplifies float noise. The bone and the cone-axis rows are wrong by more than the rate itself beyond a degree of swing — 1.6 to 6 times it. The bone row's error never exceeds 1.0045 of the |Ω⊥| tan(φ/2) bound, and at exactly zero swing, the control, all three rows agree to 9.5 × 10⁻⁷, because they are the same row.

Codman's paradox

Integrate the bone row's error round a loop and it has a name. In 1934 the surgeon Ernest Codman described it: hold your arm down by your side, palm in; raise it forward to the horizontal; swing it out to the side, still horizontal; lower it. You never turned your arm about itself, and your palm now faces forward — a quarter-turn of twist from nowhere. Biomechanics has argued about it since, and the lesson's derivation settles it in a line. "Not turning about itself" means Ω · c = 0 throughout. The twist then changes at −ω_s · c, and for a bone that goes round a closed loop that integrates to the solid angle the loop encloses on the unit sphere — the same geometric phase that turns a Foucault pendulum's plane of swing. The harness carries a body round loops with no spin about its bone, eight thousand steps each, and reads the swing–twist twist when it gets home: 

     loop                              twist after     solid angle
     Codman: forward, sideways, down     -90.000 deg       90.000 deg
     circle at swing  30.0                48.231 deg       48.231 deg
     circle at swing  60.0               180.000 deg      180.000 deg
     circle at swing  90.0               360.000 deg      360.000 deg
     circle at swing 120.0               540.000 deg      540.000 deg

Codman's octant — three quarter great circles, enclosing one eighth of the sphere, π/2 — leaves a quarter-turn to three decimals. A circle at swing φ encloses a cap of 2π(1 − cos φ): 48.231° at 30°, half a turn at 60°, a whole turn at 90°. Nothing in the harness computes a solid angle; it steps a body and reads a twist. The agreement is the derivation.

What the bone row does in a solver

The consequence is sharpest in a limb that circles its cone. The harness puts a rod on a fixed socket with a 60° cone and a ±20° twist range, sets it on the rim with no spin about itself and a velocity along the rim, and lets it go with no gravity. A great circle tangent to a small one leaves it outward, so the cone holds the rod on its rim and it goes round — and by the table above its twist must change by 180° a loop. The twist stop is at 20°. 

     twist rows             position pass   loops   worst |twist|   final swing
     half-way / cos         split impulse   4.49      20.00 deg      60.23 deg
     about the bone         split impulse   4.56      29.08 deg      60.15 deg
     about the bone         none            4.39     179.93 deg      73.33 deg
     predicted lag past the stop, naive rows: alpha'(1 - cos phi) h / beta = 9.55 deg

With the half-way rows the twist sits on its stop at 20.00° for four and a half loops. With the bone rows the section's first draft predicted that the twist would walk straight through the stop — the bone row reads Ω · c, which is zero for a rod with no spin about itself, so the row never sees the twist moving. The measurement refused it: 29.08°, and steady. The draft had forgotten the position pass. Split impulse measures the violation with the true twist — hinge_angle, not the row — and pushes back along the bone axis, which does move the twist, at unit rate. So the rod lags past its stop by the balance of two rates: the circling adds α̇(1 − cos φ)·h of twist a step and the correction removes β of the violation, which settles at 4 × 0.5 / 60 / 0.2 = 0.1667 rad = 9.55° predicted, against 9.08° measured (the rod slows as its joint dissipates, 8.11 §5). Only with the position pass turned off does the twist walk through, to 179.93°. The bone row is not a missing limit; it is a limit that holds a limb 9° wrong for as long as the limb keeps circling — which an arm windmilling in a fall does.

6. A skeleton with mass

With the angles settled, a ragdoll is a description and a build step. The description is written against the skeleton's bind pose, in model space, because that is where a person authoring one is looking: the character stands in its T-pose, capsules are drawn over it, and the numbers are read off the picture. For each part, ragdoll_part_desc names the skeleton joint the body follows (called bone, because this file has two kinds of joint in it and every part has one of each at the same place), the capsule's segment and radius, its share of the total mass, and how it hangs from its parent: a hinge axis and range, or a twist axis, a cone axis, a cone half-angle and a twist range.

build_ragdoll does four passes over that. It finds each part's parent part — the nearest ancestor skeleton joint that is also a part, so the upper arm hangs from the torso through a clavicle that is only a passenger. It stands each capsule along its segment (8.5's capsules run along body +y, so the body's orientation is 7.4's two-mirror rotor from +y to the bone) and records where the body sits in its bone's frame — the joint_from_body transform, constant, and the only link between the two representations. It authors each joint with 8.11's make_hinge or this lesson's make_cone_twist, at the bind pose, and keeps only the body-local result, which is valid at every pose. And it audits every pair of capsules for self-collision.

How good is a capsule as a limb?

A capsule puts a body's centre of mass at its middle and spreads its mass uniformly. A real limb does neither: a thigh is heavier at the hip. Dempster measured both on cadavers, and Winter's Table 4.1 gives each segment's centre of mass as a fraction of its length from the proximal joint and its radius of gyration about that centre. The number that matters to the solver is neither of those directly — it is 8.11's ρ = m·d²/(I_cm + m·d²) about the proximal joint, which sets how fast the limb's limits converge against its pin, how much energy a velocity joint takes out of it per step, and how hard it bounces off a stop. Computed both ways: 

     segment          L m    CoM: capsule  Dempster   k: capsule  Dempster   rho: capsule  Dempster
     upper arm        0.280   0.500        0.436      0.282      0.322      0.759         0.647
     forearm + hand   0.250   0.650        0.682      0.344      0.468      0.781         0.680
     thigh            0.420   0.500        0.433      0.272      0.323      0.772         0.642
     shin + foot      0.410   0.500        0.606      0.273      0.416      0.770         0.680

Every capsule's ρ comes out about 0.1 high — 0.76 to 0.78 against Dempster's 0.64 to 0.68 — and the cause is the second column more than the first: a capsule carries too little of its mass far from its own centre (k 0.27–0.34 of the length against 0.32–0.47), so too much of the inertia about the joint is the orbit. The engine keeps capsules anyway. They are what the narrow phase is fast on, the error is in the direction of more dissipation and slower-bouncing stops — a ragdoll that settles a little faster than a person would — and the honest move is to say so here rather than to hide a correction in the inertia tensor that the collision shape disagrees with.

Which pairs may collide

8.11's rule was that two bodies a joint connects must not collide: they overlap at the joint by construction, and a contact solver that sees the overlap fights the joint every step. The handover to this lesson predicted that a ragdoll would need more than that — "a forearm and a torso overlap too, two joints apart" — and the engine grew a rule for it: exclude every pair whose capsules overlap, within a centimetre, at rest. The harness then audited the humanoid. The prediction was wrong. In the bind pose, and over a whole cycle of both motions this lesson hands over, no unjointed pair comes within 4 cm of another — the closest is the two thighs, a constant 40 mm apart. So the default rule excludes exactly the ten jointed pairs and nothing else.

What the prediction was reaching for is real, but it is not about rest. It is about falls. Over twelve trips from the jog, three seconds each, the narrow phase's own manifolds record every pair of parts that touched:

The torso and a forearm touch in nine falls of twelve, more than any other pair; the two thighs in six. Both are two links apart. That matters because the obvious rule a ragdoll author writes first — "exclude anything within two links of each other", which is what the handover's sentence suggests — excludes thirteen pairs beyond the jointed ten, and those three are among them. Under it a forearm swung across the chest does not stop at the chest. The same twelve falls, each rule: 

     rule                     deepest instant   frames > 20 mm   deepest at rest
     two links                   189.9 mm          1503             179.6 mm
     overlapping (default)       106.6 mm           509              61.4 mm

The first column is not the instrument, and the section's first draft used it: a forearm swung at several metres a second arrives up to v·h deep in one step whatever the rule — 8.10 §6's arrival depth, and this engine's missing speculative contacts — so both rules reach a tenth of a metre for a frame. What separates them is whether the overlap persists. Under the two-links rule limbs spend three times as many frames more than 2 cm inside the body, and come to rest there: 179.6 mm, which is a forearm lying inside a chest whose capsule is 150 mm in radius. The default's 61.4 is something else, and §8 finds exactly the same number as the torso-on-forearm overlap of the one fall that ends face down on its own arm: a chest lying on a forearm, pressed into it from above. That is an iteration problem, not the filter's.

The control is to exclude nothing, not even the jointed pairs. After a trip and four seconds the default ragdoll holds 0.0015 J and its worst joint gap is 0.01 mm; with every jointed pair colliding it holds 0.0429 J, its worst joint has been pushed 67.51 mm open, and it is still moving — each joint's two capsules shoving apart what the joint pulls together, 8.11's pitfall at the scale of a body.

7. The first fall, and a bug 8.11 shipped

The first ragdoll this lesson dropped — tripped out of the jog, three seconds on the floor — lay still, which looked right, and then a probe printed each joint's angle against its limits. The right knee was 7.9° past its stop, bent backward, and a shoulder 6.4° past its twist range. Run for longer, the knee went on creeping: 3.9° after one second, 7.9° after three, 9.0° after ten, and the ragdoll never fell asleep. Those numbers were measured on 8.11's engine, before the fix below, and they are the one thing in this lesson the harness cannot reproduce — it links the fixed engine. What it reproduces is the mechanism.

A limit held under load is 8.11's machinery doing exactly what it was built for, so the first suspects were the ones 8.11 had already measured: the limit converging against the pin at ρ per sweep, or the contacts pushing the leg. Neither survives an isolated test. The harness puts a shin on a fixed thigh, lying straight out behind the knee so gravity drives it into its hyperextension stop, and holds it there for ten seconds under a harness-local copy of the solver's joint path, once with 8.11's position pass (kept verbatim in the harness, as 8.10 kept a private copy of the step it split) and once with the engine's:

The first row is the whole bug in one line. A violation of 0.1691° that does not change in the fourth decimal for ten seconds is not a limit converging slowly; it is a limit that is not being corrected at all. Split impulse's position pass removes a fifth of a violation every step, and here it removes none. The third row confirms it from the other side: without warm starting, the velocity pass leaves a little approach velocity every step, and with no correction to hold it the stop gives way without bound, 70° in ten seconds. Baumgarte, which corrects in the velocity pass, never had the problem — which is why the obvious diagnosis, "split impulse is too weak for a loaded stop", would have been exactly wrong.

What the position pass was doing is in the prepared rows, and the harness prints them five seconds in: 

     lower stop    C = -0.002951 rad   bias 0.03541   pseudo impulse +0.006513
     upper stop    C = +2.620945 rad   bias 0.00000   pseudo impulse +0.006513
     the knee's pseudo spin about the hinge after the pass: +3.648e-09 rad/s

The upper stop — 150° away, nowhere near violated — has accumulated exactly as much pseudo impulse as the lower one, in the opposite direction, and the knee's net pseudo spin is 3.6 × 10⁻⁹ rad/s. It cancels the correction to seven figures. And the reason is a line 8.11 wrote with a correct comment above it. Every one-sided row gets a speculative target −C/h in the velocity pass — "you may approach this stop, but not faster than a step allows" — and bias = 0, because it is not violated. 8.11's position pass solved every row against its bias. For a satisfied row that made the target zero: a hard "no pseudo-velocity toward me", from a stop the joint could not reach in a hundred steps. A hinge with both ends of its range enabled always has both rows, speculation being on, so the far stop undid every correction of the near one, and under split impulse a violated two-ended limit was never repaired. 8.11's comment on the function said "a position correction only repairs error that already exists", which is the right intent; the code skipped motors and nothing else.

The fix is one line in solve_joint_positions and a helper that takes the goal explicitly: a one-sided row that is not violated gets its own speculative target, −C/h, in the position pass too — a correction may carry the joint up to the far stop and no further. Violated rows keep their bias; a rod's bilateral row has no speculative side. The fixed pass holds the loaded knee at 0.0000°, and cold it settles at a steady lag of 1.8915° instead of giving way. On the whole ragdoll the knee now lies inside its range, and §6's default row — a trip and four seconds, measured by the harness on the fixed engine — reads a worst joint gap of 0.01 mm.

Why 8.11 never saw it, and what the fix moved

8.11 measured limits at the velocity level: a turnstile's overshoot, a door's rebound, a motor's stall. Each of those is decided in the step the limb arrives, before a violation has had time to be frozen; the position pass is barely involved. A rope has one row and a rod's row is bilateral, so neither ever had a far stop. It took a limit held against a steady load — a knee under a leg's weight, which is every ragdoll's normal state — for a violation to sit there long enough to notice that nothing was repairing it.

8. What eight sweeps do to a ragdoll

8.11 §14 left a warning for this lesson: a chain's stretch grows with its mass ratio and halves with each doubling of sweeps, and eight sub-steps of one sweep beat one step of eight sweeps by 19× on a 100:1 chain, for the same number of joint visits. A ragdoll is a tree of short chains with a 16:1 ratio in it. The harness measures four scenes against five budgets — 8, 16 and 32 sweeps, eight sub-steps of one sweep (the same visits as the shipped eight), and a control of 32 sub-steps of four sweeps each, sixteen times the shipped work: 

     budget               fall: peak   rest     hanging by a hand   chest on arm
     8 sweeps (shipped)      92.47    12.860         4.128  (KE 6.5e-05)     61.4
     16 sweeps               54.88     7.204         3.319  (KE 4.6e-05)     28.8
     32 sweeps               32.75     0.488         2.524  (KE 2.7e-05)     20.3
     8 sub-steps x 1         30.03     6.741         0.001  (KE 1.2e-08)      3.3
     CONTROL 32 x 4           3.69     0.002         0.001  (KE 1.4e-07)      0.0

A fall opens the joints for a moment. The worst joint gap anywhere in six falls peaks at 92 mm at eight sweeps — the instant a shin or a head strikes the floor and the impulse has to cross the tree — and closes again (12.9 mm at rest, in the worst of the six). More sweeps help, halving it roughly per doubling as 8.11 found. Eight sub-steps do as well as 32 sweeps, at a quarter of the visits.

A hanging character refuses 8.11's rule. Hang the whole 80 kg from one hand — 1.76 kg of forearm carrying everything, a 45:1 load — damp every part so it comes to rest, and read the gap after eight seconds. More sweeps barely help: 4.13, 3.32, 2.52 mm for 8, 16 and 32. Eight sub-steps of one sweep: 0.001 mm, four thousand times less for the same visits, and the sixteen-times-the-work control does no better. This section's first draft hung the body undamped, and the measurement was nonsense — the body swings like a pendulum for a long time after release, with tens of joules still in it five seconds later, and a gap sampled mid-swing measures the phase of the swing, not the solver. A load test needs the load at rest.

And there is a stack nobody built. §6's resting overlap turned out to be one fall that ends face down on the right forearm: 28.4 kg of chest on 1.76 kg of arm on the floor. That is 8.10's heavy-on-light tower, the case eight sweeps cannot hold, formed by accident, and the chest sinks 61.4 mm into the arm and stays there. It is an iteration problem — 28.8 at sixteen sweeps, 20.3 at thirty-two, 3.3 with eight sub-steps, nothing at the control — and it comes back in §13, because a crushed arm is a ragdoll that never stops fighting.

So the handover's first limit is confirmed and sharpened: a ragdoll's mass ratios cost it centimetres at eight sweeps, and the answer 8.11 measured for chains — small steps rather than many sweeps — is better still for a body, by orders of magnitude on a static load. The engine does not sub-step yet; the harness's scene does it by running the whole pipeline substeps times at h/substeps, and the demo's U does the same. Pricing that for a whole scene, contacts and broadphase included, is Module 9's.

9. Handing a character to the solver

At the instant of handoff each body must be where the animation put it, moving the way it was moving it. "Where" is composition: a pose gives each skeleton joint a model-space frame (anim::compose_pose, 7.6), the character's placement carries it into the world, and the part's constant joint_from_body carries it to the body. part_targets does exactly that product for every part. "Moving the way it was moving" is the interesting half.

Steer, never teleport

The obvious approach animates by writing positions: each frame, set every body to its target. The bodies then have whatever velocity they last had — zero, for a kinematic body nobody touched — and at the handoff you would need to invent one, by differencing the last two poses. This engine does the opposite, and the handoff falls out. While a character is animated its bodies are kinematic, and each step the ragdoll sets their velocities to the ones that will carry them exactly onto the next target, and lets integrate_positions move them. They are never written to directly. That is steer.

For position the velocity that lands a body exactly is the chord, (x_target − x)/h: the integrator adds v·h and nothing else. For orientation it depends on the rule the integrator uses. The calculus answer — the angle over h, about the axis of the rotation still to do — is exact only for spin_rule::exponential. The engine's default is 8.3's linearised rule, q ← normalise(q + ½·h·ω·q), whose step is proportional to (1, ½hω); so the angular velocity that makes it land exactly is whatever makes ½hω equal the remaining rotation's Δq.v/Δq.w:

\[ \omega \;=\; \frac{2}{h}\,\frac{\Delta q_v}{\Delta q_w}, \qquad \Delta q \;=\; q_{\text{target}}\;q^{*}\ \ \text{(moved to}\ \Delta q_w > 0) \] omega = (2/h) * dq.v / dq.w, with dq = q_target * conj(q) on the w > 0 side: the Rodrigues vector

— the Rodrigues vector, which has tan(θ/2) where the logarithm has θ/2. The two differ by θ²/12 of the rotation, and the harness measures what that is worth on two seconds of the jog, whose fastest limb turns 0.1054 rad in one step: steered with the Rodrigues vector every body lands within 1.57 × 10⁻⁷ rad of its target; steered with the logarithm it misses by 9.75 × 10⁻⁵, against θ³/12 = 9.76 × 10⁻⁵ predicted. Positions land to 5.8 × 10⁻¹¹ m either way. The miss is small; the principle is not. The velocity to give a body is whatever its integrator will invert — and for the linear half that is the chord, which is the next paragraph.

The chord is not an approximation

A steered body's velocity at any instant is the chord of the step it just took. At the moment of handoff, simulate() gives the body its mass back and leaves that velocity alone. Is the chord the right velocity to hand over, or a first-order estimate of it? Compare it with the clip's own velocity, by a fine difference of the targets: against the velocity at the end of the step the chord is off by up to 0.2320 m/s — half the acceleration times h, first order — and against the velocity at the step's middle by 0.0045 m/s, second order. The chord is the midpoint velocity. And that is exactly what semi-implicit Euler's velocity means: it moves a body by x(n) = x(n−1) + h·v(n), so v(n) is the chord. The solver inherits the one velocity its own integrator would have produced.

The consequence is the character's momentum. Trip it at one second, mid-stride at 2.5 m/s: 

     velocities           momentum kg m/s   KE J      centre of mass forward in 0.5 s   in 1.5 s
     the chord (steer)     204.40             301.21      1.179 m                        1.433 m
     at rest (control)       0.00               0.00      0.010 m                        0.402 m
     the animation's own momentum at the instant of the trip: 204.40 kg m/s = 80.0 kg x 2.555 m/s

The ragdoll leaves the animation with exactly its momentum — 80 kg at 2.555 m/s, the jog's 2.5 plus the swinging limbs — and pitches forward 1.18 m in the first half second. Handed over at rest, it moves 10 mm in the same half second: it stops dead in mid-stride, and only then topples, because the jog leans forward. That is the pop every first ragdoll implementation has, and it is not a small effect to be smoothed; it is 204 kg·m/s thrown away.

What the joints make of the handed-over velocities

Each body's chord is exact for that body. Is the set of them consistent with the joints — does each joint's anchor move the same on both sides? Not quite, and by an amount that has a closed form. A point at lever arm r on a body turning at ω moves along a chord, not a tangent, and the rigid-body formula v + ω × r evaluated at the end of the step differs from the chord by the centripetal term ½h·ω×(ω×r). The two bodies of a joint make different errors, so each joint starts violated by the difference:

\[ \lvert\dot C\rvert \;\approx\; \tfrac12\,h\,\bigl\lvert\,\omega_b\times(\omega_b\times r_b) \;-\; \omega_a\times(\omega_a\times r_a)\,\bigr\rvert \] |dC/dt| at handoff = (h/2) | w_b x (w_b x r_b) - w_a x (w_a x r_a) |

The harness predicts it for every joint of the jog at the trip and measures the anchor velocity mismatch: 18.4 to 72.7 mm/s for the limbs, and the worst disagreement between prediction and measurement is 0.43%. It is 8.11 §5's drift — a tangent is not a circle — seen from the other side, and the solver's first sweep removes it. Nothing needs to be done about it; it is worth knowing that the handoff's only joint error is this one, and how big it is.

10. When the animation disagrees with the joints

Everything so far used the jog, which was written for this lesson so that it stays inside every joint. Real animation does not. An animator does not see a ragdoll's limits; a mocap clip does not know them; and a clip made for one skeleton gets retargeted onto another. So what happens when the pose handed over is outside the joints?

This course has such a clip already. 7.7's walk was built to measure clip storage — a sampler does not care what a knee does — and against the ragdoll's joints it is badly wrong:

11. Handing it back

Getting up is the handoff backwards, and it has three parts. First, read the pose off the bodies. read_pose inverts part_targets: each part's joint goes where its body says, through the inverse of joint_from_body; each passenger rides on its parent at the local transform the clip last gave it (simulate kept it); and every joint's local transform is then its parent's inverse times its own, which is what compose_pose and a blend both want. Over seventy instants of the jog, placing the bodies from a pose and reading it back gives the pose again to 2.37 × 10⁻⁷ m and 3.35 × 10⁻⁷ rad.

Second, put the clip where the character fell. The character tripped at one place and slid on; its placement, world_from_model, is still where it tripped. Blend from the fallen pose to the clip in that placement and the whole body travels back to the trip point over the blend. realign_model moves the placement in the ground plane so that the clip's pelvis lands over the ragdoll's — only x and z: height is the clip's business, and realigning vertically onto a lying pelvis would put a standing character through the floor. Heading is not realigned; that is exercise 4.

Third, blend, and the engine already has the right blend. The bodies go back to kinematic with animate() — which also clears every joint's cached impulses, since they describe what it cost to hold a pose the character is no longer in — and are steered toward a pose that starts at the read-back one and blends to the clip, joint by joint in local space. The first target is where the bodies already are, so nothing jumps. 

     return                         first-frame jump   pelvis slide   worst bone change
     slerp blend, realigned            0.0581 m         0.000 m          0.06 mm
     slerp blend, NOT realigned        0.0707 m         1.302 m          0.06 mm
     snap to the clip (control)        1.6480 m         0.000 m          0.00 mm
     model-space position blend        0.0581 m         0.000 m        208.06 mm
     worst arc any joint blends across: 115.5 deg;  worst nlerp-against-slerp lag over the blend: 1.98 deg

Each row is one claim. The blended return moves no body more than 58 mm in its first frame, which is a thirtieth of the way to a pose 1.65 m away; snapping moves one by 1.648 m in a single step. Without realignment the pelvis slides 1.302 m across the floor while the character stands up — back to where it tripped. A local-space blend moves no bone's length by more than 0.06 mm, the joints' own gap; blend the model-space joint positions instead and a bone is 208 mm short at the middle of the blend. That last row is 7.7 §7 one level up, and it is why a pose blend is done on local transforms: a limb that sweeps through 115° shortens along its chord if its end is interpolated in a straight line.

And the last number decides a choice 7.5 left open. 7.7 blends keys with nlerp because two adjacent keys of a 30 Hz clip are never more than a few degrees apart, and there nlerp lags slerp by 0.017°. A return blends across whole poses — 115.5° at its worst joint — and there nlerp lags by up to 1.98°, a hundred times as much — largest a quarter of the way in from each end, since the two agree exactly at the midpoint by symmetry (this section's first draft measured there, and reported 0.00°). The return uses transform_blend_slerp.

What this does not do is get a character up convincingly. It blends a lying body toward a standing pose, and the path in between is whatever slerp makes of it: legs pass through the floor, the pelvis rises straight up. A production game blends into a get-up clip whose first frame is lying down — face down or face up, chosen from the pelvis's orientation — so that the blend is short and the clip does the standing. This engine has no such clip, and the lesson says so rather than pretending the blend is the animation.

12. A return the physics does not fight

The design has one rule — every body has exactly one owner — and this section is what breaking it costs. The tempting implementation of "animated, but still colliding" is to leave the bodies dynamic and teleport them onto the animated pose after every step, so that props still bounce off the character. It is 8.11 §1's teleport, and there it was a thought experiment. Here it is measured, on a character jogging into a 5 kg crate, three ways: 

     bodies follow the clip by        velocity lie   deepest limb in crate   crate top speed   bodies' speed when let go
     kinematic, steered                   0.000 m/s         69.7 mm              4.78 m/s           4.82 m/s
     dynamic, teleported                 20.626 m/s        286.8 mm              3.67 m/s          20.90 m/s
     dynamic, teleported + chord          0.000 m/s        159.3 mm              3.59 m/s           4.82 m/s

The velocity lie is the largest difference, over every body and step, between a body's velocity and the distance it actually moved over h. A steered body's is zero — its velocity is its motion, by construction. A teleported body's is 20.6 m/s: its velocity is whatever the solver last left in it, gravity accumulating every step and the teleport discarding the result. That lie is what every contact sees, and it is what the body carries when the ragdoll is finally let go — 20.90 m/s, where a steered body leaves at the 4.82 its limb was really moving. The third row fixes the lie by writing the chord in as well, and still loses: the solve pushes a dynamic limb out of the crate and the teleport puts it back, 159 mm deep, against 69.7 mm for the steered limb — which is one step of a fast leg's arrival, 8.10 §6's depth.

The crate that did not move

That top row is the result after the second bug this lesson found. The first time it ran, the steered character ran through the crate. The crate reached 0.09 m/s; the narrow phase reported limb–crate contacts on 48 frames; and at the end of two seconds the crate was exactly where it started. With sleeping turned off, the same run launched it at 4.78 m/s.

The crate had fallen asleep half a second after the scene began, long before the character reached it — and nothing woke it. 8.10's waking rule is elegant: an island is asleep only if every body in it is, and "a moving body that collides with a sleeping pile is joined to it by build_islands, so the pile's island now contains something awake". That is true of a dynamic body. build_islands unions an edge only when both ends are dynamic — 8.10's correct rule that a body that cannot be pushed is not a bridge — so a kinematic body never joins the island it touches, and therefore never woke one either. The crate's island stayed entirely asleep, its contacts were skipped, and the limb passed through. Every kinematic body in the engine had this bug — a lift, a moving platform — and none had met a sleeping body until a ragdoll was steered into one.

The fix is a stage in front of 8.10's: wake_touched_by_kinematic walks the step's contacts and joints and wakes any sleeping dynamic body that touches a kinematic one moving faster than the sleep thresholds. The qualifier matters: a crate resting on a stopped lift must still be allowed to sleep, and a kinematic body never sleeps (8.10), so "touches a kinematic body" alone would keep everything on every platform awake for ever. wake_islands then wakes the rest of the island by its own rule. 8.10's harness, rerun, is byte-identical to its page in every line but its timings; so is 8.11's, apart from the §10 table §7 of this lesson printed. Neither ever put a moving kinematic body against a sleeping one.

13. Settling, sleeping, and the bill

The handover's third limit was dissipation: ρ(ωh)² takes energy out of every turning joint each step, so a flailing ragdoll should settle quickly, and a settled ragdoll is one island, so 8.10's rule should put the whole body to sleep at once. The first half is true. The second refused: 

     settings                         asleep   median s   KE at 8 s (mean)   awake: crushed / turning
     split impulse (shipped)            4/12       4.42         0.01924 J           5 / 3
     Baumgarte                          1/12       7.60         0.10303 J           2 / 9
     + angular damping 1/s              7/12       4.12         0.02672 J           2 / 3
     + damping, 8 sub-steps x 1         5/12       4.33         0.00112 J           1 / 6

Every trip settles to millijoules — 19 mJ on average after eight seconds, in an 80 kg body that started with 301 J. But only four of twelve fall asleep, and the harness classifies the eight that do not. Five are crushed: the chest lies more than 5 mm into an arm, §8's accidental stack, which fights for ever — contact and joint pushing a 1.76 kg forearm in opposite directions every step. Three are turning: nothing is wrong except that some part — a forearm, the nearly spherical head — is rotating at a few tenths of a radian per second while holding a fraction of a millijoule. 8.10's thresholds are 0.05 m/s and 0.10 rad/s on every body of an island, and they were tuned on crates. A thin limb turning at 0.3 rad/s is invisible and costs nothing, and it keeps eleven bodies awake.

Baumgarte, which keeps energy (§10), sleeps one in twelve. Angular damping — which a real body has, in its muscles and ligaments — brings seven of twelve to sleep. Adding eight sub-steps to the damping leaves one ragdoll crushed instead of two, and six turning instead of three: it cures the stack, and the bodies it frees go on turning. The fix that addresses the turning directly is a sleep test on energy rather than speed — PhysX's is a mass-normalised kinetic energy — under which a slowly turning forearm and a slowly turning crate are judged by what their motion is worth. That changes 8.10's sleeping, which 8.10 measured and priced; it is exercise 3 here, with this table as the thing to beat.

The bill

Sixteen ragdolls tripped together, sleeping off so that the work is priced rather than the saving, mean of sixty steps and best of three runs: 

     falling:  198.5 us a step  (12.4 us a ragdoll: narrow phase 3.1, solve 8.1)
     lying:    265.2 us a step  (16.6 us a ragdoll)
     ragdolls per millisecond, falling: 81

About 12 µs a ragdoll a step falling and 17 lying (lying has more contacts), of which two thirds is the solve — ten joints and a few dozen contact points, eight sweeps each. Eighty falling ragdolls cost a millisecond a step, on one core, with none of Module 9's jobs. Sleeping would make the lying ones free, if they slept; that is the other reason §13's refusal matters. And the budget is spent where §8 said it should not be: on sweeps, where sub-steps would buy far more.

14. Writing it

Three files change and one is new. constraint.hpp grows the swing–twist split, a cone on the ball-socket and the position-pass fix; solver.hpp grows one stage; and phys/ragdoll.hpp is the seam between the animation system and the solver. It is the first file in phys/ that knows what a skeleton is, and the arrow points that way on purpose: anim/skeleton.hpp includes nothing but maths, a game with no physics must still be able to animate, and a ragdoll is a physics object built from a skeleton — 5.12's rule, that the arrow points at the more general.

Step 1: the split

§3's projection, four lines and a guard. The guard is the one singularity: a swing of exactly 180° leaves nothing to project, every twist is as good as any other, and the identity is the one that does not invent a turn: 

swing_twist split_swing_twist(quat q, vec3 unit_axis)
{
    swing_twist out;

    // Keep the component of q.v along the axis and delete the rest: what is
    // left is a rotation about the axis, and it is the twist because the part
    // deleted — q.v's perpendicular component — is exactly what the swing is
    // made of. The swing is then whatever turns the twist into q.
    const float along = dot(q.v, unit_axis);
    const float len2 = q.w * q.w + along * along;

    // The one singularity: a swing of 180 degrees leaves nothing to project.
    // Every twist is equally right there; the identity is the one that does
    // not invent a turn nobody asked for.
    if (len2 < 1e-12f)
    {
        out.swing = q.w < 0.0f ? -q : q;
        return out;
    }

    const float inv = 1.0f / std::sqrt(len2);
    out.twist = quat{q.w * inv, unit_axis * (along * inv)};
    if (out.twist.w < 0.0f) { out.twist = -out.twist; }
    out.swing = q * conjugate(out.twist);
    if (out.swing.w < 0.0f) { out.swing = -out.swing; }
    return out;
}

Step 2: authoring a cone that is not centred on the bone

A cone-twist joint stores the cone's axis in the parent's frame and the twist axis — the bone — in the child's, and they need not agree when the joint is made: a shoulder authored in a T-pose wants its cone between "out" and "down", or an arm hanging at rest sits on the edge of it. The one subtle line is rest. 8.11's hinge stored conj(q_a)·q_b, which makes the relative rotation the identity at the authored pose; here it is pre-multiplied by the conjugate of the swing from the cone's axis to the bone, so that the relative rotation is that swing at the authored pose — twist zero, swing the tilt — and its swing–twist split about the cone's axis is the joint's swing and twist at every pose after. hinge_angle then measures the twist unchanged: 

joint make_cone_twist(const rigid_body& a, const rigid_body& b, vec3 world_anchor,
                      vec3 world_twist_axis, vec3 world_cone_axis)
{
    joint j = make_ball_socket(a, b, world_anchor);

    const vec3 twist = normalised(world_twist_axis);
    const vec3 cone = length_squared(world_cone_axis) > 0.0f ? normalised(world_cone_axis) : twist;
    j.axis_a = local_direction_of(a, cone);
    j.axis_b = local_direction_of(b, twist);

    // `rest` must make `r = conj(q_a) q_b conj(rest)` carry `axis_a` onto the
    // bone in `a`'s frame at every pose, so that r's swing–twist split IS the
    // joint's swing and twist. `make_ball_socket` set `rest = r0 =
    // conj(q_a) q_b`, which carries the bone onto itself; pre-multiplying by
    // the conjugate of `offset` — the swing from the cone's axis to the bone —
    // makes r equal `offset` now, which is a pure swing, so the twist reads
    // zero at the authored pose and the swing reads the tilt. With no tilt
    // `offset` is the identity and `rest` is the hinge's.
    const vec3 bone_in_a = local_direction_of(a, twist);
    const quat offset = minimal_rotation(j.axis_a, bone_in_a);
    j.rest = conjugate(offset) * j.rest;
    return j;
}

In prepare_joint, at the head of the ball-socket case and guarded on the kind — the hinge falls through into this case for its pin, and a hinge's limit is its own angle. §4's swing row and §5's twist rows, through 8.11's one_sided lambda, which is what makes speculation, warm starting and the position pass apply to them without a line of solver code:

Step 4: the position pass, fixed

§7's bug, in the one line that changed. A satisfied one-sided row is given its speculative target rather than its zero bias, through a helper that takes the goal explicitly so that no row's state is borrowed and restored: 

const bool one_sided = row.lower == 0.0f;
worst = std::max(worst, one_sided ? std::max(0.0f, -row.error) : std::abs(row.error));

// *** LESSON 8.12 FIXED THIS, AND IT HAD BEEN WRONG SINCE 8.11. ***
//
// A one-sided row that is NOT violated — a limit the joint has not
// reached — used to be solved here against `row.bias`, which is zero for
// it, and that made it a hard "no pseudo-velocity toward me" row. A hinge
// with both stops enabled always has both rows, so the far stop, a
// hundred and fifty degrees away, cancelled every correction of the
// near one exactly: a violated limit was NEVER repaired under split
// impulse. 8.11 measured limits at the velocity level only — bounces,
// overshoots, stalls — and never met it. 8.12 §7 found it holding a

const float goal = (one_sided && row.error > 0.0f) ? row.target : row.bias;
(void)solve_row_position_to(row, goal, batch.inv_mass_a, batch.inv_mass_b, pa, pb);

Step 5: a moving kinematic body wakes what it touches

§12's bug, as a stage in front of 8.10's wake_islands. "Moving" is the sleep test's own definition turned round, so that a stopped lift wakes nothing: 

int wake_touched_by_kinematic(std::span<rigid_body> bodies, std::span<const contact_pair> contacts,
                              std::span<const joint_pair> joints, const sleep_config& cfg)
{
    if (!cfg.enabled) { return 0; }

    // "Moving" is the sleep test's own definition turned round: a kinematic
    // body that WOULD fail it, were it allowed to sleep, is one that can
    // disturb what it touches. A stopped lift passes it and wakes nothing.
    const auto moving_kinematic = [&cfg](const rigid_body& b) {
        return b.kind == body_kind::kinematic
               && (length_squared(b.state.velocity) > cfg.linear_threshold * cfg.linear_threshold
                   || length_squared(b.angular_velocity) > cfg.angular_threshold * cfg.angular_threshold);
    };

    int woken = 0;
    const auto edge = [&](std::uint32_t ia, std::uint32_t ib) {
        rigid_body& a = bodies[ia];
        rigid_body& b = bodies[ib];
        if (a.sleeping && moving_kinematic(b)) { wake(a); ++woken; }
        if (b.sleeping && moving_kinematic(a)) { wake(b); ++woken; }
    };
    for (const contact_pair& p : contacts) { edge(p.body_a, p.body_b); }
    for (const joint_pair& p : joints) { edge(p.body_a, p.body_b); }
    return woken;
}

Step 6: building the ragdoll

build_ragdoll is four passes. The first maps skeleton joints to parts; the second builds each part's capsule, mass, inertia and joint_from_body, places a throwaway body at the bind pose in model space, and finds the parent part by walking up the skeleton. The third authors the joints on those throwaway bodies — which is legitimate because a joint keeps only body-local anchors, axes and a relative rest orientation — and the fourth asks GJK, once per pair, which capsules overlap at rest: 

// ---- pass 3: the joints --------------------------------------------------
for (std::size_t i = 0; i < count; ++i)
{
    const ragdoll_part_desc& d = desc.parts[i];
    ragdoll_part& p = out.parts[i];

    // A part with no parent part, or with a parent that comes after it,
    // cannot be joined; it is a free body, and counted as a root.
    if (p.parent < 0 || p.parent >= static_cast<int>(i) || d.link == ragdoll_link::root)
    {
        p.kind = ragdoll_link::root;
        ++report.roots;
        continue;
    }

    const rigid_body& a = at_bind[static_cast<std::size_t>(p.parent)];
    const rigid_body& b = at_bind[i];
    const vec3 anchor = translation_of(bind[d.bone]);

    if (d.link == ragdoll_link::hinge)
    {
        p.link = make_hinge(a, b, anchor, d.axis);
        ++report.hinges;
    }
    else
    {
        const vec3 bone = rotate(b.orientation, vec3{0.0f, 1.0f, 0.0f});
        const vec3 twist = length_squared(d.axis) > 0.0f ? d.axis : bone;
        p.link = make_cone_twist(a, b, anchor, twist, d.cone_axis);
        p.link.cone.enabled = d.swing > 0.0f;
        p.link.cone.swing = d.swing;
        ++report.cone_twists;
    }
    p.link.limit.enabled = d.upper > d.lower;
    p.link.limit.lower = d.lower;
    p.link.limit.upper = d.upper;
}

Step 7: steering

§9's two inverses. The chord for position; for orientation, the Rodrigues vector under the linearised rule and the logarithm under the exponential one: 

vec3 steering_angular_velocity(quat from, quat to, float h, spin_rule rule)
{
    if (!(h > 0.0f)) { return vec3{}; }

    // The rotation still to do, in WORLD terms: `to = delta · from`, because the
    // integrator applies an angular velocity on the left (`ω q`, 8.3).
    quat delta = to * conjugate(from);
    if (delta.w < 0.0f) { delta = -delta; }   // the short way round (7.4's double cover)

    if (rule == spin_rule::exponential)
    {
        // The logarithm: an axis, and the whole angle, per step.
        const float s = length(delta.v);
        if (s < 1e-9f) { return delta.v * (2.0f / h); }
        return delta.v * (2.0f * std::atan2(s, delta.w) / (s * h));
    }

    // The inverse of `normalise(q + ½hωq)`: that step's delta is proportional
    // to `(1, ½hω)`, so `½hω = delta.v / delta.w`. A half-turn in one step has
    // `delta.w = 0` and no finite answer; it is clamped to a very fast one,
    // which a ragdoll whose animation turns a bone by 180 degrees in a frame
    // has earned.
    const float w = std::max(delta.w, 1e-3f);
    return delta.v * (2.0f / (h * w));
}

void steer(const ragdoll& rd, std::span<rigid_body> bodies, std::span<const transform> targets,
           float h, spin_rule rule)
{
    if (!(h > 0.0f)) { return; }
    const float inv_h = 1.0f / h;
    for (std::size_t i = 0; i < rd.parts.size() && i < targets.size(); ++i)
    {
        rigid_body& b = bodies[rd.first_body + i];
        if (b.kind != body_kind::kinematic) { continue; }

        // The chord. For the position half of every integrator in this engine
        // this is exact: the step adds `v·h` and nothing else.
        b.state.velocity = (targets[i].position - b.state.position) * inv_h;
        b.angular_velocity = steering_angular_velocity(b.orientation, targets[i].rotation, h, rule);
    }
}

Step 8: the two handoffs

Which are, as §2 promised, almost nothing. simulate gives the bodies their mass back and keeps their velocities; animate takes the mass away and clears the joints' cached impulses: 

void simulate(ragdoll& rd, std::span<rigid_body> bodies, std::span<const transform> pose)
{
    rd.mode = ragdoll_mode::simulated;

    // The passengers' locals, as the animation last had them. A pose shorter
    // than the skeleton keeps what was there for the missing joints.
    for (std::size_t j = 0; j < rd.frozen_local.size() && j < pose.size(); ++j)
    {
        rd.frozen_local[j] = pose[j];
    }

    for (std::size_t i = 0; i < rd.parts.size(); ++i)
    {
        rigid_body& b = bodies[rd.first_body + i];
        const ragdoll_part& p = rd.parts[i];

        // THE HANDOFF IS THESE LINES AND NOTHING ELSE. The velocities are left
        // exactly as `steer` set them — the chord that carried this body onto
        // this pose — and for semi-implicit Euler that chord IS the velocity.
        b.kind = body_kind::dynamic;
        (void)set_mass(b, p.mass);
        (void)set_inertia(b, p.inertia);
        b.force = vec3{};
        b.torque = vec3{};
        b.sleeping = false;
        b.sleep_timer = 0.0f;
    }
}

Step 9: reading the pose back

A part's joint from its body, a passenger from its parent and its frozen local, and every local transform as the parent's inverse times the joint's own. The loop runs parent before child, which the skeleton guarantees (7.6), so a parent's placement is always ready when its child reads it: 

void read_pose(const ragdoll& rd, const anim::skeleton& sk, std::span<const rigid_body> bodies,
               const mat4& world_from_model, std::vector<transform>& local_out)
{
    const std::size_t n = sk.joints.size();
    local_out.resize(n);

    const rigid model = rigid_of(world_from_model);
    const quat model_inv = conjugate(model.q);

    std::vector<rigid> posed(n);
    for (std::size_t j = 0; j < n; ++j)
    {
        const anim::joint_index parent = sk.joints[j].parent;
        const bool has_parent = parent != anim::k_no_parent && parent < j;
        const transform frozen = j < rd.frozen_local.size() ? rd.frozen_local[j]
                                                            : sk.joints[j].local_bind;
        const int part = j < rd.part_of_joint.size() ? rd.part_of_joint[j] : -1;

        if (part >= 0)
        {
            // A PART: the body says where its bone is. Into model space, then
            // back through joint_from_body.
            const ragdoll_part& p = rd.parts[static_cast<std::size_t>(part)];
            const rigid_body& b = bodies[rd.first_body + static_cast<std::size_t>(part)];
            const rigid body{rotate(model_inv, b.state.position - model.p), model_inv * b.orientation};
            const quat bone_q = body.q * conjugate(p.rotation);
            posed[j] = rigid{body.p - rotate(bone_q, p.offset), bone_q};

            const rigid local = has_parent ? relative(posed[parent], posed[j]) : posed[j];
            local_out[j].position = local.p;
            local_out[j].rotation = normalised(local.q);
            local_out[j].scale = frozen.scale;
        }
        else
        {
            // A PASSENGER: it rides on its parent at the animation's last word.
            const rigid local{frozen.position, frozen.rotation};
            posed[j] = has_parent ? compose(posed[parent], local) : local;
            local_out[j] = frozen;
        }
    }
}

Step 10: the caller's frame

The ragdoll does not run a narrow phase or own a world — the caller's, as since 8.7 — so the last step is the loop a game writes. While the clip owns the character, aim and let the step carry the bodies; at the trip, simulate; each step while the solver owns it, add the ragdoll's joints between begin and solve; to get up, realign, read the pose back, animate, and steer toward a blend. The demo's advance is exactly this: 

// animated: aim at where the clip will be at the END of this step
part_targets(rd, posed_from_clip(t + h), world_from_model, targets);
steer(rd, world.bodies(), targets, h);

// the trip: the velocities are already right
simulate(rd, world.bodies(), clip_local_pose(t));

// every step, while the solver owns it
solver.begin(world.bodies());
/* ...contacts... */
add_joints(rd, solver);
solver.solve(h, cfg, sleep);

// getting up: put the clip over the pelvis, start from where the bodies lie
world_from_model = realign_model(rd, world.bodies(), tripped_at, clip_hips_in_model_space);
read_pose(rd, skeleton, world.bodies(), world_from_model, start);
animate(rd, world.bodies());
// ...then each step: blend start -> clip with transform_blend_slerp, part_targets, steer

The narrow phase in the demo and the harness dispatches capsules for the first time — 8.10's and 8.11's scenes knew spheres and boxes — through a placed struct that holds whichever primitive a body has by value, so that as_convex, which is a view and refuses a temporary, has an lvalue to look at. That is dispatch, not geometry: collide_manifold has taken any convex pair since 8.7. It has now been written in three demos and a harness, which is the argument for Module 9's scene layer owning it.

17. Common pitfalls & debugging

An arm twists round on itself while it circles.
Symptom: a limb windmilling at its shoulder ends up turned about its own axis, past its twist range, by tens of degrees, though every limit looks enabled.
Cause: the twist rows are about the bone. The twist's rate is the half-way axis over cos(φ/2); a row about the bone misses the swing plane's turning, and a limb circling its cone twists at α̇(1 − cos φ) without the row seeing it (§5).
Fix: (a₁ + b₁)/(1 + a₁·b₁). Check any row against a finite difference of the angle it limits, binned by swing.
A shoulder limit jitters with the arm raised forward.
Symptom: the arm shakes between two poses near the horizontal, and only there.
Cause: the limits are on Euler angles, whose middle angle locks at 90° — the middle of a shoulder's range (§3: sensitivity 609.9 at 89.9°).
Fix: swing and twist, measured from a cone axis tilted into the middle of the range, so the one singularity is outside the cone.
A ragdoll twitches, or a joint sits wide open, the moment it spawns.
Symptom: a new ragdoll never settles; a joint gap of centimetres.
Cause: a jointed pair is colliding — the two capsules overlap at the joint by construction (§6's control: a 67.51 mm gap). Or the description's capsules overlap an unjointed neighbour at rest.
Fix: exclude_pairs into the collision filter, and read ragdoll_report::overlapping_colliding, which must be zero.
A hand passes through the ragdoll's own chest.
Symptom: after a fall a forearm lies inside the torso.
Cause: a "within two links" exclusion rule — the forearm and torso are two links apart and touch in most falls (§6).
Fix: exclude what overlaps at rest, not what is near in the tree.
The character stops dead when it trips, then crumples.
Symptom: a running character freezes for a frame at the handoff and falls straight down.
Cause: the bodies were handed over with zero velocity — they were animated by writing positions, so nothing ever gave them one (§9: 10 mm of travel in half a second against 1.18 m).
Fix: steer kinematic bodies by velocity, so the handoff inherits the chord — which is exactly the integrator's velocity.
Limbs snap and spin as the ragdoll takes over.
Symptom: at the handoff a knee or an elbow jerks and the limb keeps moving.
Cause: the clip was outside the joints (§10's 7.7 walk: knees 48° past the stop), and the correction is Baumgarte, which turns the repair into velocity that stays.
Fix: split impulse; and run the clip against the ragdoll's joints in the asset pipeline, which is twenty lines.
A limit held under load drifts past its stop and stays there.
Symptom: a lying ragdoll's knee is several degrees hyperextended and nothing corrects it; with warm starting off, the stop gives way entirely.
Cause: §7's bug: a position pass that solves a satisfied one-sided row against a zero target, so the far stop cancels the near one's correction.
Fix: a satisfied one-sided row takes its speculative target −C/h in the position pass too. Probe it: print a loaded joint's angle for ten seconds, and a violation that does not change in the fourth decimal is not being corrected.
An animated character, a lift or a platform passes through a crate.
Symptom: a kinematic body moves into a resting object and the object does not respond — but it does with sleeping disabled.
Cause: the object is asleep, and a kinematic body is not a bridge, so it never joins — or wakes — the sleeping island (§12).
Fix: wake_touched_by_kinematic before wake_islands.
Getting up drags the character across the floor, or shrinks its limbs.
Symptom: on the return to animation the body slides back towards where it tripped, or an arm visibly shortens halfway through the blend.
Cause: the clip was not realigned over the pelvis (1.302 m of slide); or the blend interpolated model-space positions rather than local transforms (208 mm short).
Fix: realign_model, then transform_blend_slerp joint by joint.
Ragdolls lie still and never fall asleep.
Symptom: a settled pile costs as much as a falling one.
Cause: a thin limb turning slowly fails the 0.10 rad/s threshold with almost no energy, or a chest is crushing an arm (§13).
Fix: angular damping on ragdoll parts, sub-steps for the crushing, and — the real fix — an energy-based sleep test (exercise 3).

18. Exercises

  1. Warm-up: a twist row and a loop, by hand. For a cone axis (0, −1, 0) and a bone swung 60° toward +x, write down the twist row's axis and its length. Then predict the twist a limb gains going once round a circle at a swing of 45° with no spin about itself, and check it by adding a row to §C's table.

    Hint

    The axis is (a₁ + b₁)/(1 + a₁·b₁); the loop's twist is the cap's solid angle, 2π(1 − cos φ).

    Solution

    b₁ = (sin 60°, −cos 60°, 0) = (0.8660, −0.5, 0), so a₁ + b₁ = (0.8660, −1.5, 0) and 1 + a₁·b₁ = 1.5: the axis is (0.5774, −1, 0), of length 1.1547 = 1/cos 30°, pointing along the half-way vector at 30° from the cone's axis. A circle at 45° encloses 2π(1 − 0.70711) = 1.8403 rad, 105.44°.

  2. An elliptical cone. A real shoulder swings further forward than back and further out than across. Give joint_cone two half-angles about two perpendicular axes on the parent, and derive the row for the limit (s_x/θ_x)² + (s_y/θ_y)² ≤ 1, with s the swing's rotation vector resolved on the two axes. Check it against a finite difference as §4 did.

    Hint

    The row is the gradient of the constraint with respect to the swing's rotation vector, carried to the relative angular velocity through the swing's Jacobian. Near the cone's axis the rotation vector's rate is simply the perpendicular part of Ω; exactness further out is the interesting part. Jolt's SwingTwistConstraintPart is one answer.

  3. Sleep on energy, not speed. §13's ragdolls settle to millijoules and most never sleep, because a slowly turning thin limb fails 8.10's angular threshold. Add a sleep mode that tests each body's kinetic energy per unit mass, (m v² + ω·Iω)/(2m), against one threshold, and rerun §K's table. Then rerun 8.10's harness and say what it costs there.

    Hint

    Choose the threshold so that 8.10's resting crate sleeps exactly as before: its 0.05 m/s corresponds to 0.00125 J/kg. The crushed arms will still not sleep, and they should not — something in them is really fighting.

  4. Heading. realign_model moves the clip in the ground plane and leaves its heading alone, so a character that fell sideways gets up facing the way it was running. Extract a heading from the pelvis's orientation, rotate the placement about the vertical to match, and handle the case the naive extraction gets wrong: a pelvis lying face down or face up, whose forward axis is vertical.

    Hint

    Project the pelvis-to-head direction onto the ground as well as the pelvis's forward, and use whichever is longer; a production game also chooses a face-up or face-down get-up clip from the sign of the pelvis's forward axis's vertical component.

  5. Engine challenge: an active ragdoll. Between "the clip owns it" and "the solver owns it" there is a third mode, used for characters that stumble and recover: dynamic bodies, driven toward the clip's pose by motors with limited torque. Add a three-row angular motor to the ball-socket (8.11's hinge motor, one row per axis, targeting the relative angular velocity that closes the gap to the clip's pose in one step), and make a character that can be pushed and still keeps jogging. Measure the torque it needs to hold a pose against gravity, and what happens to §8's joint gaps when every joint also has a motor row.

    Hint

    The target velocity is the Rodrigues vector of §9 — (2/h)·Δq.v/Δq.w of the relative rotation still to do — and the clamp is τ·h per row. Start with the legs only: a torso held up by motorised hips is the classic first active ragdoll.

19. Recap & next

The engine has ragdolls. A skeleton becomes a tree of capsules — eleven parts on 7.7's 23 joints, Dempster's masses, passengers riding on the parts — joined by hinges and by ball-sockets with a swing cone and a twist range. A character is steered onto its clip by velocity, handed to the solver with exactly its momentum, and handed back from the pose read off its bodies, realigned over its pelvis, with no jump and no shortened bone.

The angles were most of the work. Swing and twist are the rotation's natural split — the smallest turn that carries the bone, and the rest — and the swing is 7.4's two mirrors. The swing's row is exact. The twist's row is the half-way axis over cos(φ/2), derived because the swing's own angular velocity is perpendicular to it, and a row about the bone is wrong by the swing plane's turning — which, round a loop, is the loop's solid angle: Codman's paradox, to three decimals. And the ownership rule made the handoffs nearly nothing: the chord a steered body carries is the velocity its integrator would have produced, not an approximation of it.

The ragdoll also measured the module back. 8.11's position pass never repaired a violated two-ended limit under split impulse; 8.10's islands never let a kinematic body wake anything. Both are fixed, and both earlier harnesses still agree with their own pages. Five claims refused their sections — the overlap the handover predicted, the twist row that should have walked through its stop, the sweep count that should have halved a hanging load, the deepest instant as a measure of self-collision, and the ragdoll that should have gone to sleep — and each refusal is in the lesson in place of the claim.

Next: 8.13 — A Character Controller, the last lesson of the module, and deliberately not a rigid body. Everything since 8.9 has been about bodies that obey Newton; a player's character must obey the player — stop on a slope, step up a stair, never tunnel through a wall at speed — and the lesson is why that needs a different object, built from the queries Module 8 already has: GJK distances, EPA depths, and a capsule standing upright, which is what 8.5 said a capsule was for.
