Radiometry-Lite: What a BRDF Is

Lesson 6.1 settled what the numbers leaving the renderer mean at the output stage. This one asks what they are on the way there — and the honest answer, today, is that nobody knows, because shade() multiplies an albedo by a light and calls the product "the light leaving the surface" without a single unit anywhere in the sentence. We fix that, and the fix has a sting in it: there has been a π hiding inside the light's intensity field since Lesson 3.6, unlabelled, doing exactly the job the Lambert BRDF's π is supposed to do. Moving it into the open changes 154,240 floating-point results and not one pixel — and that empty diff is the proof, not the anticlimax.

1The Problem

Here is the line the engine has been shading with since Lesson 3.7. It is in light.hpp, it is correct, and it is about to be a problem:

Now answer three questions about it.

What are the units of the returned value? It is called "the light leaving the surface", which is not a unit. What are the units of intensity? The field's own documentation says a light's "brightness", which is not a unit either. Is this shading model energy-conserving? There is no way to even ask that question of the code above, because energy conservation is a statement about a ratio of two physical quantities and this expression contains none.

You might reasonably think this is pedantry. It is not, and the rest of Module 6 is the proof. Every remaining lesson in this module is about replacing the guess in that return statement with something better — microfacets in 6.3, Cook–Torrance in 6.4, image-based lighting in 6.15 — and every one of those is a claim about how much of the arriving light leaves in a given direction. You cannot check such a claim against an expression that does not know what arriving light is.

And there is something concrete hiding in there

By the end of §5 you will be able to show that intensity was never a free parameter. It was an irradiance divided by π — the exact constant the Lambert BRDF is supposed to carry, sitting on the wrong side of the multiplication with no name and no comment, since Lesson 3.6. This is not an unusual bug. It is the single most common thing to find when you first put units on a renderer someone else wrote, including your own from six months ago.

So this lesson buys three things. A vocabulary: four words for light, each with a unit, which is what lets the rest of the module be precise. A definition of a BRDF as a ratio, which is what makes "is this model physically plausible?" a question with an answer. And a test — a numerical integral over the hemisphere — that every BRDF from here on gets run through, starting with the one the engine already ships, which turns out to fail it in a way worth knowing about.

Almost nothing on screen changes. That is deliberate, and §8 makes the case that it is the strongest possible outcome rather than a disappointing one.

2Four Words for Light

Radiometry has a reputation for being a wall of Greek letters guarding three ideas. The reputation is deserved, and the way through it is to notice that the quantities are built from one another by division, in a chain, and that each division is answering a question the previous quantity could not.

2.1 Flux, irradiance, radiance

Start with a lamp. The most obvious thing to measure about it is how much light it puts out in total — every photon, in every direction, added up. That is radiant flux, written Φ, and its unit is the watt, because light is energy and flux is energy per second. A 40-watt bulb is a statement about flux.

Flux is useless for shading, and for a reason worth stating plainly: it is a property of the lamp, and shading is a question about a surface. A surface does not care how much light the lamp emits; it cares how much lands on it. So divide by area. How much flux arrives per square metre of surface is irradiance, written E, unit W/m². That is a property of the surface's situation rather than of the lamp, which is progress.

Irradiance is also not enough, and this is where most people's intuition stalls, so here is the failure that makes the next division necessary. Consider a white wall lit by a window. Every square centimetre of that wall has some irradiance — light from the window lands on it. Now put your eye somewhere and ask what colour you see. The wall sends light in every direction; your eye catches an extremely narrow slice of those directions. Irradiance has already thrown away which direction the light came from and which direction it leaves in, and those are exactly the two things shading is about.

So divide again, this time by solid angle — the amount of "directional space" a bundle of rays occupies. What you get is radiance, written L, unit W/(m²·sr): the light travelling in one direction, through one square metre of surface measured perpendicular to that direction, per steradian of directional spread.

Four words, then, and the fourth — radiant intensity, W/sr, flux per solid angle without the area division — is the one this course will barely use, because it describes point sources rather than surfaces. It is named here only so that when you meet it in a paper you know it is not radiance with a typo.

Worked example — the chain, with numbers

A lamp emits 12 W. All of it falls on a patch of floor 3 m² in area. The irradiance there is

E = Φ / A = 12 / 3 = 4 W/m²

Now suppose that light all arrives through a cone of 0.25 sr — a skylight, say, rather than the whole sky. The radiance along that cone is

L = E / ω = 4 / 0.25 = 16 W/(m²·sr)

Note what happened to the number: narrowing the cone made the radiance larger, with no more light involved. Radiance is a density in direction, and densities go up when you squeeze. Hold onto that; it is the same fact that lets a BRDF exceed 1 in §4.1. (This chain is asserted in verify_62 §A, with these numbers.)

2.2 The steradian, from the geometry

A steradian is to a sphere what a radian is to a circle, and if that sentence has ever felt like a definition rather than a restatement, here is the geometry it comes from.

An angle in the plane is measured by taking a unit circle around the vertex and asking how much arc the angle cuts off. The whole circle has circumference 2π, so a full turn is 2π radians. Nothing more mysterious than that. In three dimensions, take a unit sphere around the vertex and ask how much area the cone of directions cuts off. The whole sphere has area 4π, so all of directional space is 4π steradians, and a hemisphere is 2π.

Two consequences we need immediately. First, the differential element. Parameterise a direction by its polar angle θ from the normal and its azimuth φ around it. A small patch on the unit sphere spans dθ in one direction and, because a circle of constant θ has radius sin θ, only sin θ · dφ in the other. So

\[ d\omega = \sin\theta \, d\theta \, d\varphi \]

In prose, so this survives the CDN being unreachable: the solid-angle element is sine theta, times d-theta, times d-phi. The sine is there because the circles of constant θ shrink toward the pole; it is the same factor that makes lines of longitude converge at the north pole.

Second — and this is the one that matters for the π — light-transport integrals are almost never over plain solid angle. They are over solid angle weighted by cos θ, because of §3's projected-area argument. And the cosine-weighted hemisphere does not measure 2π. It measures π:

\[ \int_{\Omega} \cos\theta \, d\omega = \int_{0}^{2\pi}\!\!\int_{0}^{\pi/2} \cos\theta \sin\theta \, d\theta \, d\varphi = 2\pi \cdot \tfrac{1}{2} = \pi \]

In prose: integrating cos θ over the hemisphere gives 2π (from the φ integral) times one half (from the θ integral of sin θ cos θ), which is π. verify_62 §A computes this numerically and gets 3.1415939 on a 1024×2048 grid.

Keep that number. It is not a coincidence that it is π and it is not a coincidence that the Lambert BRDF has a π in it; §4.2 shows they are the same π, arriving by the same route, and Figure 5 draws the disc whose area it is.

2.3 Why a pixel holds radiance

Of the three quantities, exactly one can be stored in a pixel, and the reason is a small miracle of cancellation that is worth doing slowly, because it is the justification for the entire architecture of a renderer.

Put a small emitter of area A in front of a receiver, a distance d away, both facing each other. Move the receiver twice as far and ask what happens to what it measures.

The irradiance falls by four. That is the inverse-square law, and it is not mysterious either: the same flux is now spread over a sphere with four times the area. But the solid angle the emitter subtends at the receiver also falls by four — it is A/d², and d has doubled. Radiance is irradiance divided by solid angle, so the two factors of four cancel exactly and the radiance is unchanged.

That is radiance invariance along a ray, and here is what it buys. It means that "the radiance arriving at this pixel from that direction" equals "the radiance leaving that surface toward this pixel", with no distance term anywhere. A ray carries its own answer. Store irradiance in your framebuffer instead and every single shading operation would need to know how far away things are, and every compositing operation would be wrong.

The nine-tenths picture

Radiance is invariant in a vacuum, or near enough. Participating media — fog, smoke, atmosphere, skin, milk — absorb and scatter along the ray, and there radiance does change with distance. That is volumetric rendering, it is a large subject, and this course does not reach it. Everything in Module 6 assumes empty space between surfaces, which is the standard assumption of real-time rendering and is why fog in games is a hack applied afterwards rather than a consequence of the lighting model.

3The Cosine, Re-derived

Lesson 3.6 derived Lambert's cosine law from a spreading beam and left it there. This is the spiral's second turn, and it goes one step further in a direction that turns out to matter a great deal for how the code is organised: the cosine is a fact about projected area, and therefore it belongs to the light, not to the surface's material.

Take a beam with a fixed cross-section — a square metre of sunlight, say — and let it strike a surface. If the surface faces the beam square-on, that square metre of light lands on a square metre of surface. Tilt the surface by θ and the same beam now lands on a patch that is stretched in one direction, by exactly 1/cos θ. Same light, more surface, so each square metre gets less: a factor of cos θ less.

Worked example — sixty degrees, both ways round

Tilt the surface to θ = 60°. The beam's footprint stretches by

1 / cos 60° = 1 / 0.5 = 2.0000

so the patch has twice the area and each square metre of it receives half the power. Now compute the same thing from the vectors, which is what the code does. With the surface normal n = (0, cos 60°, sin 60°) = (0, 0.5000, 0.8660) and the beam arriving straight down so that the direction toward the light is l = (0, 1, 0):

dot(n, l) = 0×0 + 0.5000×1 + 0.8660×0 = 0.5000

The two routes agree, and they are not the same computation: one measured an area with a cross product, the other took a dot product of two unit vectors. verify_62 §B runs both over a range of angles and reports agreement to 1e−6 — deliberately computing the area from actual vertices, so the check owes nothing to the formula it is checking.

So far this is Lesson 3.6 with better arithmetic. Here is the part that is new, and it is a statement about where code goes rather than about geometry:

The cosine is on the light's side

Nothing in the argument above mentioned what the surface is made of. A beam striking chalk at 60° and a beam striking chrome at 60° both deliver half the power per square metre. The cosine describes how much light arrives; what the surface does with it afterwards is a completely separate question, and it is the question a BRDF answers.

Which is why albedo * n_dot_l, written as one expression the way every introduction writes it, is a small act of vandalism. It fuses two independent facts into a product, and the fusion is precisely why the π has nowhere natural to live and ends up inside the light. Lesson 6.2's whole implementation is the un-fusing.

In the engine this shows up as a new function on the light rather than a new function on the material — directional_light::irradiance_on(normal), which returns E⊥ · cos θ and nothing else. It computes what the old code computed. Its value is that it names it.

4What a BRDF Is

4.1 A ratio, per steradian

A surface is hit by light from one direction and sends some of it off in another. The bidirectional reflectance distribution function — BRDF, and the name is worse than the idea — is the number that relates those two directions. It is a ratio, and stating it as a ratio is most of understanding it:

\[ f_r(\mathbf{l}, \mathbf{v}) \;=\; \frac{dL_o(\mathbf{v})}{dE(\mathbf{l})} \]

In prose: the BRDF is the radiance leaving toward v, divided by the irradiance arriving from l. Two directions in, one number out.

Radiance divided by irradiance. W/(m²·sr) divided by W/m². The watts and the square metres cancel and the unit that survives is inverse steradians, sr⁻¹ — which looks like a bookkeeping detail and is in fact the single most useful thing to know about a BRDF, because it settles a question that confuses everybody once.

"But a BRDF can be greater than 1 — doesn't that create energy?"

No, and the unit is why. A BRDF is a density per steradian. A surface that takes everything arriving and sprays it evenly over the whole hemisphere has a small value per steradian; a surface that funnels the same light into a narrow cone has a large one. Same total energy, different concentration. A perfect mirror concentrates it into a single direction — zero solid angle — so its BRDF is a Dirac delta: unbounded, and still reflecting at most what arrived.

The quantity that is genuinely bounded by 1 is the integral of the BRDF over the hemisphere, which is what §6 computes. Confusing the function with its integral is the actual mistake behind the question.

Worked example — two surfaces that reflect exactly everything

Take a surface that reflects all arriving light uniformly into a cone of half-angle α around the mirror direction. The projected solid angle of that cone is π sin²α, so its BRDF is the reciprocal — one over the space the light is spread across.

At α = 90° the cone is the whole hemisphere, and this is Lambert:

fr = 1 / (π · sin²90°) = 1 / π = 0.3183 sr⁻¹

At α = 20°, a glossy surface:

fr = 1 / (π · sin²20°) = 1 / 0.3675 = 2.7210 sr⁻¹

Both reflect 100% of the light that hits them. The second one's BRDF is 8.5× larger, and no energy has appeared from anywhere — it is the same light, in a cone one eighth the size. Let α go to zero and the BRDF goes to infinity, which is the mirror. Once you have done this arithmetic once, "a BRDF above 1" stops being alarming for good.

4.2 Deriving the π

Now the derivation this lesson exists for. Everybody writes the Lambert BRDF as albedo/π; almost nobody says where the π came from, and a large fraction of graphics code has it in the wrong place as a result — including, as §5 shows, this engine's.

Start from the definition of a Lambertian surface: it scatters light equally in every direction, so that it looks the same brightness from every angle. In BRDF terms that is the simplest possible statement — the BRDF is a constant. Call it k:

\[ f_r(\mathbf{l}, \mathbf{v}) = k \quad \text{for every } \mathbf{l}, \mathbf{v} \]

So what is k? Nothing in "scatters equally" fixes it. What fixes it is the demand that the surface not invent light. Ask: of all the light arriving from one direction, what fraction leaves — summed over every outgoing direction? That total is the surface's reflectance, and it is what "albedo 0.8" is supposed to mean: eighty percent comes back out.

To total it up we integrate the BRDF over the outgoing hemisphere — and the integral carries a cos θ, for §3's reason: radiance leaving at a grazing angle spreads over more area than radiance leaving straight up, so it contributes less to the flux actually leaving the patch. That cosine is not optional and it is not decoration; it is the whole reason the answer is π and not 2π.

\[ R \;=\; \int_{\Omega} f_r \cos\theta \, d\omega \;=\; k \int_{\Omega} \cos\theta \, d\omega \;=\; k\pi \]

In prose: the total reflectance is the integral of the BRDF times cos θ over the hemisphere. For a constant BRDF k the constant comes out of the integral, and what is left is §2.2's cosine-weighted hemisphere, which measures π. So the reflectance is k times π.

Set that equal to the albedo — no more light out than the albedo promises, and no less — and k is forced:

\[ k\pi = \text{albedo} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad f_r = \frac{\text{albedo}}{\pi} \]

That is the entire derivation, and the π is the area of a disc. §2.2 got there by integrating; Figure 5 gets there by looking, and the picture is worth having because it is the one you will still remember in a year. Every patch of the hemisphere, projected straight down onto the surface, casts a shadow of its own size times cos θ. Those shadows tile the unit disc underneath, exactly once, with nothing left over and nothing double-covered. The cosine-weighted hemisphere is the disc. Its area is π·1² = π.

Worked example — a real albedo through the real function

The demo's amber tint decodes to a linear albedo of about (0.80, 0.55, 0.18). Its Lambert BRDF is that divided by π:

fr = (0.80, 0.55, 0.18) / 3.14159 = (0.2546, 0.1751, 0.0573) sr⁻¹

Sanity-check the red channel by integrating back: 0.2546 × π = 0.7999, which is the albedo we started from, so no light has gone missing. verify_62 §C does exactly this numerically for albedos 0.18, 0.50 and 0.80 — and for a white surface at increasing grid resolutions, where the answer converges as

64×128 → 1.0001004 · 256×512 → 1.0000062 · 1024×2048 → 1.0000004

The residue shrinks with the grid, which is how you know you are looking at quadrature error rather than at a wrong constant. A wrong constant does not converge to 1; it converges to something else.

4.3 One light, and the integral collapses

The general statement of what leaves a surface is an integral over every direction light could arrive from — the reflection equation:

\[ L_o(\mathbf{v}) \;=\; \int_{\Omega} f_r(\mathbf{l}, \mathbf{v})\, L_i(\mathbf{l})\, \cos\theta \, d\omega \]

In prose: the outgoing radiance toward v is the integral, over the hemisphere, of the BRDF times the incoming radiance times the cosine.

An integral is not a thing a fragment shader wants to evaluate. Fortunately a directional light is not spread over the hemisphere at all — it arrives from exactly one direction, which is what "the sun, near enough" means. Its incoming radiance is a spike: zero everywhere except one direction, where it is infinite in just the way that makes the integral finite. The integral then collapses to a single evaluation:

\[ L_o(\mathbf{v}) \;=\; f_r(\mathbf{l}, \mathbf{v}) \; E_{\perp} \cos\theta \]

In prose: outgoing radiance equals the BRDF, times the light's irradiance measured perpendicular to the beam, times the cosine of the angle between the surface normal and the direction to the light.

That is the shading equation, and it is three factors with three separate meanings. The BRDF is what the surface does. E⊥ is how strong the light is. The cosine is how squarely this bit of surface faces it. Each can be got wrong independently, which means each can be checked independently — and that is the practical payoff of the whole section. The old code had one expression and one place to be wrong in an unfalsifiable way.

4.4 The ambient term, where the π cancels

The engine's lighting struct has carried an ambient constant since Lesson 3.6, and that lesson called it "a fudge, and calling it anything else would be dishonest". Now that we have the machinery, it is worth asking what it would be if it were not a fudge — and the answer is a genuine surprise.

Give it the most charitable physical reading available: a uniform radiance La arriving from every direction of the hemisphere at once. That is a real thing — it is what an overcast sky approximately is. Put it through the reflection equation with the Lambert BRDF:

\[ L_o \;=\; \int_{\Omega} \frac{\text{albedo}}{\pi}\, L_a \cos\theta \, d\omega \;=\; \frac{\text{albedo}}{\pi} L_a \int_{\Omega} \cos\theta \, d\omega \;=\; \frac{\text{albedo}}{\pi} L_a \pi \;=\; \text{albedo} \cdot L_a \]

In prose: constant radiance from the whole hemisphere, through a constant BRDF, gives the albedo times that radiance — because the π from the hemisphere cancels the π in the BRDF exactly.

The π cancels. Which means albedo * ambient — the expression shade() has used since Lesson 3.6, chosen because it looked right — is not an approximation of the correct answer. It is the correct answer for a uniform environment, and the one term in the whole shading equation that needs no π is the one term nobody thought to put a π in.

What is still wrong with it, now that we can say

The arithmetic is exactly right; the assumption is what fails. Real bounced light is not uniform — it comes mostly from the sky above and the floor below, and a surface facing up is lit quite differently from one facing sideways. Lesson 6.15 replaces the constant La with an environment map and a precomputed irradiance map, which is the same integral with the "uniform" dropped. And the specular half of the ambient has no term here at all, which is the other half of that lesson.

Notice that we can now state the defect precisely, in one sentence, instead of calling it a fudge. That is what putting units on things buys you.

5Where the π Was Hiding

5.1 The algebra, and what intensity meant

We now have a shading equation with units. The engine has one without. Put them side by side — the diffuse channel only, to keep it readable:

\[ \underbrace{\text{albedo} \cdot (\text{key} \cdot \text{intensity} \cdot n{\cdot}l)}_{\text{what the engine computes}} \qquad\text{vs}\qquad \underbrace{\frac{\text{albedo}}{\pi} \cdot E_{\perp} \cdot n{\cdot}l}_{\text{what it should compute}} \]

In prose: the engine multiplies the albedo by the light's colour, its intensity and the cosine. The correct form multiplies the albedo over π by the light's irradiance and the cosine.

Set them equal and solve. The albedo and the cosine appear on both sides and cancel, leaving

\[ \text{intensity} = \frac{E_{\perp}}{\pi} \]

So intensity was never a free parameter. It was an irradiance with a π already divided out of it — the Lambert BRDF's own constant, living inside the light instead of inside the material, unlabelled, since Lesson 3.6. Every scene in this course has been set up with intensity = 1.0f, which we can now read: an irradiance of π.

This is worth dwelling on because of how ordinary it is. Nothing was wrong: the pictures were fine, the two renderers agreed to a float ULP, twelve lessons of golden images matched. A constant in the wrong place produces correct output right up until the moment you want to add a second thing that also needs it — and that moment is Lesson 6.4, where the specular BRDF gets its own normalisation and the two would silently disagree about what the light's units are.

How to find this in code you did not write

The question that finds it is: what value of the light makes a perfect white surface render at exactly full scale? If the answer is 1, the renderer's diffuse BRDF has no π in it, and the π is hiding in the light. If the answer is π, the BRDF has it. If the answer is something else, there is a third constant somewhere and it is worth finding out what.

The question works because it is a measurement that does not depend on reading the code correctly. Two minutes with a white quad and a slider settles it.

5.2 π as an exposure, derived

Once the π moves into the BRDF, the light needs a real number. Ask the question from the callout above, of our own renderer this time. A perfect white Lambertian surface (albedo 1) facing the light square-on (cos θ = 1) emits

\[ L_o = \frac{1}{\pi} \cdot E_{\perp} \cdot 1 \]

For that to come out at exactly 1.0 — full scale, white on the display — the irradiance must be π. Not approximately: exactly.

Worked example — and it is exact in floating point too

This is the kind of claim worth checking rather than assuming, because "the constants cancel" is true in algebra and often false in IEEE single precision. They do cancel here:

std::numbers::pi_v<float> = 3.14159274 (0x40490FDB)
std::numbers::inv_pi_v<float> = 0.318309873 (0x3EA2F983)
product = 1.00000000 — exactly 1.0f, 0 ULP

Both roundings happen to land favourably, so the re-parameterisation costs nothing at the constant itself. It costs one to two ULP elsewhere, from re-associating the multiplies, and §8 measures that separately rather than pretending it is zero. verify_62 §C asserts both facts, plus that 1.0f/π has the same bits as inv_pi_v<float>, so the two spellings cannot drift.

So k_reference_irradiance = π goes into light.hpp with a name and a derivation. It is worth being clear about what kind of decision this is:

This is an exposure, and it is a choice

Nothing in physics says a fully-lit white surface should read 1.0. That is a statement about our display and our lack of a tonemapper, and it is exactly the convention a photographer's white card encodes. A real engine authors lights in lux — a sunny day is around 100,000 — and converts to display values through an explicit exposure and a tonemapping curve. Lesson 6.12 builds that, at which point this constant stops being the rule and becomes a default.

Naming it now is the point. The engine has had an exposure convention since Lesson 3.6; it simply had no name, no derivation, and no place to be written down. An unnamed convention is one that gets contradicted by the next person to touch the code.

5.3 Why the field had to be renamed

directional_light::intensity becomes directional_light::irradiance, and the rename is doing real work rather than tidying.

Consider the alternative. Keep the name, move the π into the BRDF, and every existing call site still compiles — and every one of them is now wrong by a factor of 3.14, silently, producing a scene that is uniformly too dark in a way that looks like an art direction decision. There is no compiler diagnostic for "this number still has the same type but no longer means the same thing".

A rename is the only tool that reaches every caller. It turns each one into a compile error that a human has to look at and answer, which is exactly what you want when the correct new value is not the old one. This is the same bargain Lesson 3.7 made when it gave shade() a to_eye parameter with no default, and Lesson 3.1 made when it inserted z ahead of colour in vertex.

Five call sites in the demos and one in verify_48 broke, were looked at, and were fixed. One of them was not a mechanical fix at all — see §8.

The default gets the same treatment from the other direction: it becomes k_reference_irradiance rather than 1.0, so that code which never mentions the field keeps working. A default of 1.0 would compile everywhere, mention nothing, and darken every defaulted scene by π — which is the precise failure the rename exists to prevent, walked back in through the constructor.

6Energy Conservation as a Number

6.1 Directional-hemispherical reflectance

"Energy conserving" gets used as a compliment in graphics writing, usually without a definition. Here is the definition, and it is computable in about twenty lines.

For a BRDF and one outgoing direction v, the directional-hemispherical reflectance is what fraction of the arriving light comes back out — the same integral as §4.2, but for a BRDF that is not constant:

\[ R(\mathbf{v}) \;=\; \int_{\Omega} f_r(\mathbf{l}, \mathbf{v}) \cos\theta \, d\omega \]

A model conserves energy when R(v) ≤ 1 for every v. Above 1 and the surface is a light source. That is the whole test, it needs no theory, and it is a loop over a θ/φ grid — which is what verify_62 does, at 512×1024 samples.

Run it on Lambert and you get the albedo back, to five decimal places, which is the sanity check that the harness works. Then run it on the highlight this engine has shipped since Lesson 3.7.

6.2 What the highlight actually costs

The Blinn-Phong specular term is coss of the angle between the normal and the halfway vector, and it has never had a normalising constant in this engine — Lesson 3.7 introduced it as a shape that looked right, and said so. Now we can price it.

The first finding is the one everybody expects. With no constant at all, the raw lobe's reflectance exceeds 1 for every shininess below about 12, reaching 2.6650 at shininess 1 — a matte-ish plastic that emits nearly three times the light falling on it. That is a genuine defect and it is why the term needs some constant.

The second finding is the one I got wrong before measuring, and it is the more interesting half. Putting the lobe on the same 1/π scale as the diffuse term — which is what this lesson does, so that one irradiance can multiply both — brings it comfortably under 1 across the whole useful range: 0.1386 at shininess 32, the engine's default. So the runaway is fixed. And the model still fails, for a different reason:

Diffuse and specular are simply added, with no coupling

A white surface with a white highlight reflects R_diffuse + R_specular, and since the first is already 1.0, the total is over 1 at every shininess: 1.1386 at 32, 1.7333 at 2. The light that bounced off the surface as a highlight is being counted again as light that penetrated and scattered back out. Physically it can only do one or the other.

This is exactly what Cook–Torrance fixes in Lesson 6.4, and it fixes it with a mechanism rather than a constant: the Fresnel term says what fraction of the arriving light reflects off the interface, and only the remainder — 1 − F — is available to scatter diffusely. The two terms stop being independent. Having the number 1.1386 in hand first is what turns that from a formula into an answer.

There is a third defect visible in the same measurement, and it is the one that most offends an artist. The lobe's reflectance depends on where the eye is: at shininess 32 the same surface reflects 0.1386 toward an eye 37° off the normal and 0.0259 toward one at 75° — a factor of 5.36, which nobody asked for and no parameter controls. A physical surface's reflectance does vary with view angle, but it varies in a specific way (it goes up at grazing angles — that is Fresnel again), and this one goes the wrong way.

Why the engine has looked fine anyway

The demo's shiny surfaces use specular 0.30 at shininess 32, which spends 0.0416 of the energy budget on the highlight and leaves room for an albedo up to 0.9584. The scenes have been inside the limit the whole time — by luck, not by construction, and the distinction only becomes visible when someone authors a bright white glossy material and the picture starts to glow. verify_62 §D asserts the demo's number, so that if a future lesson raises it, something says so.

7Implementation

Six changes, and every one of them is a renaming of something the code already did. That is unusual for this course and it is the right shape for this lesson: we are not adding a capability, we are giving the existing one a vocabulary it can be checked in.

7.1 The two constants

light.hpp needs 1/π. C++20 has it, and this is the first place in the course that has needed it, so here is the C++ note.

C++ in place — <numbers> and variable templates

M_PI was never standard C++. It is a POSIX extension, MSVC hides it behind _USE_MATH_DEFINES, and that is why every cross-platform codebase eventually grows its own copy of the digits — including this one, which has 3.14159265358979f written out in eleven places. C++20's <numbers> ends that.

The _v<float> suffix marks a variable template: a family of constants, one per type. pi_v<float> is correctly rounded to float, rather than being a double that gets narrowed at each point of use. That distinction is not academic in a shading function — a narrowing conversion in a per-pixel loop is a real instruction as well as a rounding question.

The constants live in light.hpp rather than a maths header because the BRDFs below are the only things in the engine that need them. Retiring the other eleven literals is Exercise 13.1.

7.2 The light gains a unit

intensity becomes irradiance, defaulting to the reference value, and the light gains a function that computes the light's own half of the shading equation.

irradiance_on computes nothing new. Its value is that the cosine now has a home on the light's side of the equation, which is §3's argument turned into a symbol — and it gives the next light type (a point light, in an exercise) an obvious place to put its inverse-square falloff, where a fused albedo * n_dot_l would have had none.

7.3 Two BRDFs, with units

Note what the second one is not called. Dividing the Blinn-Phong lobe by π is not a normalisation, and the header says so at length: it puts the lobe in the same units as the diffuse term so that one irradiance can multiply both, and that is all it does. §6 measured exactly how un-normalised the result remains. Calling it a normalisation would be the kind of comment that is worse than no comment, because it would stop the next reader from asking.

specular_term() is untouched. It still returns the bare coss, and the local variable in shade() that receives it is renamed from spec to lobe — because a lobe is a shape and a BRDF is a shape with units, and the two now have different names in a function that contains both.

7.4 The return statement

Read the three groups against §4.3's equation and they line up one for one. That is the deliverable of this lesson: not a faster or prettier renderer, but a return statement whose shape is an argument.

7.5 The shader, changed identically

scene.frag.hlsl gets the same three groups. HLSL has no <numbers>, so the constant is written out — and written to more digits than a float can hold, deliberately, so the compiler rounds once and lands on the same value the CPU has.

A constant that exists in two languages is a constant that can disagree in its last bit, and the symptom would be a one-code difference in a pixel diff six lessons from now. So verify_62 §F parses the shader source, converts the literal to a float, and compares the bit patterns against engine::k_inv_pi. Both are 0x3EA2F983. It is three lines of harness and it removes a whole category of afternoon.

7.6 The uniform block, which did not change

scene_light_uniforms::key is the lamp's colour times its scalar, computed on the CPU. Because the field was always the product rather than the two factors, giving one factor a physical unit could not reach it: the struct is unchanged, every offset is unchanged, and only its documentation moved.

That is worth a sentence, because it is the test of whether a uniform block was designed or merely filled in. Had it carried colour and intensity separately, this rename would have crossed the CPU/GPU boundary and the shader would have needed to know what a lamp's authoring model is. A boundary that carries results rather than inputs is one the far side cannot be wrong about.

8What Changed on Screen

Nothing. And this section is about why that is the result rather than the anticlimax.

The plan for this lesson, written down at the end of 6.1, predicted the opposite: "the golden is genuinely at risk this time… if the π lands in the BRDF, every shaded pixel moves". That prediction was right about the mechanism and wrong about the outcome, because it assumed the light would keep its old value. Once §5.2 derives that the light's value must be π, the two parameterisations are algebraically the same product — and the interesting question becomes whether they are the same product in floating point, which is a different question with a measurable answer.

verify_62 §E asks it over 342,225 channel samples, sweeping albedo, cosine, specular lobe and highlight colour against the reference render's own light:

Both halves matter. The first says the change was real: re-associating three multiplies moved 45% of the sampled results in their last one or two bits, and claiming otherwise would have been the easy lie. The second says the output stage absorbed all of it — through the exact encoder and the fast one, which the reference render actually uses.

And then the golden, which is the whole reference render rather than a sweep: seven frames, 1,209,616 bytes, hash 905BF27E, byte-identical for the thirteenth lesson.

Why an empty diff is evidence here

The claim of §5.1 is that the π was hiding inside intensity. That claim makes a prediction: take the π out of the light, put it into the BRDF, and set the light to π, and the picture must come back exactly. If the π had been somewhere else — folded into an albedo convention, absorbed by the ambient, or simply absent — this experiment would have produced a different image.

1,209,616 bytes of agreement is the negative control passing. It is only evidence because the float measurement above shows the arithmetic genuinely changed; a change that did nothing at all would prove nothing at all.

8.1 The harness bug the rename found

One call site did not take a mechanical fix. verify_48 §F — Lesson 4.8's pixel-by-pixel comparison of engine::shade() against scene.frag.hlsl — filled the GPU's light uniform like this:

The colour alone. The scalar is missing. That was silently correct for as long as the scalar was 1.0 — which it was, in every scene, because nobody had a reason to change it. The moment it became π the two sides disagreed by a factor of 3.14 and §F said so: worst |CPU − GPU| went from 1.2e−07 to 5.3e−01.

This is Lesson 6.1's finding again, in a new costume: a test that constructs its own environment tests the environment it constructed. There 4.8's harness built an _SRGB target while the program rendered to a UNORM swapchain; here it built a light the shipped renderer does not build — gpu_scene.cpp has always multiplied by the scalar. The instrument was wrong, in a way only detectable by changing the thing the instrument assumed was constant.

With the multiply restored, §F reports 1.788e−07 — one to two ULP, exactly what it reported before this lesson. The CPU and the GPU each moved their π independently and still agree.

9What This Lesson Does Not Do

Four things, named here rather than discovered as gaps later.

It does not normalise the specular. §6 measured the failure precisely and stopped there, because the correct fix is not a constant — it is Fresnel deciding the split between reflected and scattered light, which needs the microfacet machinery of Lessons 6.3 and 6.4. Bolting a normalising factor onto Blinn-Phong now would produce a model that passes the energy test and is still not a description of any surface, which is a worse place to be than honestly un-normalised with a number attached.

It does not make the BRDF reciprocal. A physical BRDF satisfies fr(l,v) = fr(v,l) — swap the light and the eye and the answer is unchanged, which follows from time-reversal symmetry. Blinn's term happens to be reciprocal (the halfway vector does not care which of the two it came from); Phong's is not. Reciprocity matters for bidirectional path tracing and for nothing this course builds, so it is named and set aside.

It does not give the light a real unit. irradiance is in "engine units" where a white surface reads 1.0. Lux would need an exposure and a tonemapper to get back to a display value; that is Lesson 6.12, and §5.2 says what changes when it arrives.

It does not touch transmission. The T in BRDF is for reflectance: light that goes into the surface and comes back out of the same side. Glass, water, leaves and skin also send light out the far side (a BTDF) or scatter under the surface before re-emerging (subsurface scattering). Both are out of scope for this course, and both are why a BRDF-only renderer cannot make a convincing wax candle.

12Common Pitfalls & Debugging

Everything is π times too dark — or π times too bright

Symptom: the scene looks flat and murky, or blown out, by an amount that is suspiciously uniform.

Cause: the π is in the BRDF and not in the light, or in both, or in neither. Factor 3.14 is the single most common magnitude of error in a renderer that has just been given units.

Fix: the white-surface test from §5.1. Render a quad with albedo 1 facing the light and read the pixel. It should be 255. If it is 81, the π is missing from the light (255 × 1/π ≈ 81). If it saturates immediately at a light value that used to be reasonable, the π is applied twice.

The hemisphere integral converges to something that is not 1

Symptom: your own energy-conservation harness reports 0.5, or 2, or 6.28 for a white Lambert BRDF, and refining the grid does not help.

Cause: a missing factor in the solid-angle element, and the value tells you which. 2.0 means the cos θ weight is missing — you integrated 1/π over 2π steradians. 0.5 means the sin θ in dω is missing. 6.28 means the π is missing from the BRDF.

Fix: integrate the constant 1 first and check you get 2π; then integrate cos θ and check you get π. Two lines, and they isolate the element from the integrand. Not converging is the useful signal: quadrature error shrinks with the grid, a wrong constant does not.

A rename that compiles everywhere

Symptom: you renamed the field, the build is green first try, and the picture changed.

Cause: something is reading the light through a defaulted struct, or through a copy made before the rename, so the compiler never had to tell anyone. This is the exact failure the rename exists to prevent, walked back in through a default.

Fix: grep for the type as well as the field — grep -rn "directional_light\|lighting " engine demos — and check what each construction site relies on. In this engine the default is k_reference_irradiance precisely so a defaulted light is still correctly exposed; if you choose 1.0 instead, every defaulted scene silently darkens.

The CPU and the GPU disagree by exactly π

Symptom: a comparison harness that agreed to a float ULP now reports a difference around 0.53, or a ratio of 3.14.

Cause: one side multiplies the light's scalar into the uniform and the other does not — §8.1's bug. It is invisible while the scalar is 1.

Fix: the ratio names it. Divide the two values; if you get π, you are looking for a missing multiply by the light's scalar, not a shading bug. And when you find it, check whether the shipped path has it too; here the renderer was always correct and only the instrument was wrong.

Two spellings of one constant

Symptom: a one-code difference between two renderers, in a diff taken months later, that resists every explanation.

Cause: HLSL has no <numbers>, so the shader carries its own digits, and somebody typed 0.3183f.

Fix: write more digits than a float can hold and let the compiler round once — and then assert it, as verify_62 §F does by parsing the shader source and comparing bit patterns. Three lines of harness against a category of bug that is otherwise found by accident.

13Exercises

  1. 13.1 — Retire the other eleven. grep -rn "3\.14159" engine demos finds eleven hand-written copies of π, in camera code, demo field-of-view constants and an angle-in-degrees helper. Replace them with std::numbers::pi_v<float>, and decide where the constant should live now that a second subsystem needs it.

    Hint

    light.hpp is the wrong home the moment camera.hpp wants it — a camera header should not include the lighting model to get a number. The choice is between a new engine/math/constants.hpp and using <numbers> directly at each site.

    Solution sketch

    Either is defensible; what is not defensible is leaving k_inv_pi in light.hpp and adding a maths header, so that the engine has two homes for the same idea. If you add the header, move k_inv_pi into it and leave k_reference_irradiance behind — that one really is a lighting constant, and the split is the useful part of the exercise. Watch the degree-to-radian sites: several are 50.0f * pi / 180.0f, which is worth a named radians() function rather than a shared constant.

  2. 13.2 — Break the π, and predict the picture first. Change k_reference_irradiance to 1.0 and, before rebuilding, write down what you expect: how much darker, and whether it is uniform.

    Hint

    The diffuse and specular terms both scale by 1/π. The ambient term does not — look at §4.4 and at which side of the equation it sits on.

    Solution sketch

    Not uniform. Lit surfaces fall to 1/π ≈ 32% of their light, but the ambient floor is untouched, so the contrast collapses as well as the brightness — shadows stay put while everything else comes down to meet them. Then remember that this is measured in light, and the display encodes it: 32% of the light is code 152, not code 81, so on screen it looks like a mild 40% darkening rather than the two-thirds cut it actually is. Lesson 6.1 is why those two sentences differ.

  3. 13.3 — Integrate a BRDF you have not been given. Write the hemisphere integrator yourself, from dω = sin θ dθ dφ, and use it to find the shininess at which the raw Blinn lobe's reflectance crosses 1. Then find it for Phong's lobe and explain the difference.

    Hint

    The crossover for Blinn is between 8 and 16 — verify_62 §D brackets it and asserts the bracket. Bisect. For Phong, note that verify_62's numbers put it well below Blinn's at the same exponent.

    Solution sketch

    Blinn crosses around shininess 11–12 (0.9500 at 12). Phong's lobe is narrower for the same exponent — the halfway vector moves at half the rate the mirror direction does, so cos^s about h covers roughly four times the solid angle of cos^s about R. That is the same factor-of-four Lesson 3.7 gave you when it said Blinn's exponent must be about 4× Phong's for the same visual tightness, arriving here as an energy statement instead of a visual one. Two lessons, two routes, one geometric fact.

  4. 13.4 — Give the light an inverse square. Add a point_light with a position rather than a direction, and the 1/d² falloff §2.3 derived. Put the falloff in irradiance_on's counterpart, not in the shading equation.

    Hint

    The signature has to change: a point light's irradiance depends on where the surface is, not only on which way it faces. And the naive 1/(d*d) goes to infinity at zero distance, which is a real problem in a scene where a lamp can be inside geometry.

    Solution sketch

    irradiance_on(vec3 surface_pos, vec3 normal_unit), returning irradiance * max(0, dot(n, l)) / max(d*d, r_min*r_min). The clamp is the standard trick and it is worth knowing it is a lie with a physical reading: a real lamp has a size, so beyond a certain closeness you are inside the emitter and the point approximation has failed. Choosing r_min as the lamp's radius makes the lie a model. Note also that "irradiance" for a point light is now a derived quantity — the authored number becomes radiant intensity, W/sr, which is the fourth word from §2.1 finally earning its place.

  5. 13.5 — The white furnace, for real. Build the classic test: a white Lambertian sphere lit by uniform radiance 1 from every direction, rendered with no directional light at all. A correct renderer produces a sphere that is indistinguishable from the background — a flat white disc with no shading whatsoever. Make our renderer do it, and explain what you had to change.

    Hint

    Set the albedo to 1 and the ambient to 1, and turn the key light off. §4.4 says what albedo * ambient evaluates to.

    Solution sketch

    You need change nothing, which is the point of the exercise: shade() returns exactly albedo * ambient = 1 for every normal, and the sphere vanishes. That is a genuine white-furnace pass for the diffuse term, and it works because of §4.4's cancellation. Now add a white specular and watch it fail — the sphere reappears, because the specular term reads the key light which is off, so it contributes nothing, and the ambient has no specular counterpart at all. That missing term is the specular half of image-based lighting, and it is Lesson 6.15's subject. A test that passes and then fails when you extend it is worth more than one that only passes.

14Recap & Next

The engine's shading equation now has units, and it has them in three separable places:

\[ L_o \;=\; \big(f_{\text{diffuse}} + f_{\text{specular}}\big)\, E_{\perp} \cos\theta \;+\; \text{albedo} \cdot L_{\text{ambient}} \]

In prose: outgoing radiance is the sum of the two BRDF lobes, times the light's perpendicular irradiance, times the cosine — plus the albedo times the ambient radiance, whose own π cancelled.

What the engine gained, concretely: a light whose scalar is a physical quantity with a name; lambert_brdf and specular_brdf, each in inverse steradians; an exposure convention that is written down and derived rather than implied; and — most useful for what comes next — a numerical hemisphere integrator that turns "is this model physically plausible?" into a number. That integrator is the shape every BRDF in the rest of this course gets checked with.

What it learned about itself: the π that Lambert's BRDF is supposed to carry has been sitting inside the light's intensity field since Lesson 3.6, and the proof is that moving it out and setting the light to π reproduces the reference render byte for byte, across 45% of sampled floats changing in their last bits. And the highlight it has drawn since 3.7 is not energy conserving — not because the lobe runs away (the 1/π stops that), but because diffuse and specular are added with no coupling, so a white glossy surface reflects 1.1386 of what hits it.

Next, Lesson 6.3: microfacet theory. That number — 1.1386 — is the door into it. The fix is not a better constant but a better story about what a surface is: not a smooth plane with an arbitrary shininess exponent, but a landscape of microscopic mirrors whose slopes have a statistical distribution. "Roughness" stops being a slider and becomes a statement about that distribution; the highlight's shape stops being pow(x, s) and becomes a consequence; and the coupling between the two lobes stops being missing, because light that bounced off a microfacet demonstrably did not go into the surface. Everything in this lesson — the ratio, the units, the integral that tests it — is the apparatus 6.3 needs to make that argument in numbers rather than adjectives.
