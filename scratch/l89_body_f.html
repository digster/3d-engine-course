15. Build & run

Nothing new is needed in the toolchain. The solver is two files in the existing library target and the demo is one more executable.

And the harness, which is where every number in this lesson came from. It refuses to run against a library with no optimisation flag, for 7.6's reason — an unoptimised libengine.a measured skinning 18.1× too slow, and §13 here would be measuring a debug build:

Expected result

The demo opens on the bounce scene: a ball falls, lands, and bounces to a sequence of peaks that sit on the faint horizontal ghost lines — those lines are e²ⁿ·h₀, drawn from the first measured peak, so the ball landing on them is the closed form being confirmed in front of you. It comes to rest.

Now press [B]. The ghost lines stay where they are and the ball starts landing above them, and instead of coming to rest it settles into a small permanent hop. Hold [E] to raise e toward 0.95 and the permanent hop grows to something you cannot miss. The panel prints the predicted hop height beside it.

Press [2] for the slope. The slab sits on a 20° ramp; the panel shows the slope angle and atan(μ) side by side. Hold [Down] to lower μ below 0.36 and the slab lets go — near enough the same angle the panel predicts. Then press [O] to swap the solve order, and it slides as though the ramp were ice, because the cone it is clipping against has radius zero.

Press [3] for the roll. The ball's spoke lets you see the spin start from nothing and build; the panel's slip line falls to zero and v/v0 settles at 0.7139 against the 0.7143 printed under it. Change μ and watch the time change while the end speed does not.

Press [4] for the creep, and this is the one to sit with. The crate lands and turns red — red means penetrating by more than a millimetre — and then it keeps going down, slowly, for as long as you watch. Hold [Right] to raise the pass count and it stops at the depth it arrived with.

In the bottom-right corner throughout is the friction clip itself, in units of μ·j_n: the admissible set, with this frame's tangential impulse plotted in it. When the impulse reaches the boundary it turns red and the contact is sliding. Press [F] and the square appears around the disc — the extra area in its corners is §7's 43%.

The harness prints eleven sections and ends with 82 checks, 0 failures.

16. Common pitfalls & debugging

Everything sticks to everything, or contacts pull instead of push.
Symptom: objects glue themselves to surfaces, or fly into the floor on contact.
Cause: a sign. Either the impulse is applied the same way to both bodies instead of oppositely, or u was computed as a-minus-b where the normal points a-toward-b. Both give a solver that removes the wrong sign of velocity.
Fix: assert the convention once, in a test: put a body above a floor moving down, and check dot(contact_velocity(floor, body, p), normal) < 0. Then check that momentum is conserved — if it is not, the two bodies are getting the same sign.
A stack of boxes slowly launches itself into the air.
Symptom: things creep upward over seconds, gaining energy from nowhere.
Cause: clamping the impulse increment to be non-negative instead of the accumulated total. Every iteration then adds a push and none can take one back, so the accumulator ratchets.
Fix: the three lines in §11 — clamp normal_impulse + delta, then recover delta from the difference. Invisible at one pass, fatal at sixteen.
A ball bounces forever at a constant small height.
Symptom: the bounce decays nicely and then stops decaying, hopping a centimetre or two indefinitely.
Cause: §6. Restitution is being applied to a velocity that already contains this step's g·h.
Fix: set solver_config::restitution_bias to gravity * h. Confirm with the closed form: the hop you are seeing should be (e·g·h/(1−e))²/2g, and if it is, that is the bug and not a coincidence.
Friction does nothing at all, but μ is set.
Symptom: everything slides on everything as though μ were zero, and the friction impulse in the debug panel is always the zero vector.
Cause: §10. The friction solve is running before the normal solve, so Coulomb's limit is μ × 0.
Fix: normal impulses for every point, then friction for every point. It will not reproduce on a flat floor, so write the test on a slope.
Friction is direction-dependent, and the direction makes no sense.
Symptom: a puck slides further north than north-east on the same floor; the preferred directions change when the floor is re-oriented.
Cause: the box clip. The admissible set is a square in a basis that came out of the surface normal.
Fix: friction_model::cone, for 6.8%.
Everything sinks a few millimetres a second, forever.
Symptom: bodies at rest slowly descend into whatever they are resting on.
Cause: §12 — one pass over a multi-point manifold is not a converged solve. Check the residual before assuming anything else: if solve_report::max_residual is millimetres per second rather than 10⁻⁶, the solver has not finished.
Fix: 8.10. In the meantime, iterate.
Bodies sink at exactly 16 cm a second.
Symptom: a much faster and more regular sink than the one above, and it does not improve when you iterate.
Cause: the solve is running after the position update. Each step, the position advances at the velocity the solver then cancels, so the body loses g·h² = 2.7 mm per step whatever the solver does.
Fix: velocities, then contacts, then positions. The regularity is the diagnostic — divide the sink rate by h and see whether you get g·h.
Warm starting makes everything worse.
Symptom: turning on warm starting launches bodies off surfaces.
Cause: the inherited impulses are being applied inside the iteration loop, so they are applied once per iteration. §11's callout — it cost this lesson an afternoon and presented as friction failing.
Fix: warm_start_contacts once per step, outside the loop.
Two spheres bounce off each other at a slight angle.
Symptom: a perfectly head-on collision between two balls produces sideways motion and spin.
Cause: not the solver. §5's aside — EPA's normal for a sphere-sphere pair is 4.26° off, because epa_config::max_iterations was tuned on boxes and a sphere has no flat face to terminate on.
Fix: raise max_iterations to 64 for curved pairs and measure what it costs, or accept it; 8.7's reference-face snapping already hides it whenever one shape is a box.

17. Exercises

  1. Warm-up: predict the permanent hop. Run ./build/demos/impulse --scene 1 --e 0.9 --no-bias. Before you look at the panel, compute the height the ball will settle into hopping at, from e·g·h/(1−e) with h = 1/60. Then check.

    Hint

    g·h = 0.1635 m/s. The terminal speed is a velocity; the height is v²/2g.

    Solution

    v∞ = 0.9 × 0.1635 / 0.1 = 1.4715 m/s, so the hop is 1.4715²/(2 × 9.81) = 0.1104 m — eleven centimetres, forever. Turn the bias back on with [B] and it decays to nothing.

  2. Break the clamp on purpose. In solve_one_normal, replace the three-line accumulator clamp with delta = std::max(0.0f, delta) — clamping the increment rather than the total. Verify it changes nothing at one pass, then run the demo's creep scene with sixteen passes and watch what happens. Explain why the bug is invisible in the configuration this lesson ships.

    Hint

    What is the accumulator's value at the start of the first iteration?

    Solution sketch

    At one pass the accumulator starts at zero, so total = max(0, 0 + delta) and delta = max(0, delta) are the same expression. From the second iteration on they diverge: the correct version lets a point give back impulse that a later point's solve made unnecessary, and the broken one cannot, so the total only ever grows. The crate gains a small upward velocity every step and lifts off. This is the bug worth having seen once — it is silent in every single-iteration test you could write.

  3. Measure the box model's anisotropy yourself, on a different quantity. §7 measured sliding distance. Write a fixture that measures the sliding direction instead: give a crate a tangential velocity at 22.5° to tangent[0] and record the angle of the friction impulse that comes back. Under the cone it must be exactly opposite the velocity; under the box it will not be. How far off does it get, and at what angle is the error largest?

    Hint

    The box clips each component independently, so it pulls the impulse toward whichever diagonal is nearest. The error in direction is zero on the axes and on the diagonals — look between them.

    Solution sketch

    The worst direction error is near 22.5° from an axis, and for a fully sliding contact it reaches about 8°. That is friction pointing eight degrees away from directly opposing the motion, which means the box model does not merely apply the wrong amount of friction — it curves a sliding object's path. Worth knowing before you use it for a driving game.

  4. Engine challenge: a speculative contact. §12 established that the frozen penetration is one step of approach travel, because the narrow phase reports nothing until the shapes actually overlap. Use the two hooks that already exist — broadphase_config::margin from 8.8 and manifold_config::keep_slop from 8.7 — to see the contact one frame early, and give the solver a target velocity that closes the remaining gap in exactly one step rather than zero (u* = −separation/h when the point is separated). Measure the resting penetration before and after.

    Hint

    A separated point has a negative depth. The target velocity for it is “approach at exactly the rate that closes the gap this step, and no faster” — which is a cap, not a push, so it must not be allowed to make a point approach faster than it already is.

    Solution sketch

    This is the technique Box2D calls a speculative contact and it is genuinely the right answer to §12's third problem — better than a position correction, because it never adds energy. The resting penetration should fall to near zero for bodies arriving below some speed, and the failure mode to watch for is a body that arrives faster than margin/h and tunnels anyway. 8.10 revisits it with the position correction beside it so the two can be compared.

  5. Open: is the minimum really the right combine rule? §8 measured five published surface pairs and found min beating sqrt(a·b) 100% of the time under ±30% perturbation. Find more pairs whose three coefficients are all published — the constraint is that you need a on a, b on b and a on b from a source that measured all three the same way — and see whether the result survives. Then argue the other side: construct a scene where the minimum's insensitivity to the grippier material is a usability problem a designer would actually hit.

    Solution sketch

    The measurement is thin — five pairs — and the honest answer is that it is weak evidence strongly held. Metals on metals are where more data lives, and they are also where the minimum does worst (copper on steel: the minimum says 0.74 where the real number is 0.53, because two dissimilar metals are less adhesive than either is with itself). A defensible position is that the minimum is right whenever one surface is lubricious and wrong whenever both are clean metals, and that the geometric mean is a compromise that is never very right and never very wrong. If you can make that case with data, change the default back — and write down what it cost, because that is what this section is for.

18. Recap & next

The engine can resolve a contact. That is the first thing engine::phys has ever done that changes the world rather than describing it, and it took one equation: the relative velocity at a point, the effective mass along a direction, and a division.

What the engine gained:

  • A crate dropped on a floor lands on it and stays there, at 60 Hz, with no stiffness to tune and no per-scene constants.
  • Restitution that reproduces e²ⁿ·h₀ to a millimetre, with the gravity contamination removed exactly rather than masked by a threshold.
  • Coulomb friction with an isotropic cone clip, holding a slab on a slope to within half a degree of atan(μ), and turning a sliding sphere into a rolling one at exactly 5/7 of the speed it landed with.
  • A prepared-constraint shape that hoists the per-body work out of the inner loop, at 139 ns per manifold — so collision detection and one solver pass together are 0.65 ms of a 16.67 ms frame.
  • And the impulse fields carry_impulses has been matching across frames since 8.7 finally have numbers in them.

And what it did not gain, stated plainly because §12 measured it: one pass over a four-point manifold does not hold a crate up. It sinks 275 mm in fifteen seconds. Sixteen passes stop the sinking and leave the crate 7.2 mm inside the floor — the depth it arrived with, which no velocity solve can repair. Two different problems, needing two different pieces of machinery.

Lesson 8.10 builds both. Sequential impulses turn the single pass into an iteration that converges; warm starting — whose plumbing is already here and already measured — is what makes a handful of iterations enough rather than hundreds, by starting each contact from the impulse that held it last frame. Then a position correction for the frozen overlap, and islands and sleeping so that the crates that have settled stop costing anything at all. That is the lesson where a stack becomes possible, and it will redeem the promise Lesson 1.8 made in Module 1: “one impact per step for now — Module 8 iterates until the budget is spent.”

19. Further reading

  • Erin Catto, “Iterative Dynamics with Temporal Coherence” (GDC 2005) and the later Soft Constraints and Understanding Constraints talks. The clearest published account of the sequential-impulse solver this lesson is the first half of, by the author of Box2D. Read the 2005 paper for the impulse formulation and the 2014 talk for why the accumulated impulse is the thing you clamp.
  • Chris Hecker, “Physics, Part 3: Collision Response” (Game Developer, 1997). The derivation in §4 and §5 in its original game-development form, done in 2D and by hand. Thirty years old and still the clearest first exposure to the effective mass.
  • Brian Mirtich, Impulse-based Dynamic Simulation of Rigid Body Systems (PhD thesis, Berkeley, 1996). Where the impulse formulation is worked out properly, including the friction cases this lesson simplifies — in particular what happens when a contact transitions between sticking and sliding during the impulse.
  • W. J. Stronge, Impact Mechanics (Cambridge, 2nd ed.). For the honest version of restitution: why e is not a material constant, how it depends on impact speed, and the three different definitions of it that the literature uses interchangeably and should not.
  • David Baraff, “Fast Contact Force Computation for Nonpenetrating Rigid Bodies” (SIGGRAPH 1994) and the Pixar Physically Based Modeling course notes. The other tradition — solving all contacts simultaneously as a linear complementarity problem rather than iterating. Worth reading to know what the iteration in 8.10 is an approximation of.
  • Glenn Fiedler, “Collision Response and Coulomb Friction” (Gaffer on Games). The friction cone with pictures, from the same series that gave this course its fixed-timestep loop in Lesson 1.4.
  • Coulomb, Théorie des machines simples (1785). The friction model in §7 is unchanged since this, which is worth a moment's reflection given how much else in this course was invented after 1990.
