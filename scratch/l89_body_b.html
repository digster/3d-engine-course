2. Building intuition: a collision is an event, not an interval

The penalty method's mistake is not the spring. It is a modelling assumption so quiet that nobody states it: that the contact is a thing that lasts, with a force acting throughout. Ask what force acts during a collision and you are already committed to the contact having a duration for the force to act over, and to your simulation resolving that duration.

Look at what a real collision does. A steel ball bounces off a steel plate in something like a hundred microseconds. At 60 Hz your step is 16,667 microseconds — one hundred and sixty times longer. Whatever happened inside that collision, your simulation was not present for it. You do not know the force, you cannot resolve the force, and you do not need the force. What you can observe is the outcome: the ball arrived going one way and left going another.

So stop asking about force. Ask instead: what single, instantaneous change in velocity leaves the two surfaces moving the way a contact requires? That quantity — a change in momentum with no duration attached — is an impulse, and Lesson 8.2 §6 already argued the general case for it. A force needs a step size to become a velocity change, so the same physical event written as a one-step force produces different results at 30 Hz and 144 Hz; 8.2 measured a jump spanning 3.85 m at 30 Hz and 16.7 cm at 144, a factor of 23. Written as an impulse it has no h in it at all:

\[ \Delta \mathbf{v} = \frac{\mathbf{J}}{m}, \qquad \Delta \boldsymbol{\omega} = \mathbf{I}^{-1}(\mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{J}) \] delta v = J / m, and delta omega = inverse_inertia * (r cross J)

Both of those are already in the engine: add_impulse_at has been in rigid_body.hpp since 8.3, and its doc comment has said for two lessons that “everything Lesson 8.9 adds is deciding what J should be”. That is now literally the task.

Where the force went. If it bothers you that a force has vanished from a Newtonian simulation, it has not: an impulse is the integral of a force over the time it acts, J = ∫F dt. The impulse formulation keeps that integral and throws away the integrand, on the grounds that the integrand was never observable at this time step and the integral is. It is the same move as replacing a rendered motion-blur streak with a single sample — except that here the integral is exactly what the dynamics needs, so nothing is approximated at all.

3. Three demands on one velocity

A contact happens at a point — one of the four that 8.7's manifold produced — and the velocity that matters there is not either body's centre-of-mass velocity. It is the velocity of the material points that are touching, which for a rigid body is v + ω × r: the formula point_velocity has implemented since 8.2, and the one that makes a rigid body rigid.

Define the relative velocity at the contact, of body b's point with respect to body a's:

\[ \mathbf{u} = \big(\mathbf{v}_b + \boldsymbol{\omega}_b \times \mathbf{r}_b\big) - \big(\mathbf{v}_a + \boldsymbol{\omega}_a \times \mathbf{r}_a\big) \] u = (v_b + omega_b cross r_b) - (v_a + omega_a cross r_a)

with r_a and r_b the lever arms from each centre of mass to the contact point. The manifold's normal n points from a toward b — collide.hpp's convention, unchanged since 8.4 — so u·n is negative when the two are approaching, positive when separating, and zero at a resting contact. Get that sign backwards and every contact in the scene sucks instead of pushing, which is a memorable ten minutes.

Now: everything a contact wants is a statement about u, and there are exactly three of them.

Non-penetration

The surfaces may separate and may slide past each other; they may not approach. So after the impulse, u·n ≥ 0. And a contact can only push: a floor does not reach up and grab a crate that is leaving. That makes the impulse along the normal non-negative, j ≥ 0, and that clamp is the only nonlinearity in the entire normal solve. Nothing else in this lesson has an inequality in it.

Restitution

A bouncy contact leaves at some fraction of the speed it arrived with. Newton's experimental law, which is a definition dressed as a law: the coefficient of restitution e is the ratio of separation speed to approach speed, so we want u·n = −e·(u·n)before.

Notice what kind of demand that is. It is the same shape as non-penetration — “make u·n equal to this number” — with a different number on the right. So restitution needs no second equation and no second impulse; it changes the target from zero to −e·(approach) and the same solve delivers it. That is why e will not appear anywhere in the solver code below: it appears once, when the target is computed, and never again.

Friction

The third demand lives in the plane perpendicular to n. “Not sliding” means the tangential part of u is zero, so the natural demand is to drive it there — but only as far as the surfaces can manage. Coulomb's condition, which is four centuries old and still the model every engine ships, says the tangential impulse cannot exceed a fixed multiple of the normal one:

\[ \|\mathbf{j}_t\| \;\le\; \mu\, j_n \] the magnitude of the tangential impulse is at most mu times the normal impulse

j_t is a two-dimensional vector in the contact plane, so the admissible set is a disc of radius μ·j_n. Stack that disc over the normal axis — the radius grows in proportion to the height — and you have swept out a cone, which is where the phrase friction cone comes from. §6 is about what happens when you use a square instead.

And notice the coupling, because it is the reason the two solves cannot be independent: the cone's radius is μ times the answer to the normal demand. Friction cannot be computed in parallel with the normal impulse, because until the normal impulse exists there is no cone to clip against. §9 measures what happens when you get that order wrong, and the answer is worse than “slightly less friction”.

Why each is one equation in one unknown

Three demands, and each turns out to need one scalar. The reason is linearity, and it is worth seeing explicitly before we do any algebra.

Apply an impulse J = j·d along some direction d at the contact. Each body's linear velocity changes by ±j·d/m and each angular velocity by ±j·I⁻¹(r × d) — both proportional to j, because every step from impulse to velocity is a linear map. So the change in u·d is proportional to j too:

\[ \Delta(\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{d}) \;=\; j\,k_{\mathbf{d}} \] the change in u along d equals j times a constant k that depends only on d

for some number k_d that depends on the masses, the inertia tensors, the lever arms and the direction — but not on j. Which means “how big an impulse do I need to change this velocity by that much?” is a division, not a solve. The whole of the next section is working out what that number is.
