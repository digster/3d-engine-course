12. The budget

8.2 measured a whole body update at a couple of nanoseconds. The angular half is considerably more than a couple, and the first version of this section did not find that out because it was measuring the wrong thing.

The first draft of §12 measured a memcpy. Each arm was written as body_world w = seed; … w.step(h), so that both arms would start from identical state — a reasonable instinct, and it put a 557 KB copy of the pool inside the timed region. Every arm reported 34–58 ns/body against 8.2's 1–2, every ratio came out near 1, and the conclusion would have been "the angular half is free". Each arm gets its own world now, built once, and the timed body is a single step.

12.1 Where the time actually went

With that fixed, the step came out at 33.793 ns/body — twenty times 8.2's, which is a lot for an update whose physics is one matrix-vector product and an integrator step. Breaking it down piece by piece found that most of it was not physics:

  • mat3_from_quat, 7.843 ns — and the step was calling it twice, once inside world_inverse_inertia and once twenty lines later inside angular_momentum, on the same quaternion.
  • angular_momentum, 13.418 ns — 40% of the whole step, for a diagnostic that is computed whether or not anybody reads it, and which carried a 3×3 inverse because the body stored only the inverse tensor.
  • The rest — the sandwich, the integrator, the report — accounting for the remainder.

Two changes followed, and both are visible in the listings. The forward tensor is now stored beside its inverse, which removes the 3×3 inverse from the report and from the gyroscopic term. And the step builds its rotation matrix once and uses it for both, which is why step spells the sandwich out rather than calling world_inverse_inertia.

The hoist is measurable on its own, inside one run, which is what makes it a fact rather than a before-and-after story: 

  I.3  mat3_from_quat, built twice or hoisted
       twice    14.577 ns/body
       hoisted  11.139 ns/body
       ratio hoisted/twice 0.764

  I.4  R I^-1 R^T rebuilt    8.545 ns/body
       read from a cache     0.621 ns/body
       ratio cached/rebuilt 0.073

Together the two changes took the step from 33.793 to 18.778 ns/body, a factor of 1.80, with no change to a single number this lesson reports. That is the whole of §12's optimisation: it is not clever, it is noticing that a rotation matrix was being built twice.

Three things to read out of that.

The exponential map costs 17% of the angular half, for an attitude error four orders of magnitude smaller. Whether that is worth it depends entirely on what the body is doing: for gravel it is not, for a spinning object the player is watching it obviously is, and for a camera or a vehicle it is not close. Hence a switch.

The accurate gyroscopic mode is also the expensive one, at 2.85× the angular half — and this is not what 8.2 found about gravity, where the exact arrangement also turned out to be the cheap one. Two lessons, two optimisation questions, opposite answers. What the 2.85× buys is figure 9: three orders of magnitude of conservation. Whether to pay it is a per-body decision, which is why it is a per-body field.

And 172 bytes is now three cache lines, from 8.2's one. That is the number to watch when 8.6's broadphase starts walking thousands of bodies: 5.5's ECS lessons argued that a struct which outgrows a cache line starts paying for fields the loop does not touch, and this one has just tripled. Nothing in this lesson has enough bodies for it to matter; 8.10's solver will.

13. What this does not do

The engine can turn. Six things it deliberately cannot, each of which is somebody's later lesson or somebody's standing debt.

No centre-of-mass offset. A body's centre of mass is its state.position, always. inertia_assembly computes where a compound body balances so that you can place it there, rather than carrying an offset through every subsequent calculation. That is a real restriction — an authored mesh whose origin is at its feet needs its inertia and its position reconciled by the caller — and it removes an offset from every line of 8.9's solver, which is what it was traded for.

No diagonalisation. Every closed form here is diagonal because its shape is symmetric, but a compound body's tensor generally is not, and this engine never finds its principal axes. Doing so is an eigenvalue problem on a symmetric 3×3 — Jacobi's method, about fifty lines — and it is genuinely out of scope here rather than hard. What it would buy is the ability to store three floats instead of nine, which is what Bullet does. Exercise 4, and Golub & Van Loan §8.5 if you want it done properly.

L is not stored, so the momentum formulation's conservation is as good as a round trip through two rotation matrices, which is §9.4's counterintuitive column. Fixing it means adding angular_momentum to the struct and converting at every solver iteration. Named here so that it is a decision rather than an omission.

No angular tunnelling protection. max_spin measures how fast the fastest body is turning, which is the rotational twin of max_travel, and nothing does anything about it. A body turning more than about a radian per step cannot have its contacts tracked frame to frame, because the features that were touching are on the other side by the time the next test runs. 8.8's manifold persistence is where that comes due.

No collision, still. Four lessons away. The spin demo has no floor and no gravity, which is the experiment rather than a missing feature — every claim in it is about a body with nothing acting on it.

And the gyroscopic default is off, which means that out of the box this engine's bodies do not tumble. It is PhysX's default too — its PxRigidBodyFlag::eENABLE_GYROSCOPIC_FORCES is opt-in — while Bullet decided the other way: since 2.83 every btRigidBody starts with BT_ENABLE_GYROSCOPIC_FORCE_IMPLICIT_BODY set, which is this lesson's implicit mode. Off is a defensible default — but it means the most interesting thing in this lesson is something you have to ask for.

Expected result

A window, 960 × 540, split into two panels. On the left, a wireframe box drawn in parallel projection with its three body axes in red, green and blue, and one amber line that does not move — that is the angular momentum. On the right, the three components of the angular velocity in body axes, plotted against twelve seconds of simulated time and wrapping round.

Within three seconds the green trace — the spin axis — drops through zero to its negative, while the red and blue ones spike and subside. The box on the left visibly turns over. 16. Common pitfalls & debugging

Symptom: a body will not rotate about its own long axis, however much torque you apply. Cause: an inertia tensor built with the derived form |r|² 1 − r ⊗ r on a long thin body, where catastrophic cancellation returns an exact zero for the small principal moment (§4.4). Fix: build the diagonal from the terms that belong in it. Diagnosis: print inspect_inertia(...).diagonal and look for a zero where you expected a small number. inverse_inertia's relative test catches the near-singular cases, and it catching them is the signal.

Symptom: a tumble that looks plausible and is wrong, with no error anywhere. Cause: Rᵀ I R where R I Rᵀ was meant. It is symmetric, has the same trace, the same determinant and the same principal moments, and passes every test inspect_inertia can run (§6.2). Fix: read the sandwich right to left and check that the first thing applied to ω carries it into body axes. Diagnosis: put the body at a generic orientation — never a right angle, which passes either way — and check that the world Ixx matches the body moment you expect.

Symptom: a body shears, then flattens, then disappears. Cause: an orientation integrated without renormalising. The linearised update inflates |q| by (|ω|h)²/8 per step, 23% in a second at 10 rad/s, and mat3_from_quat on a non-unit quaternion produces a matrix with a scale in it (§7.3). Fix: advance_orientation renormalises; if you are writing orientation from outside the step, normalise it yourself. Diagnosis: step_report::max_unit_error exists exactly for this and reads about 1e−7 when everything is well.

Symptom: the rotation is right for a body at the origin facing forward and drifts for any other. Cause: an angular velocity used on the wrong side of the quaternion product — a body-space ω composed as though it were world-space, or vice versa. Both produce unit quaternions and smooth motion; they differ by 57.6° after one second in §7's F.5. Fix: world space multiplies from the left, body space from the right. Diagnosis: the bug vanishes when the body starts at the identity orientation, which is a strong tell and is why it survives testing.

Symptom: a torque-free body slowly speeds up until the simulation is unusable. Cause: an explicitly integrated gyroscopic term (§9.1). Fix: implicit_term or momentum. Diagnosis: plot |L| on a body with no torque on it. It must be flat, and a rising |L| means the integrator is inventing angular momentum — there is nothing else it can mean.

Symptom: a compound body's tensor is far too small and nothing complains. Cause: parts added without shifting them to the common centre of mass (§5.2) — 15.3× too small on the dumbbell, and it passes every validity test. Fix: inertia_assembly. Diagnosis: compare against a hand estimate of the dominant parallel-axis term, m d², which for a spread-out assembly is usually most of the answer.

Symptom: a measurement of a perturbation oscillates instead of growing, and a fitted rate is 20% low. Cause: measuring ω in world space when Euler's equations are body-frame equations (§10.1's callout). Fix: transpose(mat3_from_quat(q)) * ω. Diagnosis: the oscillation is at the spin rate, which is a strong tell — a perturbation has no reason to know about it.

Symptom: a benchmark says the angular half is free. Cause: a copy of the body pool inside the timed region (§12's callout). Fix: build the state once, outside. Diagnosis: the control arm. Two identical arms that come out at ratio 1.000 while every real comparison also comes out near 1.000 is the shape of a benchmark measuring something both arms have in common.

17. Exercises

1. The hollow box. Add inertia_hollow_box — a box of six thin plates rather than a solid volume, which is what a crate actually is. Build it with inertia_assembly from six rectangular plates, using the plate's own tensor (m(w² + h²)/12 about its normal, and the perpendicular-axis theorem for the other two) plus the parallel-axis shift to each face. Check it against inertia_of_points sampling only the surface. Hint: for a thin plate in the xz plane, Iy = Ix + Iz exactly — that is the perpendicular-axis theorem, and it is the equality case of the triangle inequality inspect_inertia checks.

2. The wheel that does not slip. Write a demo with a single body on which point_velocity at the lowest point is exactly zero, given a forward velocity v and a radius R. Derive the angular velocity that makes it so before you write it, then verify the contact patch's speed numerically at several points around the rim. Hint: the answer is ω = v/R about the correct axis, and getting the sign right the first time is the exercise.

3. The reaction wheel. Give a body a second, small body embedded in it, and spin the small one with add_torque. The big one must counter-rotate, and the total angular momentum of the pair must not change. Measure it. Hint: this is how a satellite points itself with no fuel, and it is Newton's third law applied to torques — apply the equal and opposite torque to the carrier.

4. (Engine challenge) Principal axes. Implement diagonalise(mat3) for a symmetric matrix using the cyclic Jacobi method: repeatedly find the largest off-diagonal element and apply the 2×2 rotation that zeroes it, accumulating the rotations. Return the three principal moments and the rotation to principal axes. Then use it to store a body's inertia as three floats and a quaternion, as Bullet does, and measure what that saves against §12's numbers. Solution sketch: each sweep drives the sum of squared off-diagonal elements down by the element you zeroed, so it converges quadratically; six to ten sweeps is plenty for a 3×3 in float. Golub & Van Loan §8.5 has it done properly, including the rotation angle that avoids cancellation.

5. (Engine challenge) Store the momentum. Add vec3 angular_momentum to rigid_body as a stored field, make the momentum mode use it instead of rebuilding L each step, and re-run §9's G.5 table. The third column should stop getting worse with a finer step and become flat at the float noise floor. Then find out what it cost: every place that writes angular_velocity — add_impulse_at, add_angular_impulse, damping, and every solver iteration in 8.10 — now has to write both, or convert. That is the trade the engine declined, and doing it is the only way to see its real size.

18. Recap & next

The engine can turn. A rigid_body carries an orientation, an angular velocity, a torque accumulator and an inertia tensor; add_force_at is the first function in eighty-six lessons that can make something tumble; and inertia.hpp knows what six shapes weigh, how to carry a tensor to a new point and how to carry it into a new basis.

The ideas worth keeping, in the order they arrived:

  • Resistance to turning is a tensor, because the two cross products in L = Σ m r × (ω × r) do not cancel into a scalar. The outer product is where a cross product becomes a matrix, and that is the step the whole derivation turns on.
  • A derivation tells you what to compute, not how. |r|² 1 − r ⊗ r returns exactly zero for a plank in float, and −[r]ₓ[r]ₓ — the same algebra — returns the right answer and is faster.
  • A tensor is about a point and in a basis. The parallel-axis theorem moves the point and only works from the centre of mass; R I Rᵀ changes the basis and has a plausible wrong twin that passes every structural test there is.
  • An orientation is not a vector, so it does not integrate by addition. The update that replaces it inflates the quaternion by (ωh)²/8 and turns short by (ωh)²/12, both predictable before measurement.
  • L is conserved and ω is not, which is a fact about I_world changing rather than about anything acting on the body — and the reachable range of |ω| is derivable by hand, because both conserved quantities are linear in Lᵢ².
  • The gyroscopic term is the price of a state variable. Store the momentum and it vanishes from the equations — and conserving the right quantity is still not enough, because the body can do the wrong thing with it.
  • Two of a body's three principal axes are stable and one is not, which is decided by nothing more than the ordering of three numbers, and is why a thrown book flips.

And the measurement worth remembering is §12's, because it is the shape of most real optimisations: the step was twenty times slower than 8.2's, and the largest single cause was a rotation matrix being built twice on the same quaternion, twenty lines apart, in two functions that were each perfectly reasonable on their own.

Next is Lesson 8.4, and the module turns a corner. Everything from 8.1 to here has been about a single body moving on its own; from 8.4 onward, bodies start to notice each other. It opens with the cheapest questions — does this sphere overlap that one, does this axis-aligned box overlap that one — and then the Separating Axis Theorem, which answers it for boxes that are not axis-aligned and is one of the most useful ideas in the module. The point_velocity and add_impulse_at we built today are what 8.9 will apply the answers with.

19. Further reading

  • Baraff, Physically Based Modeling: Rigid Body Simulation (SIGGRAPH course notes, freely available) — the canonical derivation of everything in §4 through §9, and notably the one that uses the momentum formulation of §9.3 as its primary state. Read parts I and II; the numbered equations map almost one-to-one onto inertia.hpp.
  • Catto, Numerical Methods and Soft Constraints (GDC) — the practical side of §9, including why engines integrate the way they do and what they give up. Catto's Box2D writings are the clearest explanation anywhere of the gap between a correct integrator and a shippable one.
  • Goldstein, Classical Mechanics, chapter 5 — the inertia tensor, principal axes, Euler's equations and the Poinsot construction, which is the geometric picture behind figure 8. Heavier than this lesson and worth it if you want the tumble in full.
  • Golub & Van Loan, Matrix Computations, §8.5 — the symmetric eigenvalue problem, for exercise 4.
  • Higham, Accuracy and Stability of Numerical Algorithms, chapter 1 — catastrophic cancellation, which is §4.4's subject, done properly and with a great deal more rigour than one worked example.
  • Terzopoulos/Witkin's and Bullet's source — specifically btRigidBody::computeGyroscopicImpulseImplicit_Body, which is §9.2's Newton step as a shipping engine writes it.
  • "Dzhanibekov effect" — the original orbital footage is thirty seconds long and worth watching once, because a wing-nut flipping in free fall is more convincing than any amount of §10.
