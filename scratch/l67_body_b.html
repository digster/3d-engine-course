4Deriving the Tangent Frame

Here is the part that usually arrives as a formula to be copied. It is four lines of algebra and it follows from one observation, so there is no reason to copy anything.

Take a triangle. Its three corners have positions p0, p1, p2 in metres, and texture coordinates w0, w1, w2 in texture units. Two edges from the first corner describe the triangle in space:

\[ e_1 = p_1 - p_0, \qquad e_2 = p_2 - p_0 \]

…and the corresponding uv differences describe the same two directions in the chart:

\[ (\Delta u_1, \Delta v_1) = w_1 - w_0, \qquad (\Delta u_2, \Delta v_2) = w_2 - w_0 \]

That correspondence is the entire content of the derivation, so it is worth saying slowly. e1 and (Δu1, Δv1) are two descriptions of one walk across the surface — one measured in metres, one in texture units. If T is the world direction you move when u increases by one, and B is the direction for v, then walking Δu1 in u and Δv1 in v must land you exactly where e1 lands you:

\[ \begin{aligned} e_1 &= \Delta u_1\,T \;+\; \Delta v_1\,B \\ e_2 &= \Delta u_2\,T \;+\; \Delta v_2\,B \end{aligned} \]

Two equations, two unknowns. That is all a tangent frame is — and the whole reputation of this subject for being fiddly comes from being shown the answer rather than the question.

@@FIG_DERIVATION@@

Solving is inverting a 2×2. Write the system as a matrix equation with T and B as the unknowns:

\[ \begin{bmatrix} e_1 \\ e_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \Delta u_1 & \Delta v_1 \\ \Delta u_2 & \Delta v_2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} T \\ B \end{bmatrix} \]

and multiply through by the inverse. With det = Δu1Δv2 − Δu2Δv1:

\[ \begin{aligned} T &= \tfrac{1}{\det}\big(\Delta v_2\,e_1 - \Delta v_1\,e_2\big) \\ B &= \tfrac{1}{\det}\big(\Delta u_1\,e_2 - \Delta u_2\,e_1\big) \end{aligned} \]
Worked example — the triangle verify_67 §B uses

p0 = (0, 0, 0), p1 = (2, 0, 0), p2 = (0, 0, −2), lying flat in the xz plane so N = (0, 1, 0). Texture coordinates (0, 0), (1, 0) and (0, 1).

e1 = (2, 0, 0) and e2 = (0, 0, −2). The uv deltas are (1, 0) and (0, 1), so the 2×2 is the identity and det = 1.

T = 1·(2,0,0) − 0·(0,0,−2) = (2, 0, 0), which normalises to (1, 0, 0).
B = 1·(0,0,−2) − 0·(2,0,0) = (0, 0, −2), which normalises to (0, 0, −1).

Sanity-check it against the picture: the triangle's u runs along +x, so the direction of increasing u is +x. It does. And v runs toward −z, so B points that way. It does. verify_67 §B gets exactly these to six decimals.

What the determinant is, and why zero is not a bug

det is twice the signed area the triangle occupies in the uv chart — which is the same signed-area quantity Lesson 2.4 derived barycentric coordinates from, applied to a different pair of axes.

So det = 0 means the triangle has no area in texture space: three corners on one line of the chart, or all three at the same point. That is not a degenerate input to be guarded against — it is an untextured face, or a collapsed unwrap, and both are ordinary. There is genuinely no tangent frame to compute, so the face contributes nothing and its vertices fall back to whatever their other faces say:

Dividing anyway would put an infinity into every vertex the face touches and spread it through the average — so a single untextured face would poison a region of an otherwise fine model. verify_67 §B feeds a triangle whose three uvs are collinear and asserts the output is finite.

And it is why cube.gltf looks odd under a normal map

Lesson 6.6 gave assets/cube.gltf one uv per box corner, so that the round-trip test in §A could compare index-for-index against cube_mesh(). That was the right call for that test and it has a consequence nobody could see until now: on four of the cube's six faces, u is constant — so those faces have zero uv area and no tangent frame at all.

Normal-map the cube and the result is banded rather than dimpled, because only two faces have a chart. That is not a bug in this lesson; it is a property of that asset, which normal mapping is simply the first thing to make visible. Use --model torus.obj for a mesh with a real chart on every triangle.

From per-face to per-vertex

The solve gives a frame per triangle, and a vertex shared by several triangles needs one answer. The same treatment with_normals gives normals applies without a word of new argument: accumulate, then normalise.

And the weighting is free, for the reason Lesson 3.6 gave about area-weighted normals. The solved T is not normalised before accumulation, and its magnitude scales with the triangle's own size — so summing the raw solutions weights each face by its area without a single extra multiply.

Then one step that has no counterpart in the normal case:

The accumulated tangent is an average of what several faces wanted, and the accumulated normal is an average of what several faces wanted, and there is no reason the two averages should be perpendicular. Subtracting the component along N leaves what lies in the tangent plane.

Note which vector is held fixed. The normal is what we refuse to move, because it is what the lighting is actually about; the tangent only has to span the plane, so it is the one that gets adjusted. Doing it the other way round would tilt the shading normal to accommodate a texture coordinate, which is precisely backwards.

5The Fourth Component

mesh::tangents is a span of vec4, not vec3, and the fourth component is not padding. It is the frame's handedness: +1 or −1.

Once N and T are known, the bitangent is almost determined — it is perpendicular to both, so it is cross(N, T) up to a sign. Which sign is not determined by the geometry at all. It is determined by the uv chart, and a chart can be mirrored.

Every symmetric model has a mirrored chart

An artist unwrapping a character does not unwrap both arms. They unwrap one, reflect it, and lay both halves on the same region of texture — which halves the texture memory and means a detail painted once appears on both sides.

On the reflected half, u increases in the opposite direction, so the frame comes out left-handed where the other half is right-handed. That is not a defect in the model; it is the point of mirroring.

So the sign has to be recorded, and it is recorded by testing the solved B against the one cross(N, T) would produce:

Rerun §4's worked example with u negated — texture coordinates (0,0), (−1,0), (0,1) — and the arithmetic falls out: det = −1, so T = (−1, 0, 0) and B = (0, 0, −1). But cross(N, T) is now (0, 0, +1), which disagrees with B. Handedness −1. verify_67 §C asserts exactly that.

Why store a sign instead of the bitangent

Twelve bytes a vertex to save one cross product a fragment is a poor trade on its own. The decisive argument is different: a stored bitangent can disagree with the N and T beside it, and a recomputed one cannot.

After interpolation across a triangle, three separately-interpolated vectors are no longer mutually perpendicular and no longer unit length. Recomputing B from the N and T you actually have guarantees a frame that is consistent with itself. This is the same argument Lesson 6.5 made for deriving material::textured(): a value you compute cannot contradict the value it came from.

glTF stores it this way too (§3.7.2.1: "the w component […] is 1 or −1 and indicates the handedness of the tangent basis"), which is a useful independent vote.

Get the sign wrong and the failure is specific and recognisable: the lighting on the mirrored half of a model is the mirror image of the lighting on the other half. Bumps lit from the left on one arm are lit from the right on the other. verify_67 §C measures it — the two handednesses give normals whose bitangent components are exact negatives — and it reads as a modelling error rather than a shading one, which is why it survives.

6The Basis Change, and the Lavender

With the frame in hand the fragment's job is four lines. Sample, decode, build, multiply.

@@FIG_BASIS@@

The decode is the step worth pausing on, because it explains something you have already seen a hundred times without being told why.

A direction has negative components. A byte does not. So the encoding is an offset — the stored value is (c + 1) / 2 and the decode is c = 2·value − 1. Which means the direction (0, 0, 1) — "no change at all" — is stored as (0.5, 0.5, 1.0), or (128, 128, 255) in bytes.

That is why every normal map is lavender

The pale blue-violet you see in every normal map ever authored is not a convention somebody chose, or a tint applied for recognisability. It is arithmetic. It is what "this surface is flat here" looks like when a signed direction is packed into unsigned bytes, and the more nearly flat a region is the more nearly that exact colour it becomes.

Which also tells you how to read one at a glance: reddish means tilted toward +u, greenish means tilted toward +v, and the strength of the tint is the steepness.

Then the basis change itself, and it is a matrix multiply written as its own definition. Lesson 2.5 established that a matrix is where the basis vectors land — and here T, B and N are the basis vectors, so the multiply is:

\[ n_{\text{world}} \;=\; T\,x \;+\; B\,y \;+\; N\,z \]

Read the z term and the round trip is obvious: a flat map has x = y = 0 and z = 1, so the result is N exactly. Nothing changes. That single property is the most valuable test in this lesson, because every mistake it is possible to make here breaks it — a wrong colour space, a wrong decode range, a frame that is not orthonormal, a bitangent with the wrong sign, a basis change written in the wrong order, a tangent that was never re-orthogonalised. One assertion, six bugs.

And the round trip is not exact, which is the finding

verify_67 §D asserted worst < 1e-6 on its first run and measured 5.5460 × 10−3. The renderer was right and the assertion was wrong, for a reason worth considerably more than the check:

0.5 is not an 8-bit code

The codes either side are 127 and 128, giving 0.498 and 0.502. Take 128 — the conventional choice — and decode it: 128/255 × 2 − 1 is 1/255, not zero.

So the flattest normal map that can be stored encodes the direction (0.0039, 0.0039, 1), and every flat normal map in existence tilts its surface by 0.318°. Not the ones authored badly. All of them.

That is why 16-bit normal maps exist, and why you will find pipelines that argue about 127 against 128 — neither is exactly right, and the argument is about which direction to be wrong in.

The fix in the harness is not to loosen the tolerance — Lesson 6.3's rule is that a tolerance widened to admit a failure is a test deleted slowly. It is to predict the floor from the encoding and assert the measurement equals it:

Which is a strictly stronger test than the one that failed: it now catches an implementation that is better than the quantisation floor as well as one that is worse, and either would mean the encoding is not what we think it is.

7Which Matrix Carries a Tangent

The frame is computed in model space and used in world space, so something has to carry it across. Lesson 3.6 established that a normal is not carried by the model matrix — it needs the inverse transpose, because a non-uniform scale does not preserve perpendicularity. The obvious move is to use that matrix for the tangent too.

It is wrong, and the reason is the same reason, arriving on the other side of it.

@@FIG_MATRIX@@
The distinction in one sentence each

A normal is defined by being perpendicular to the surface. Perpendicularity is the property that a non-uniform scale destroys, and the inverse transpose is exactly the correction that restores it.

A tangent lies in the surface. It is the direction the surface goes as u increases — which makes it a difference of positions, and a difference of positions transforms precisely the way positions do.

verify_67 §F establishes that rather than asserting it, by transporting two nearby points through the model matrix and subtracting — which is what "difference of positions" means operationally. Under a scale of (2, 1, 1) applied to a tangent at 45°:

The two candidate transforms for a tangent under a non-uniform scale, against the ground truth
RouteResultAgainst the truth
the model matrix(0.894, 0.447, 0) 0.0 — exact
the normal matrix(0.447, 0.894, 0) 0.632
…which is 36.9° apart, on one object.

Note what makes the wrong answer dangerous rather than obvious: both results still lie in the surface. The frame is not broken, it is skewed — the normal map's bumps are rotated within the plane — and a skewed normal map reads as an asset that was authored at the wrong angle.

And, exactly as with the normal-matrix bug that Lesson 3.6 put on a key: under a uniform scale the two agree to 8.4 × 10−8. An engine that gets this wrong looks perfect on every object nobody stretched, which is most of them.

8Implementation

8.1 — A fourth varying, through four places

"What a vertex carries" is written down in three separate places in this engine, and a tangent has to be threaded through all of them plus the clipper:

The four definitions of a vertex a new attribute must pass through
TypeWhereWhat it is for
mesh_data::tangentsgfx/mesh.hpp the stored geometry — a fifth parallel array
clip_vertex::tangentgfx/clip.hpp so a corner the clipper invents has one
vertex::tangentgfx/raster.hpp the varying the fragment interpolates
gpu_vertex_pnu::tx…twgfx/gpu_mesh.hpp the interleaved GPU form

The clipper is the one people forget. A triangle cut by the near plane gets new corners that were never in the mesh, and there is nowhere to get their tangent from except interpolation — so it is one more line beside the normal's, lerped with the same crossing parameter (Lesson 3.3).

And w is lerped too, which is the interesting case

Across an ordinary triangle both corners carry the same handedness and the interpolation is the identity. Across one that spans a mirroring seam they carry +1 and −1, the interpolated value passes through zero, and the frame collapses in the middle of the triangle.

That is a real artefact and it is not the clipper's to fix: an exporter splits the vertices at such a seam, exactly as it does at a uv seam. Clamping the sign here would hide it while leaving the tangent itself interpolated through the same degeneracy.

8.3 — The fragment

The Gram-Schmidt appears again, here at the fragment, having already been done at the vertex — and that is not redundancy. The vertex pass makes the frame orthonormal at the vertices; interpolation across the triangle destroys both properties again, exactly as it does for the normal (Lesson 3.8 measured that). The per-vertex pass is what makes the inputs sane; the per-fragment pass is what makes the frame sane.

And the guard on the whole branch is worth reading, because it needed no new flag:

Geometry with no tangents cannot be normal mapped, because tangent space is the uv parameterisation. The zero xyz that tangent_at returns for a mesh without them is what makes that checkable here rather than needing a flag alongside — the same trick normal_at has used since Lesson 3.6.

8.4 — A normal map with an analytic answer

The test image is generated rather than painted, for the reason Lesson 3.9 generated its checkerboard and 6.6 generated its glTF: the course ships no third-party assets, and every number a lesson quotes has to be reproducible from the repository alone.

It is an egg-carton — a height field whose normal has a closed form, so verify_67 §E can compute the right answer and compare rather than looking at it:

\[ k = 2\pi c, \qquad h(u,v) = \tfrac{s}{k}\cos(ku)\cos(kv), \qquad \mathbf{n} \propto (-\partial_u h,\; -\partial_v h,\; 1) \]
The 1/k, and the build where it was not there

The first version used s as the amplitude directly. A·cos(ku)cos(kv) has a peak gradient of A·k, and k grows with the cell count — so an amplitude of 1 at six cells is a slope of 37.7, a surface tilted 88° everywhere. Every normal points sideways, n·l collapses, and the render goes dark.

It took a minute to realise that was a parameterisation bug pointing at the renderer. Dividing the amplitude by k makes strength mean the maximum slope: 1.0 is a 45° tilt at any cell count. A parameter whose effect changes when you change an unrelated parameter is one nobody can author against — and its failures accuse the wrong subsystem.

§E compares 1,369 samples against the closed form and agrees to 5.18 × 10−3, against a bound of √3/255 = 6.79 × 10−3 — and that bound is derived, not chosen: rounding to the nearest of 256 codes is a half-code error, a half code in the stored [0,1] range is 0.5/255, the [−1,1] decode doubles it to 1/255, and three channels of that in the worst direction is √3/255.

(The first draft of that line said 1/510 and forgot the doubling, which produced a bound below the true floor and failed a correct implementation. A tolerance that is wrong rather than merely loose is the more expensive of the two mistakes.)
