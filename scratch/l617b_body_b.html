6A spot's shadow: 6.8's machinery through a pyramid

Lesson 6.8 built the whole shadow-mapping apparatus — a depth-only pass from the light, a comparison against the stored depth, a bias sized from the surface's slope and the filter's reach, PCF — and every part of it was written for a camera that happened to be orthographic. Most of that was a coincidence of the sun, not a dependency. shadow_map takes a light_camera and never asks how it was built; the depth-only pass draws through whatever matrix it is handed; the comparison compares. So the plan for a spot light is the obvious one: build a perspective camera at the lamp, looking down its axis, and let everything else run. This section is about the one part of 6.8 for which that plan fails, and it opens with the failure, because the failure is the clearest statement of what a bias is for.

6.1 — The failure first

Render the two lamps of the harness scene — a warm point light on the right, a cool spot from the left, a box hovering above the ground and a thin sign standing on it — with shadows on and no bias at all. The left half of Figure 4 is the result, seen from straight above. The ground shadows itself, across both pools of light. This is the acne of 6.8, and it is here for 6.8's reason: the map stores the depth of the surface at each texel's sample point, a fragment between samples recomputes its own depth, and on a surface tilted with respect to the light half of all fragments are "downhill" of the sample they are compared with, and lose. verify_617b §F judges 67,715 points on the spot light's side and calls 19,502 of them acne — 29%. The right half is the bias this section derives: zero acne, and zero leaks, against a ground truth that shares no code with the engine.

@@FIG_ACNE@@

The shape of the pattern is the tell that these are perspective maps. An orthographic map's acne is straight stripes, because its texels are the same size everywhere. Here the bulb's comes in nested squares, one family per face of its cube, and the spot's bends into arcs centred under the lamp, because a perspective map's texels grow with distance from it.

6.2 — The camera

fit_spot puts a square perspective pyramid at the lamp, looking along direction, with half-angle equal to the outer cone angle so the round pool of light is inscribed in the square map. Three decisions are worth defending.

  • The corners are wasted, deliberately. A circle inscribed in a square covers π/4 of it, so 21.5% of every spot map's texels lie outside the cone and are never read. The alternative, a circular map, does not exist; the alternative of fitting the square inside the circle would leave the edge of the pool unshadowed.
  • The near plane is 5 cm, and it cannot go far. Lesson 2.10 showed that a perspective depth buffer spends half its codes before twice the near distance, and 4.9 measured what pushing the near plane out buys. Here it cannot be pushed: anything nearer the lamp than the near plane is clipped from the shadow pass, casts nothing, and lets the light straight through. A moth on a bulb, a hand on a torch. Five centimetres is k_local_shadow_near.
  • The far plane is the range, and the cone is capped at 80°. Nothing beyond the range is lit, so nothing beyond it can matter. A spot wider than 80° from its axis is not given a spot map: its edge texels would stretch by 1/cos² 80° = 33 times, and at 90° without bound. That is a job for a cube, which is what a point light is (§7) — and since the fix after this lesson, a shadowed spot that wide is given one: shadows_through_cube sends it to six faces on both renderers, and its cone still does the masking. The first version of this page stopped at the cap, and §M of the harness measured the ring it left unshadowed: 304 ground points lit behind the box, and 69 pixels on which the two renderers disagreed away from any edge. Through a cube, none of either.

Figure 5 draws the pyramid and the property the rest of this section turns on. One texel of a 512 map on this spot covers 1.652 mm at one metre, 3.3 mm at two, and 13.2 mm at eight. An orthographic texel has one size; a perspective texel's size is proportional to the axial distance w of whatever it lands on. So light_camera::world_per_texel is redefined under perspective — it is the footprint at one metre — and every use of it multiplies by w first.

6.3 — 6.8's bias, carried through the depth curve

6.8 §4.4 derived the bias in three steps. The farthest filter tap is reach texels from the fragment; a texel is texel metres; walking that far across a surface tilted at θ changes its depth by that distance times tan θ. That is a bias in metres. The fourth step converted metres into the device units the comparison runs in by dividing by depth_range, the depth of the orthographic box — correct, because an orthographic projection makes device depth an affine function of distance, so one scale factor converts everywhere.

A perspective projection makes device depth a hyperbola. Written as a function of axial distance w — which is exactly what Lesson 2.10's zndc is — the engine's perspective() stores

\[ \text{depth}(w) = \frac{f\,(w - n)}{(f - n)\,w} \] depth(w) = far * (w - near) / ((far - near) * w)

— zero at the near plane, one at the far one, and steep near the lamp. There is no single scale factor. The honest conversion is also the simplest: move the point back toward the lamp by the bias in metres and ask the curve how far that moved it.

\[ \text{bias}_{\text{device}} = \text{depth}(w) - \text{depth}(w - b), \qquad b = \text{reach}\cdot(\text{texel}_{1\,\text{m}}\, w)\cdot\text{slope} \] bias_device = depth(w) - depth(w - b), where b = reach * (texel_at_1m * w) * slope

No derivative taken, nothing assumed small. Both renderers compute exactly this — the CPU in shadow_map::visibility, the GPU in local_depth_bias — with w clamped so the moved point never goes behind the near plane.

Worked example. The harness's spot: near 0.05 m, far 8 m, a 512 map. A surface at 45° on the axis, one metre out, one tap (reach ½√2 = 0.7071 texel).

texel here   = 1.652 mm × 1.0              = 1.652 mm
b           = 0.7071 × 1.652 mm × tan 45°    = 1.168 mm
depth(1.000000) = 8 × 0.950000 / (7.95 × 1.000000) = 0.9559748
depth(0.998832) = 8 × 0.948832 / (7.95 × 0.998832) = 0.9559160
bias        = 5.88 × 10−5

6.8's formula, applied unchanged, divides the same 1.168 mm by the depth range of 7.95 m and gets 1.469 × 10−4 — two and a half times too much. §F of the harness prints both across the whole range: 

   d (m)   texel mm    bias mm    derived dev  6.8 as-is dev     ratio
     0.5      0.826      0.584      1.177e-04      1.469e-04     1.248
     1.0      1.652      1.168      5.883e-05      1.469e-04     2.497
     2.0      3.303      2.336      2.939e-05      1.469e-04     4.999
     4.0      6.606      4.671      1.472e-05      1.469e-04     9.978
     8.0     13.212      9.342      7.331e-06      1.469e-04    20.036
  (6.8's formula is right at one distance only, d = near * far / 1 m = 0.40 m;
   inside it too small, outside it too large, in proportion to the distance)

Why a constant in device units goes so wrong is visible in the two curves. The texel grows like w; the depth curve's slope, fn/((f−n)w²), shrinks like 1/w²; their product — the right device bias — falls like 1/w. 6.8's constant is right exactly where those meet: at w = n·f/(1 m) = 0.40 m, the crossover the harness prints. (The "/1 m" is there because world_per_texel was defined at one metre; the first draft of that line printed "0.40 m²" and meant something dimensionally wrong.) Beyond it the constant is too large in proportion to distance. Too large is not acne — it is peter-panning, the shadow detaching from the foot of its caster — and §6.6 finds out how badly.

6.4 — The slope that an orthographic derivation cannot see

That conversion was the change this section set out to make. With it in place, the CPU and GPU renderers were compared pixel by pixel (§11 describes the instrument), and they disagreed about nineteen pixels that were nowhere near any shadow edge — five pixels and more from one, all on the ground inside the spot's pool, where the GPU's spot read slightly occluded and the CPU's read fully lit. Both were using 6.8's tan θ. A bias that is merely too small does not show up identically through two different filters, and that is how the disagreement exposed it: somewhere the slope was bigger than tan θ said.

The reason is in Figure 6, and it is the one assumption 6.8's derivation made without saying so. The map stores axial depth — the distance along the camera's axis, w — not the distance along the ray. Under an orthographic camera every ray is the axis and the two are the same number. Under perspective the rays diverge, and a wall that faces the lamp squarely, but sits off to one side of the axis, has points whose distance from the lamp is the same while their axial depth is not.

@@FIG_AXIAL@@

So derive the slope again, for axial depth. Put the lamp at the origin with its axis a. Lay the image plane at w = 1, and let a unit vector e in it, perpendicular to a, be the direction one texel step moves; a step of one texel moves the image point by t, the footprint at one metre. The ray through image point x along e is w·(a + xe) — every point on it at axial depth w. The receiving surface is a plane n·P = c. The ray meets it where

\[ w(x) = \frac{c}{\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf a + x\,\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf e}, \qquad \frac{dw}{dx} = -\frac{w^{2}\,(\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf e)}{c} \] w(x) = c / (n.a + x n.e); dw/dx = -w^2 (n.e) / c

and c is n·P for the point hit, which is the distance to it, dist, times the cosine between the normal and the ray: |c| = dist·cos θ. One texel is dx = t, and the worst direction e in the image plane — the one along the surface's steepest descent — makes |n·e| as large as it can be for a unit vector perpendicular to a, which is sin α, α the angle between the normal and the axis. Collecting, with w/dist = cos φ, φ the angle between the ray and the axis:

\[ \frac{\Delta w}{\text{texel}} = (t\,w)\cdot\frac{\sin\alpha\,\cos\varphi}{\cos\theta} \] axial depth change per texel = (t * w) * sin(alpha) * cos(phi) / cos(theta)

Three angles, each doing something you can name. θ (normal against ray) is the grazing term, and it diverges at the silhouette exactly as 6.8's did. α (normal against axis) is how steeply the surface cuts across the map's depth, and φ (ray against axis) is how far off-centre the texel is. On the axis φ = 0, the ray is the axis, α = θ, and the expression is tan θ — 6.8's result, recovered as the on-axis special case of this one. Off the axis they part company — on the harness's ground, lit obliquely by the spot, by enough to acne through one filter and not the other — and most sharply for a wall that faces the lamp squarely, which has θ = 0 but α = φ, and a slope of sin φ cos φ where 6.8's says zero.

Worked example. A wall facing the spot squarely, 30° off its axis, at axial distance 1 m.

tan θ                = tan 0° = 0                  (6.8: no slope bias)
sin α cos φ / cos θ = sin 30° · cos 30° / 1 = 0.4330
per texel           = 1.652 mm × 0.4330       = 0.715 mm of axial depth

Nearly half the depth change of a 45° surface, on a surface 6.8 says needs none. The fix is one function, perspective_slope, used by both renderers in place of tan θ under perspective; it cleared sixteen of the nineteen. (The last three were not a bias at all — §11 tells that story.) The normal-offset policy keeps using sin θ, correctly: it moves the point across the surface toward the lamp, and that is still about the ray.

6.5 — A slope of exactly zero, and the knife edge it exposes

The new slope does something 6.8's never did: it reaches exactly zero on a surface the light still sees. Take a point light directly above a floor and look at the floor through the cube's −Y face. The normal is +Y and the face's axis is −Y, so α = 180° and sin α = 0. That is not an approximation — every point of the floor really is the lamp's height below it, so the axial depth really is constant, and the derivation is right to ask for no geometric bias at all.

And then the comparison is on a knife edge. The pass stored the depth the rasterizer interpolated; the lookup recomputes it from the fragment's position; the two are the same real number, computed two different ways in float, and they disagree in the last bit roughly half the time. §F saw 90 points of acne appear out of nothing, in a clean square under the lamp. The cure is the term 6.8 called quantisation and set to zero for a 32-bit depth format, because an orthographic bias with the sun at an angle never reached zero to expose it: k_perspective_depth_floor, 2−22. Device depth near 1 is spaced 2−24 apart in a float; both values can be half a spacing off before they meet, and four spacings covers both with room to spare. It is 0.03 mm at 2.5 m on the harness's spot, and 90 became 0.

Why a floor and not an epsilon

"Add a tiny constant" is the folklore version of this fix, and the reason to derive it anyway is that the size is not arbitrary. Too small and the knife edge is back; too large and it is a constant bias again, with every one of §6.3's problems. The floor is sized from the float format and the depth curve near 1, where a local light's depths live — half the codes are spent before w = 0.1 m, so every surface a lamp shadows is in the part of the curve where the spacing is 2−24.

6.6 — What the judge said

Four policies, the same spot light, the same 67,715 judged points (§11 explains "judged"). The ground truth casts a ray from each point to the lamp against the analytic box, sign and ground, and shares no code with the engine. 

  spot light, 512 map        judged     acne     leak
  no bias (the acne)          67715    19502        0
  6.8's formula, as-is        67715        0       50
  derived, per fragment       67715        0        0
  normal offset               67715        0        0

The 50 leaks were harder to find than the acne, and the finding is worth recording because the prediction was refused twice first. §6.3 said 6.8's constant is too large beyond 0.4 m, so it should peter-pan: the shadow should come away from the base of whatever casts it. The first test scene put a solid half-metre crate on the ground and found no leaks. The second stood a 3 cm wall there, and again found none. Both refusals were right. An over-large bias pushes the comparison back, away from the lamp, by a distance that grows with the square of the distance and with the receiver's slope — 2.9 mm for a 45° surface one metre out, several centimetres for the floor meeting a caster at a grazing angle — and a receiver can only leak if the caster in front of it is thinner than that. Behind a solid crate, the floor lies half a metre beyond the crate's lit face along the ray, which no bias of centimetres reaches. Behind the 3 cm wall, the floor is within the bias of the lit face for about 1.3 cm — all of it inside the two-texel band around the wall's base that the judge rightly calls ambiguous (§11.2). A sign 4 mm thick leaves a predicted band of about 3.9 cm, and the third scene found 50 lit points in it where the derived bias finds none.

That is the honest size of 6.8's formula's error on a local light: it is always wrong by d/(n·f), but it is only visible through casters thinner than the over-bias. Leaves, cloth, paper, a fence of thin boards. The derived bias has no such floor on what it can shadow, and costs one call to a function both renderers already have.

7A point's shadow: six cameras and a mirror

7.1 — Six faces

A point light shines everywhere, and a perspective camera cannot see more than a hemisphere even in principle; in practice its texels stretch without bound long before that (§6.2's 1/cos²). The standard answer, and the one the hardware is built for, is a cube map: six square 90° cameras, one down each axis, whose pyramids tile the sphere around the lamp with no gap and no overlap. Lesson 6.15 built cube maps for the sky, by evaluating a function of direction into each face texel. The table that says which direction lands on which texel of which face was written there, once, as k_rules in cubemap.cpp, and verified against the hardware's own lookup: 

{ 2, 1, 0, -1.0f, -1.0f },   // +X : sc = -z, tc = -y
{ 2, 1, 0, +1.0f, -1.0f },   // -X : sc = +z, tc = -y
{ 0, 2, 1, +1.0f, +1.0f },   // +Y : sc = +x, tc = +z
{ 0, 2, 1, +1.0f, -1.0f },   // -Y : sc = +x, tc = -z
{ 0, 1, 2, +1.0f, -1.0f },   // +Z : sc = +x, tc = -y
{ 0, 1, 2, -1.0f, -1.0f },   // -Z : sc = -x, tc = -y

This lesson renders into a cube for the first time, and so needs the same table in the other direction: for each face, which camera draws an image that lands on that face the way the lookup reads it. cube_face_axes turns each row into three vectors — the face's u (the direction that increases the horizontal texture coordinate), v (the vertical one, which grows down the image) and major (the axis the face looks along) — derived from k_rules itself, so the two directions of the table can never disagree. SDL orders the faces +X, −X, +Y, −Y, +Z, −Z, so face f of cube c in an array is layer 6c + f.

7.2 — The camera that is a mirror

The obvious camera for the +X face is look_at(lamp, lamp + (1,0,0), up) with some sensible up vector, and it is wrong in a way that looks right. Work it through with one direction.

Worked example. The direction d = (1, 0.2, −0.5) from the lamp. Its largest component is x, so the hardware reads it from the +X face.

the lookup (k_rules, +X): sc = −z = 0.5, tc = −y = −0.2, ma = 1
texture coordinates          : u = (0.5 + 1)/2 = 0.75, v = (−0.2 + 1)/2 = 0.40

fit_cube_face, rows (u, −v, −major) = ((0,0,−1), (0,1,0), (−1,0,0)):
view space                   : (0.5, 0.2, −1)  → ndc (0.5, 0.2)
viewport, y down             : x = 0.75, y = (1 − 0.2)/2 = 0.40  ✓

look_at's right-hand frame, rows (−u, −v, −major):
view space                   : (−0.5, 0.2, −1) → x = 0.25  ✗ (half a face away)

The two frames differ in the sign of one row. The face's (u, v, major) with v flipped for the image is a left-handed frame — its determinant is −1 — and no rotation, which is all look_at can build, has determinant −1. So the correct camera for a cube face is a reflection, and fit_cube_face builds it from the rows directly.

This is the trap that looks entirely right, and it is worth being precise about why. A mirrored face still contains the scene's depth, drawn from the right place, in the right direction, at the right resolution. Rendered to the screen, each face looks like a perfectly good picture. The error only shows as shadows that land on the wrong side of their casters, mirrored about each face's centre line — which in a busy scene looks like "the shadows are a bit off", not "the image is flipped". §E of the harness measures both frames on all six faces, 1,089 directions each: 

   face  det(ours)  det(look_at)   worst uv err   look_at err
     +X    -1.0000        1.0000       1.19e-07        0.9697
     -X    -1.0000        1.0000       1.64e-07        0.9697
     +Y    -1.0000        1.0000       1.64e-07        0.9697
     -Y    -1.0000        1.0000       1.34e-07        0.9697
     +Z    -1.0000        1.0000       1.34e-07        0.9697
     -Z    -1.0000        1.0000       1.79e-07        0.9697

The mirror lands every direction on the texel the lookup reads, to 1.8 × 10−7 of a face — rounding. look_at misses by up to 0.97 of a face on every one of them. (The mirror reverses triangle winding too. Both shadow passes cull nothing by default, so they do not care — but 6.8's third acne cure, culling front faces, would cull the wrong faces on a cube, and §15 lists it as a pitfall.)

7.3 — Reading it back with no matrix at all

A spot's lookup projects the fragment through the map's clip_from_world, as 6.8's did. A point light's needs none, and this is the elegant part. The hardware picks the face from the direction's largest component; that same component is the distance along the face's axis — w in the face camera's clip space — so the depth the pass stored for this direction is perspective_depth(max(|dx|, |dy|, |dz|), near, far), predicted from the direction alone. The shader passes the direction and the reference depth to one SampleCmpLevelZero on a TextureCubeArray; the CPU asks direction_to_cube for the face and texel, and runs the ordinary map lookup on that face. §E checks the claim: the stored depth is perspective_depth(major axis) to 1.8 × 10−7.

7.4 — The seams

A direction exactly on the 45° seam between two faces has two largest components. The lookup picks one; the other face's camera also contains it, on its edge. With float rounding, a point the lookup assigns to face A can project a hair past −1 on face A's own camera. 6.8's orthographic lookup rejects anything outside [−1, 1] as "not in this map, so lit" — the right answer for a box fitted around the scene, and the wrong one here, where the face was chosen because it owns this direction. The CPU found 54 points on the seams leaking light through a crate's shadow before the rule changed. Under perspective the lookup now clamps to the face instead of rejecting (for a spot the clamp can only touch points outside the fitted square, where the cone is already zero), and §E closes the argument from the other side: of 20,000 random directions, 0 fall outside the frustum of the face that owns them.

7.5 — What a cube costs

A 256² cube is six 256² faces: 393,216 texels against a 512² spot map's 262,144, 1.5 times the texels and six render passes to one. The defaults — 512 for a spot, 256 for a cube face — are a budget, not a law, and they buy different sharpness: a cube face spreads 90° over 256 texels, 7.8 mm per texel at one metre, while a spot with glTF's default 45° cone spreads the same 90° over 512, 3.9 mm. A default point light's shadow is exactly twice as coarse as a default spot's, for 1.5 times the memory. On the CPU, in a release build, the harness's spot map takes 0.72 ms and its six-face cube 0.35 ms, which says the cost is covered texels, not passes: most of a point light's six faces see only a little of the scene. On the GPU, gpu_local_shadows gives each kind its own budget — four spots and two points by default, as a depth Texture2DArray and a depth TextureCubeArray — and a shadowed lamp past its budget is drawn unshadowed with a warning, not dropped (§J: one dropped, slot −1). A spot too wide for a map (§6.2) draws on the point budget, because its shadow is a cube.

8A Module 3 bug, found by a lamp

With the bias of §6 derived and the cube of §7 built, the CPU renderer's spot light still had acne on 88% of the floor, and no amount of bias removed it. The map was checked directly: at a point where the floor sat at device depth 0.987, the map held 0.950. Not a bias problem. The map itself was wrong.

The cause had been in the software rasterizer since Lesson 3.3, and it is a lesson in the difference between finite and small. Near-plane clipping bounds the perspective divide: every vertex that survives has w ≥ near, so x/w is finite. But a vertex on the near plane sits near metres in front of the camera and may sit metres to the side, and its screen coordinate is that sideways distance over near, times the focal length, times half the width of the target. A player camera 1.7 m above a floor with a 0.1 m near plane keeps that within a few thousand pixels, which is why nothing in Modules 3–6 ever noticed. A lamp's camera has a 5 cm near plane over an 8 m floor, and its clipped corners project to about 40,000 pixels.

to_pixel clamps every coordinate to ±8,000, and it was written for a good reason: under near_mode::none — Lesson 3.3's "watch it break" mode — coordinates can be anything, and the clamp keeps the float-to-integer conversion and edge_function's products defined. Under near_mode::clip it was never supposed to fire. When it does, it moves the vertex. The triangle changes shape; the plane of depths across it tilts; every depth interpolated inside the 512-pixel map is wrong, by an amount that depends on how far the corner was dragged. Figure 8 draws it.

@@FIG_GUARD@@

The fix is the one GPUs use, and it is the reason hardware has a guard band at all. Clip against four more planes, placed where the rasterizer's integers stop being exact, so nothing reaching the rasterizer can land outside that range. A clipped vertex is placed on the true edge, by the same interpolation 3.3's near clip uses, so the plane of depths is untouched. The planes are derived in clip space from the viewport map: screen x = vp.x + vp.w(x/w + 1)/2 stays below the band exactly when x/w ≤ hx, and multiplying through by w — positive, because the near clip ran first — gives the plane hxw − x ≥ 0. The band sits at ±7,936 rather than 8,000, leaving 64 pixels for the rounding of a vertex that lands exactly on a guard plane, so the clamp can never fire on geometry that went through the guard clip.

Two properties make the fix safe to put under fifty lessons of pictures.

  • It runs only when it must. outside_guard_band checks the near-clipped polygon first; a triangle that never touched the band takes exactly the path it always took, bit for bit. clip_stats::guard_clipped counts the ones that did.
  • The reference render proves it. §D compares the eight-frame golden render byte for byte: 1,382,416 bytes, 0 differing, E917C06C. No frame in Modules 3 to 6 ever reached the band, so none of them changes; and the harnesses of every later lesson agree (§11).

The polygon bound follows the same argument as 3.3's: a triangle is three vertices, each plane can add at most one, and there are five planes in total — the near plane's one extra (3.3's k_clip_max_vertices = 4) and four guards' — so k_guard_max_vertices is 8.

9Two renderers, two filters, one reach

6.8 sized the bias from the filter's reach: how far, in texels, the farthest sample the filter reads can be from the point being shaded. With the lamps in place, the two renderers turned out to need different reaches for different reasons — and to end at the same number.

9.1 — The GPU reads four texels

The CPU's lookup compares against the nearest texel, whose sample is at most half a texel away along each axis: a reach of (r + ½)√2 for a PCF radius r, 6.8's pcf_reach_texels. The GPU's one tap is SampleCmpLevelZero through a linear comparison sampler, which compares four texels and blends the results — hardware 2×2 PCF — and any of those four can carry weight while a whole texel away along each axis. Sized for the CPU's reach, the GPU showed acne across both pools: 11,098 pixels on which the renderers disagreed, none near a shadow edge. With (r + 1)√2, zero.

Which raised an uncomfortable question about the sun. 6.8's GPU path samples through the same kind of sampler and sized its bias with the CPU's pcf_reach_texels — with its default 3×3 kernel, 2.12 texel diagonals where 2.83 are reachable. The first version of this page left it there, "a quarter short", as a pending correction. Then it was measured, the way that cannot be argued with: a bare ground under the sun and nothing else in the map. Nothing can shadow a plane under a directional light, so every pixel darker than the unshadowed frame is acne. §K of the harness:

Both halves of that table matter. With one tap the shortfall is enormous — 27–36% of a bare ground is acne at every elevation, where the CPU's picture of the same scene is clean. With the default 3×3 kernel the old reach shows nothing, at any elevation. That is why the sun carried the CPU's number from 6.8 to here: every picture anyone looked at used the default. The "quarter short" was true arithmetic that predicted a symptom the default configuration does not have, which is exactly the kind of claim that needs a measurement before it goes in a sentence. The fix is one named function, gpu_pcf_reach_texels, beside the GPU shadow code, and fill_uniforms now writes it. Rerunning the harnesses of 6.1 to 6.18 and 7.1 to 7.8 against the fixed engine moves no number but timings: the enlarged bias removes acne that only a one-tap sun had, and no harness had measured one.

The same rig found something larger on the way, and it is the better lesson of the two. The sun's lookup reads its texel size and depth range from the cascade block, since 6.9. A caller with one map and no cascades — fill_uniforms and then render(…, shadow, sampler), which is exactly how demos/sandbox draws — gets the block gpu_scene_renderer pushes in their place, and that fallback held 1.0 for both. A one-metre texel over a one-unit depth range makes the slope-scaled bias larger than the whole depth range, so every comparison passes and every shadow vanishes. §L of the harness measured it: a box whose shadow covers 528, 792 and 1,240 pixels at three sun elevations with a one-cascade block covered 0 at all three without one. Nothing had failed, because nothing rendered that path and looked; a missing shadow is not an error message. The fallback now copies the single map's own numbers, which fill_uniforms had been writing into the light block all along, and the two pictures are identical to the channel.

9.2 — The CPU snaps its vertices

Then a bare floor under a bulb, with nothing at all to cast a shadow, came out striped along one cube face on the CPU — Figure 9, 713 points of acne out of 128,122. Nothing casts there; every dark mark is the map disagreeing with itself. The cause is Lesson 2.2's integer edge functions: the rasterizer snaps every vertex to a whole pixel before it fills. Snapping moves a triangle's corners by up to half a pixel, and so slides its plane of depths sideways by up to half a texel's worth of slope. A small triangle barely notices. A floor clipped to the guard band is thousands of pixels across, and a sub-pixel shift at a corner 7,936 pixels away is a real tilt across the map. The harness measured a stored depth 0.36 of a texel's depth step nearer than the analytic floor — 1.19 × 10−4 in device units.

So the CPU's perspective reach grows by half a texel diagonal, 0.7071: half a texel from the nearest sample, half from the snap, (r + 1)√2. 713 became 0; in gltf_view's scene, 1,895 became 0. The 713 is measured by running the same code with its slope scale halved, which is exactly 6.8's reach again (½√2 of a texel instead of √2), so the only thing that differs between the two panels of Figure 9 is the half texel — and the zero is evidence that the reach did it rather than that the floor stopped being tested.

The two renderers now apply the same reach for two different reasons, and both comments say which reason is theirs. That matters for the next person to touch either: "the GPU and CPU have the same constant" invites someone to share it, and the day the GPU gets a nearest-texel sampler or the CPU gets sub-pixel precision, a shared constant would be wrong for one of them without anything changing in the file that defines it.
