Module 8 — Physics · Lesson 8.10

Sequential Impulses: Warm Starting, Islands, and Sleeping

Lesson 8.9 ended on a confession. One pass of impulses over a four-point manifold does not hold a crate up: it leaves 2.6 × 10⁻² m/s of residual approach velocity, the crate sinks 275 mm in fifteen seconds, and it never stops. That was one of two failures, and the honest half of the confession was that they are different problems needing different machinery. This lesson builds both, and then the two things that make the result affordable. Iteration is the first: sweep every contact again, against the velocities the other contacts have since produced, and the residual falls by a measured factor of 0.53 per sweep — geometric, constant over four decades, until it hits the float floor. Warm starting is what makes a handful of sweeps enough instead of hundreds, and it is already built: 8.7 gave every contact a feature id, 8.9's write_back puts the impulse in the manifold, and this lesson closes the loop by reading it back. A settled five-crate tower reaches a residual of 10⁻⁴ in 89 iterations warm and has not reached it in 400 cold; at the eight iterations this engine ships, the cold tower collapses — 2,020 mm of sink — where the warm one holds at 40. Closing that loop also exposed a bug three lessons old, and it is the best kind: the two cached friction impulses are coordinates in a basis, the basis was never stored with them, and tangent_basis re-chooses it every frame by comparing two components of the normal that are both around 10⁻⁵. They cross constantly. Measured: 0.96% of manifold-frames rotate by more than thirty degrees, worst case 134.6, and last frame's friction is applied sideways. Then the second failure, which no number of velocity iterations can touch, because a converged solve says the overlap stops growing and nothing at all about the overlap that is already there. §6 sets out to prove that arrival depth is exactly one step of travel and the measurement refuses it: it is a ceiling, and the depth is uniform on [0, v·h] with a mean of 0.49. Two corrections fix it and one of them adds energy — a crate spawned 200 mm inside the floor is thrown 99 mm clear of it by Baumgarte and settles quietly at the slop under split impulse, for the same time constant and 46% of a velocity iteration. Then islands, whose whole content is one rule: a body that cannot move is not a bridge. Break that rule and a yard of twenty separate towers becomes one island, which is not a slow simulation — it is no sleeping at all. And sleeping, which takes a settled yard's solve from 131 µs to 3, with two findings that are not in the textbooks: the sleep test cannot run before the solve, because every resting body in the scene is travelling at g·h then (0 of 100 quiet, against 96 of 100 after), and sleeping has to be decided per island, because a body slept on its own hangs in the air when the crate under it is knocked away. Along the way body_world::step is split in two so that a contact solve has somewhere to go — checked bit-for-bit against the function it replaced, 400 bodies over 120 steps — and the lesson ends where it must: eight sweeps hold a five-crate tower and do not hold a ten-crate one, which is the honest capability of a Gauss–Seidel solver and the reason Lesson 8.11 writes a constraint as a Jacobian.

1. The problem: one pass, and two different failures

Here is where Lesson 8.9 left the engine. A crate falls onto a floor. The broadphase reports the pair, GJK and EPA say how deep and which way out, the manifold gives four contact points with ids that survive across frames, and solve_contacts applies an impulse at each of them that leaves the relative normal velocity exactly on target. Every stage works. The crate lands — and then it sinks, 275 mm in fifteen seconds, a quarter of its own height, and it never stops.

8.9 §12 diagnosed that carefully enough to hand this lesson two separate jobs, and the most useful thing you can do before writing a line of code is to keep them separate:

  1. One pass is not a converged solve. The four impulses are applied one after another, and each one disturbs the velocity the previous one just set. What is left over is 2.6 × 10⁻² m/s of approach speed — small, but it is a speed, so it accumulates into a distance without limit.
  2. The overlap the contact arrived with does not go away. Iterate the same solve thirty-two times and the residual vanishes — 9.9 × 10⁻⁹ — and the crate stops sinking. It stops at 7.2 mm inside the floor, which is where it was on the frame the contact was first detected, and no number of velocity iterations moves it by a micron.

Those are not two symptoms of one bug. They are a velocity error and a position error, and every equation in solver.hpp so far has been about velocity. §3 and §4 are about the first; §6, §7 and §8 are about the second. Conflating them is how people end up tuning an iteration count to fix a penetration, which does not work and cannot work, and §6 measures exactly how much it does not work.

And then there is a third thing, which is not a failure of the physics but of the shape of the program. body_world::step integrates velocities and positions in one call, so there is nowhere for a contact solve to go except after the body has already moved. §11 measures what that costs and the answer is a closed form: g·h² per resting step, forever, which at 60 Hz is 2.725 mm and puts a crate through a half-metre floor in four seconds.

2. Building intuition: a stack is a system

Start with the smallest interesting case, which is not two crates but one. A crate resting flat on a floor has a four-point manifold, and 8.9's solve walks those four points in turn. Point 1's impulse is computed to make the relative normal velocity at point 1 exactly zero, and it does. Then point 2's impulse is computed the same way — and it changes the crate's velocity, and its angular velocity, and therefore the velocity at point 1, which is no longer zero.

That is not an implementation mistake and there is no clever ordering that avoids it. The four impulses are coupled: any one of them moves the velocity at all four points, because they all act on one rigid body. What the four constraints ask for together is a system, and solving each one in isolation is not solving the system.

Worked, with real numbers. Take a 10 kg cube, half-extent 0.25 m, resting flat. Its inertia tensor about each axis is m(w² + h²)/12 = 10 × (0.25 + 0.25)/12 = 0.4167 kg·m². A contact at a corner has a lever arm of r = (±0.25, −0.25, ±0.25), so w = r × n with n = (0, 1, 0) is (−0.25, 0, 0.25) at one corner: length 0.3536. The angular term in the effective mass is w·(I⁻¹w) = 0.125 / 0.4167 = 0.300, against a linear term of 1/m = 0.1. So three quarters of the effect of an impulse at a corner is rotation — and rotation is exactly what is shared between the four points. An impulse at one corner lifts the opposite corner. That is the coupling, in one number.

Gauss–Seidel, and why sweeping again converges

The fix is the oldest idea in numerical linear algebra. Solve each constraint in turn, using the current velocities — which already include everything the earlier constraints in this sweep have done — and then sweep the whole set again. Each sweep leaves each constraint satisfied at the moment it was visited and slightly violated afterwards, but less violated than the sweep before, because the corrections compose rather than compete.

That is Gauss–Seidel, and the reason it converges here is worth stating precisely, because it is also the reason it converges slowly on a tall stack. Write the whole system as A j = b, where j is the vector of contact impulses, b is the vector of velocity errors to remove, and A is the matrix whose entry A[i][k] is "how much does a unit impulse at contact k change the relative velocity at contact i". The diagonal of A is the effective mass 8.9 derived — that is the same quantity, seen as one entry of a matrix. A Gauss–Seidel sweep is one pass of

\[ j_i \;\leftarrow\; j_i + \frac{1}{A_{ii}}\Bigl(b_i - \sum_k A_{ik}\,j_k\Bigr) \] j_i += (b_i - sum over k of A[i][k]*j_k) / A[i][i]

and the error after a sweep is the error before it multiplied by a matrix that depends only on A. So the error falls geometrically, by a constant factor per sweep, and that factor is the one number you actually want to know about a solver. §3 measures it.

We never build A, and that is the whole trick. The sum Σ A[i][k] j_k is "the relative velocity at contact i right now", which is three dot products on state the bodies already carry — so a sweep costs O(contacts) and needs no matrix, no allocation, and no knowledge of how the contacts are connected. That is why this shape scales to thousands of contacts where a direct solve would not, and it is also why it inherits Gauss–Seidel's weakness: information travels one contact per sweep.

There is one honest complication, and it is what makes this a solver rather than a linear solve. A contact may push and may not pull, so the true problem is not A j = b but A j ≥ b with j ≥ 0 and complementarity between them — a linear complementarity problem. Sequential impulses handle it with the max(0, …) 8.9 already wrote: each sweep solves each constraint as an equality and then projects the accumulated impulse back onto the admissible set. That is projected Gauss–Seidel, it is what every game physics engine of the last twenty years does, and it converges for the same reason the unconstrained version does. It is also why 8.9's insistence on clamping the accumulated impulse rather than the increment was not pedantry: the projection is onto the total, and at one pass the two happen to agree.
