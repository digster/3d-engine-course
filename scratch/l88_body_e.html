15.1 What you should see

The demo opens on a scene of 256 crates seen from directly above, with the grid drawn at the cell size in use. Each cell is tinted by how much work its pair loop is doing — the shade is keyed on C(n, 2) rather than on the occupancy, because the work is quadratic and the picture is meant to show where the work is. Candidate pairs are drawn as amber lines between proxy centres. On the right, the counters.

Four things to do with it, in order.

Leave [B] on and watch the “agree” line. It runs brute_force_pairs beside the grid every frame and colours the verdict. That is the contract, checked live, sixty times a second, against a function that cannot be wrong.

Hold [Up], then [Down]. Entries move by orders of magnitude, bucket tests move the other way, the milliseconds trace §7's cost curve — and the PAIRS line does not move at all. That is the whole of §7.3 in one gesture.

Press [3] for the crowd preset. The heat map concentrates: a few cells glowing while most of the lattice stays dark, and the pair count climbing far faster than the body count. That is why the cost model's second term grows with density.

Press [T], then [Y]. A ground plate appears, the entry count jumps by more than the entire rest of the scene, and the milliseconds follow. [Y] turns the guard back on and the number falls back to where it was — with the pair list unchanged. That is §10, watchable.

A headless run for a reproducible frame:

Preset 4 is “mixed sizes”, and it is the one to look at: one proxy in seventeen is several metres across, and each of those produces a visible star of candidate pairs. That picture is §10's argument in miniature — the cost of a broadphase is dominated by its largest objects, long before any of them is large enough to trip the guard.
