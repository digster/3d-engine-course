Module 6 — Advanced Rendering · Lesson 6.17b

Local Lights: Point and Spot, and Their Shadows

Every light this engine has ever drawn is the sun: infinitely far away, arriving from one direction at one strength everywhere. A desk lamp, a torch on a wall and a car's headlight are all somewhere, and being somewhere changes two facts at once — the direction of the light varies across the scene, and its strength falls with the square of the distance. This lesson adds point and spot lights to both renderers, derives the inverse-square law from a sphere and measures it, compares the three published ways of forcing a light to zero at its range, and ramps a spot's cone in cosine. Then it gives both kinds of lamp a shadow: a perspective map for a spot and six mirrored cameras for a point. That is where the lesson stops being a port. 6.8's bias turns out to be the on-axis special case of a formula it never needed; a cube map rendered with look_at is the mirror image of the one the hardware reads; and a bug that has been in the CPU rasterizer since Module 3 — a clamp that quietly moves vertices — surfaces the first time a camera with a five-centimetre near plane looks across a floor. Every claim is judged against a ray-cast ground truth that shares no code with the engine, the two renderers agree to within one pixel of a shadow edge, and twenty-five other lessons' harnesses produce the same results they did before a line of this lesson existed.

1The problem: one light, and it is the sun

engine::lighting has held exactly one light since Lesson 3.6, and its name says what it is: directional_light. A direction of travel, a colour, and — since 6.2 — an irradiance: the power landing on each square metre of a surface held square-on to the beam. Everything Module 6 built on top of it is correct for that one kind of light. The microfacet BRDF of 6.4 does not care where the light came from; the shadow map of 6.8 was fitted to a box because the sun has no position to put a camera at; the cascades of 6.9 split the camera's frustum because the sun is the same everywhere and only the viewer's need for detail varies.

Now put a lamp on a desk. Try to fake it with what the engine has, and the failure is not subtle. A directional light pointed from the lamp toward the desk lights the desk — and it lights the floor, the far wall and the ceiling behind the lamp with the same irradiance, from the same direction, because a directional light is by definition the same everywhere. There is no pool of light. There is no falloff. The object under the lamp and the object across the room are lit identically, and the shadow of the lamp's own stand falls in parallel lines like the shadow of a building at noon. You have drawn a small sun.

What is missing is two facts, and each is a part of this lesson.

  • The direction varies. The light arrives at each surface point along the line from the lamp to that point, so l stops being a constant and becomes normalise(position − p), different at every fragment.
  • The strength falls with distance. A lamp's light spreads out as it travels, and the irradiance a surface receives falls with the square of its distance from the lamp. This is not a tuning curve; §3 derives it from the area of a sphere and then measures it.

And then the parts that are not about light at all, which are where most of the work turns out to be.

  • A scene has many lamps, and the shader must be handed all of them. Lesson 4.6 measured (and the post-Module 8 review corrected the explanation of) how SDL copies a uniform push into a 32 KiB block of which the Vulkan backend binds 4 KiB. A light list whose length the scene decides cannot live in a push. This lesson gives the fragment stage its first storage buffer.
  • A lamp needs a shadow, and 6.8's shadow was built for the sun. Its bias derivation leaned, in one place, on the camera being orthographic — every ray parallel, every texel the same size, device depth a linear function of distance. A spot light's camera is a pyramid and a point light's is six of them. §6 and §7 find out which of 6.8's results survive that, and one of them does not.

There is also something this lesson did not set out to find. It is in §8, and it is a bug that has been in the software rasterizer since Lesson 3.3 — invisible for fifty lessons because no camera in the course had ever looked the way a lamp looks.

2Building intuition: a point that pours light over spheres

Picture the lamp as a single point, pouring out power in every direction at a steady rate. Now draw a sphere around it of radius one metre. Every bit of the power the lamp emits crosses that sphere — there is nowhere else for it to go. Draw a second sphere at two metres. The same power crosses it too, but that sphere has four times the area, because a sphere's area grows with the square of its radius. The same power over four times the area is a quarter of the power per square metre. Figure 1 draws exactly this: a narrow beam of rays cuts a patch from each sphere, and the far patch has twice the width in each direction and therefore four times the area.

@@FIG_SPHERES@@

That is the whole of the inverse-square law, and you should notice what it is not. It is not a model of fog, or of the lamp getting tired, or of light "weakening" as it travels. Nothing is lost in empty space. The light is simply spread thinner, the way paint spread over a larger wall is a thinner coat. That framing matters, because it tells you which parts of the shading equation change. The BRDF — what a surface does with the light that reaches it — is untouched. Only the part that says how much light arrives gets a new factor.

A spot light is the same point with a hood over it. The hood blocks the light outside a cone and lets it through inside; it does not concentrate anything. Seen that way a spot light is a point light multiplied by a mask, and every result about point lights carries over with one extra factor. §5 builds the mask, and discusses the other way to see it — a hood that focuses — and why this engine, like Frostbite and like glTF's specification, chose not to.

And a shadow for either is 6.8's idea — render the scene from the light, keep the depth, compare — with a camera that is no longer a box. A spot light's camera is a pyramid down its axis. A point light sees the whole sphere, and no single camera can: six cameras with 90-degree fields of view, one per face of a cube, tile the sphere with no gap and no overlap. 6.15 built cube maps for the sky, by evaluating a function of direction into each face. This lesson is the first time the engine has to render into one, and §7 is about the one way that goes wrong that looks entirely right.

3The inverse-square law, derived and measured

3.1 — From the sphere to the formula

Call the lamp's total emitted power Φ (the radiometric "flux", in watts). If it emits equally in every direction, then a sphere of radius d centred on it — area 4πd² — receives all of it spread evenly, so the power per unit area on the sphere is

\[ E(d) = \frac{\Phi}{4\pi d^{2}} \] E(d) = Phi / (4 * pi * d^2)

That quantity — power per unit area on a surface held square-on to the light — is irradiance, the same E⊥ Lesson 6.2 gave the sun. The constant part, Φ/4π, is the power per unit solid angle leaving the lamp: its intensity I, in watts per steradian (a whole sphere of directions is 4π steradians, so dividing the total by 4π shares it out). With that name the law is

\[ E_{\perp}(d) = \frac{I}{d^{2}}, \qquad E(d) = \frac{I}{d^{2}}\,\cos\theta \] E_perp(d) = I / d^2 ; on a tilted surface, E(d) = I / d^2 * cos(theta)

and the cosine is Lesson 3.6's, unchanged: a surface tilted θ away from the direction to the lamp intercepts less of the beam, by exactly cos θ. So a local light enters the shading equation in the one place 6.2 cleared for it. Where the sun contributed E_perp · cos θ, a lamp contributes (I / d²) · cos θ, and the BRDF multiplying it is the same function.

3.2 — What the engine's intensity means

The engine stores I, not Φ, and it chooses the unit so that a lamp is directly comparable with the sun: local_light::intensity is the irradiance the lamp delivers at one metre, square-on. That is literally what I/d² means at d = 1. It inherits 6.2's exposure convention for free. The sun's default irradiance is k_reference_irradiance = π, chosen so a white Lambertian surface facing it renders at exactly 1.0; a lamp of intensity π renders that same surface at 1.0 from one metre away, and at a quarter of that from two.

Worked example. A white surface (albedo 1) faces a lamp of intensity π, and we use the pure Lambert BRDF, albedo/π, so the rendered radiance is (1/π) · E.

d = 1 m:  E = π/1  = 3.14159  →  L = 1.0000
d = 2 m:  E = π/4  = 0.78540  →  L = 0.2500
d = 4 m:  E = π/16 = 0.19635  →  L = 0.0625

verify_617b §A pushes these three through shade_local and gets 1.0000000, 0.250000 and 0.062500 — to the bit, because every factor here is exact in float. The first line is the one to hold on to: at one metre, a lamp of intensity π is the engine's sun, and the rest of the lesson can compare the two without a conversion.

glTF's KHR_lights_punctual extension specifies point and spot intensity in candela — lumens per steradian — which is I's photometric twin: the same shape of quantity, weighted by the eye's response. Its directional lights are in lux, which is E's twin. So the engine's two light types sit in exactly the relationship glTF's two do, and an importer converts both with one exposure factor (Exercise 6.17b.2).

3.3 — Measuring it rather than believing it

The derivation made one claim that can be checked directly: the same power crosses every sphere. So integrate. verify_617b §A walks spheres of radius 0.5, 2 and 8 metres on a 400×800 grid of latitude and longitude, evaluates sample_local_light at every point, and adds up irradiance × area. If the attenuation really is 1/d² every sphere collects 4πI, which for I = π is 39.47842. 

flux through a sphere around the lamp (should be 4*pi*I = 39.47842):
  r =  0.5 m   flux 39.47852   relative error 2.60e-06
  r =  2.0 m   flux 39.47852   relative error 2.60e-06
  r =  8.0 m   flux 39.47852   relative error 2.60e-06

Identical to the last digit across a sixteen-fold change in radius, and 2.6 × 10−6 from the exact value — which is the midpoint rule's own error on that grid, the same for every radius because the integrand is the same after the d² cancels. That cancellation is the law. A wrong exponent — 1/d, say, which shows up in hand-rolled lighting more often than you would think — would make the three numbers differ by factors of four.

3.4 — The singularity, and the size of a light bulb

I/d² is infinite at d = 0, and a surface can touch a lamp. The infinity is not physics: a real bulb has a size, and nothing gets inside it. The honest cure is to give the point a size, and Frostbite's course notes do exactly this: a punctual light is treated as having a physical size of one centimetre, and the squared distance is clamped below at 0.01² (Lagarde and de Rousiers 2014, §4.7 and Listing 4). The engine does the same:

\[ \text{inverse\_square}(d^{2}) = \frac{1}{\max(d^{2},\ 0.01^{2})} \] inverse_square(d_squared) = 1 / max(d_squared, 0.01^2)

Measured: at 5 mm the factor is 10,000 instead of the unclamped 40,000; at 2 cm it is 2,500, exactly 1/d². Beyond one centimetre the clamp changes nothing at all.

Unreal's answer is different and worth knowing because you will read it: Karis's 2013 notes use 1/(d² + 1) (eq. 9), which removes the singularity by changing the curve everywhere. §4 measures what that costs; the short version is that at one metre it delivers half the light the inverse square says it should.

3.5 — A far lamp is a sun, and by how much

If a point light is moved far away and its intensity scaled up by the square of the distance, it should become indistinguishable from a directional light. That is a good check of the whole construction, and it also says precisely when you may replace a distant lamp with the sun. Put the lamp D metres above the centre of a one-metre square patch, with intensity E·D². Directly beneath it the irradiance is exactly E. At a corner of the patch, r = 0.7071 m off axis, the distance is √(D² + r²) and the cosine is D/√(D² + r²), so the irradiance is E · (D/d)³ — distance and cosine together. 

   D (m)  worst deviation (D/d)^3 predicts
       2         16.1947%         16.1948%
      10          0.7453%          0.7453%
     100          0.0075%          0.0075%

The harness measures the formula exactly. The practical reading: at ten times the size of what it lights, a lamp is a sun to three-quarters of a percent, and at a hundred times, to a hundredth of that. A street light over a car park is a local light; the moon is not.

4Range: forcing zero without drawing a ring

4.1 — Why a light that never reaches zero is a problem

The inverse square never reaches zero. A lamp in the kitchen contributes a tiny, nonzero amount of light to a surface in the garage, and a shader that evaluates every lamp for every fragment will dutifully compute it. That is the cost problem: with N lamps every pixel pays N BRDF evaluations, including all the ones that add a millionth of a code. Real engines solve it by culling — deciding per tile of the screen which lamps can reach it — and culling needs every lamp to have a finite reach. So each lamp gets a range, a distance beyond which its contribution is defined to be zero. glTF's KHR_lights_punctual makes the same promise: when a light gives a range, its contribution beyond that distance is to be treated as zero.

The question is how to get from I/d² to zero at the range without the picture showing where it happened.

4.2 — Three published answers

A hard cutoff draws a visible circle on every surface a lamp reaches — a step in brightness, which the eye finds instantly. So all three published answers multiply the inverse square by a window, a function of x = d/range that is 1 near the lamp and 0 at the range:

  • glTF's recommended recipe: clamp(1 − x⁴, 0, 1) / d². The specification's README gives exactly this line.
  • Karis's window, adopted by Frostbite: saturate(1 − x⁴)² / d² — the same quartic, squared. Frostbite's notes adopt it "with value n = 4 … as presented by Karis" (eq. 26) and apply it to every punctual and area light.
  • Unreal's, from the same Karis notes: the squared window over d² + 1 instead of d², which folds the singularity cure into the curve.

The engine uses the second, with Frostbite's 1 cm clamp from §3.4 rather than Unreal's +1. Figure 2 shows why, and the next two sections measure it.

4.3 — What the window costs, and where it spends it

The fourth power keeps the window close to 1 near the lamp and spends its descent in the outer half of the range: 

       x  Karis/Frostbite    glTF recipe
   0.250         0.992203       0.996094
   0.333         0.975461       0.987654
   0.500         0.878906       0.937500
   0.700         0.577448       0.759900
   0.900         0.118267       0.343900
   0.990         0.001553       0.039404
   1.000         0.000000       0.000000

Be honest about what that means: at half its range, a lamp windowed this way delivers 12% less light than the inverse square says. That is a real change to the picture, and it is why a range is chosen generously — the window bends the part of the curve where the light is already a quarter of what it was at half the range, and dimming the dimmest part of a curve is the only place bending it is cheap. The first draft of this lesson's header comment said the window was "0.99 at half the range"; the harness says 0.879, and the comment was corrected before anything shipped.

4.4 — The edge: a crease is a ring

Why square the quartic? Look at what each window does in the last few centimetres before the range, which is the right-hand plot of Figure 2. The quantity that matters is the slope of the light just inside the edge, because the eye's edge detectors respond to a change of slope as readily as to a change of value — that is the machinery behind Mach bands, the bright and dark fringes you see beside a gradient that suddenly stops. 

dE/dd just inside the range, per unit intensity (r = 10 m):
  inverse square alone     -0.002000   (-2/r^3)
  glTF recipe              -0.004000   (-4/r^3: twice as steep, then 0)
  Karis / Frostbite        -0.000000   (0: no crease)
[PASS] glTF's edge falls 2.000x as steeply as the inverse square; the squared window's -4.8e-07

The derivation is two lines. With w(x) = 1 − x⁴, the product E = I·w/d² has derivative I·(w′/r)/d² + I·w·(−2/d³); at the range w = 0 and w′ = −4, so the slope is −4I/r³ — twice the inverse square's own slope, and then zero the instant after. That jump is a crease, and on a large floor it draws a faint ring at the range. Squaring the window makes w′ at the edge 2w·(−4x³) = 0, and the light meets zero tangentially. The harness's finite difference, taken a millimetre inside the edge, agrees: 2.000 times the inverse square's slope for glTF's recipe, and −4.8 × 10−7 — zero to within the step — for the squared window.

Note

Unreal's +1, measured as a fraction of the true inverse square: 0.20 at half a metre, 0.50 at one metre, 0.80 at two, 0.96 at five, 0.99 at ten. Karis's notes are candid that the +1 is a practical choice rather than a physical one, and suggest exposing it to artists where physical correctness is not wanted. The 1 cm clamp measures exactly 1.0000 at every one of those distances. The +1 is also a statement in metres — it assumes the world's unit is a metre — which is one more reason to prefer a clamp whose constant has a physical meaning.

5The spot: a mask, ramped in cosine

5.1 — Two angles, and what they mean

A spot light keeps everything a point light has and adds an axis — direction, the direction the light travels, the same convention directional_light pinned in capitals in Lesson 3.6 — and two half-angles measured from that axis. Inside the inner angle the lamp is at full intensity; outside the outer angle it is dark; between them it falls smoothly. These are glTF's innerConeAngle and outerConeAngle, in radians, with glTF's defaults of 0 and π/4, so a light imported with no spot block means here what it meant to the tool that exported it.

5.2 — Why cosine and not angle

The obvious ramp is linear in angle: compute the angle between the axis and the ray to the surface, and interpolate from inner to outer. It needs the angle, and a fragment does not have it — it has vectors. The dot product of the axis with the unit vector from the lamp to the surface is the cosine of that angle for the price of three multiplies, and recovering the angle would cost an acos per lamp per fragment. So the ramp is done in cosine, in the form glTF's reference code gives, with the two constants computed once per lamp on the CPU:

\[ s = \frac{1}{\max(0.001,\ \cos\theta_i - \cos\theta_o)}, \quad o = -\cos\theta_o \, s, \quad \text{cone} = \operatorname{saturate}(\cos\theta \cdot s + o)^{2} \] scale = 1 / max(0.001, cos(inner) - cos(outer)); offset = -cos(outer) * scale; cone = saturate(cos(theta) * scale + offset)^2

The max keeps a hard-edged cone (inner equal to outer) from dividing by zero; the square is the range window's trick again — the ramp reaches zero with zero slope, so the edge of the pool is soft rather than creased. glTF's reference code squares the ramp for the same reason: a falloff that drops quickly past the inner cone and then eases into darkness.

@@FIG_CONE@@

Cosine is not linear in angle, so this ramp is not the linear-in-angle one, and Figure 3 plots both. Worked through for the harness's cone (inner 0.30 rad, outer 0.45):

Worked example. The angular midpoint, 0.375 rad.

cos 0.300 = 0.955336   cos 0.450 = 0.900447   cos 0.375 = 0.930508
scale = 1 / (0.955336 − 0.900447) = 18.2185
offset = −0.900447 × 18.2185 = −16.4048
t = 0.930508 × 18.2185 − 16.4048 = 0.5477  →  t² = 0.2999

Halfway in angle, the cosine ramp has fallen to 0.2999, where the linear-in-angle ramp would give 0.25. The harness prints 0.29993. The difference is small and systematic — the cosine ramp holds its light a little longer, then falls a little faster — and it is the price of never calling acos, which is a price worth paying.

5.3 — A mask, not a lens

There is a second way to think of a spot light, and it matters because it changes what the intensity field means. Give the lamp a fixed total power Φ and let the hood focus it: all of it leaves through the cone, whose solid angle is 2π(1 − cos θo), so the intensity inside is Φ over that. Narrow the cone and the pool gets brighter. That is how a real reflector behaves, and Frostbite's notes derive it (eq. 16) before deliberately setting it aside: coupling brightness to the cone makes spot lights harder for artists to control, because narrowing a cone to light fewer pixels would also brighten them. Instead the reflector is treated as absorbing the light outside the cone, "acting like a simple masking" (eq. 17), and glTF's specification makes the same choice by defining a spot's intensity as the candela inside the cone.

The engine follows both, which is why the struct stores I directly and why §C of the harness checks that on its axis a spot light is its point light exactly — 0.109318 against 0.109318. The solid angles are still worth having in your head, because Exercise 6.17b.1 converts between the two conventions: 

 15.0 deg   0.2141 sr    1.70% of the sphere
 30.0 deg   0.8418 sr    6.70% of the sphere
 45.0 deg   1.8403 sr   14.64% of the sphere
 90.0 deg   6.2832 sr   50.00% of the sphere

glTF's default cone, 45 degrees, masks 85% of the sphere. A focusing lamp of the same power would be 6.8 times brighter inside it; a masking one is exactly as bright as the bare bulb.
