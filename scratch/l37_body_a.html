Specular and Blinn-Phong

Everything the engine has drawn so far looks the same from every angle — and Lesson 3.6 did not merely claim that, it measured it. Today the shading learns where you are standing, and surfaces stop being made of plaster.

1The Problem

Build the demo from Lesson 3.6, put the torus on screen, and hold one of the arrow keys. The camera swings around the object; the object turns in your view; and the shading does not change. Not "changes subtly". Does not change.

That was not an accident, and 3.6 did not ask you to take it on faith. Take one fixed point on a surface, one fixed light, and shade it from two different camera positions:

Zero. Not "small". The two numbers are the same float. Lambert's law is a statement about two directions — where the surface faces and where the light is — and the viewer appears nowhere in it, so moving the viewer cannot possibly change the answer.

For a matte surface that is exactly right. Chalk, unfinished wood, a dusty wall, a sheet of paper: these really do look about the same from anywhere, because their microscopic surface is so disordered that light arriving from one direction leaves in all directions more or less equally. Lambert is the mathematical statement of "equally in all directions", and it is a good model of those things.

It is a poor model of almost everything else. A billiard ball, a wet stone, a car bonnet, a kettle, an apple, a varnished floor, an eye, skin — all of these have a sheen: a bright patch that sits in one place on the surface and slides across it as you move your head. That patch is the single most reliable cue your visual system has for "this thing is smooth and hard", and the engine currently cannot draw it at all.

The failure mode

Everything in the scene is made of the same material. Not "made of different colours of the same material" — the colours differ, but the substance reads identically: a chalky, self-lit plaster. A metal slab and a plastic ball are distinguishable only by hue. This is the most common reason a first renderer looks like a first renderer, and it is not a matter of tuning: no choice of albedo can make a matte surface look wet.

Measured on the torus, one light, one camera: turning the highlight on changes 3,182 of the 6,346 covered pixels — 50.1% of the object — with a worst channel difference of 165 out of 255. The brightest pixel goes from 229 to a clipped 255. Half of what you were looking at was wrong, and it was wrong in the half that carries the material.

So the shading has to learn something it has never known: where the eye is. Every term the engine has computed so far could be evaluated without knowing that, and this one cannot. That is not a small addition — it changes the shape of the vertex pipeline, as §4.5 will show — and it is worth noticing the moment it happens.

2Building Intuition

2.1 A mirror, and then a slightly rough one

Start from the extreme case, because the extreme case is the one you already understand completely.

A perfect mirror takes light arriving from one direction and sends all of it out along exactly one other direction. Stand anywhere else and you see nothing of that light at all; stand in exactly the right place and you see the lamp itself, at full brightness. There is no falloff and no in-between: the answer is all or nothing, and the boundary between them is infinitely sharp.

Now rough the mirror up very slightly — sandpaper it, or breathe on it. Under a microscope the flat surface has become a landscape of tiny facets, each still a perfect mirror but each tilted a little differently. Light arriving from one direction now leaves in a spray of directions clustered around where the flat mirror would have sent it, because most facets are tilted only a little and a few are tilted a lot.

That is the whole picture, and everything else in this lesson is bookkeeping on it:

  • There is one special direction — call it R, the direction a perfect mirror would use.
  • The surface returns the most light along R, and less the further you are from it.
  • How quickly "less" sets in is what we mean by glossiness. A rougher surface spreads the spray wider; a smoother one narrows it toward the mirror's single direction.

Figure 1 draws the spray as an outline: the distance from the surface point out to the curve is how much light leaves in that direction. Notice that the shape has no physical thickness — it is a plot, not an object — and that it is fattest along R.

@@FIG1@@

Read the three sample directions off it. An eye at A, exactly along R, gets the full 1.000. Move 22° off and it gets 0.404; move 44° off and it gets 0.019. The falloff is brutal, and the brutality is the point: a highlight is small because a smooth surface's spray is narrow.

Those linear numbers are worth converting, because Lesson 1.6 has a say here. Encoded for a screen they are codes 255, 170 and 38 out of 255. Note the third one: a value that is under 2% of full brightness is not invisible — it is a clearly discernible dark grey, because sRGB spends most of its 256 codes at the dark end. The tail of a specular lobe is more visible than its linear value suggests, which is exactly why the difference between the two models in §3.5 shows up at all.

This is a real model of a real thing, not a hack

The tilted-facet story is not a teaching analogy invented for this lesson. It is microfacet theory, and it is what Module 6's physically based renderer is built on — the difference being that Module 6 asks how many facets are tilted each way (a distribution function), how much they shadow each other, and how their reflectivity varies with angle. Blinn-Phong is what you get when you answer the first question with a guess and skip the other two. It is the ninety-percent picture; the missing ten percent has a name and a lesson number.

2.2 Two ways to ask the same question

"How much light reaches my eye?" can be asked from two directions, and the difference between them is the difference between the two models in this lesson's title.

The ray question. Work out where a mirror would have sent the light — that is R — and then ask how close the eye is to lying along it. This is Phong's question, it follows the intuition above word for word, and it is the obvious thing to compute.

The surface question. Ask instead: what would a facet have to look like to send this light straight into my eye, and how much of the surface looks like that? This sounds more roundabout and is in fact more direct, because there is exactly one answer to the first half. A facet reflects the light into your eye only if its normal points exactly halfway between the direction to the light and the direction to you — a mirror splits the angle, so the normal that splits it is the one that works.

Call that direction h, the halfway vector. Figure 2 draws it, with the facet it describes.

@@FIG2@@

Now the question "how much of the surface is tilted like h?" becomes "how far is h from the surface's actual normal n?", because a nearly smooth surface has most of its facets nearly aligned with n. That is Blinn's question, and dot(n, h) is its answer.

Both questions are about the same physical situation, and near the peak they give nearly the same answer. They come apart in two places, and both are measured later in this lesson: the shape of the falloff far from the peak (§3.4), and what happens when the eye falls outside the half of the world the mirror ray points into (§3.5). The second is not a matter of taste. It is a case where one model produces a hard edge that no real surface has.

3The Theory

3.1 The mirror direction, derived

We need R: the direction a perfect mirror sends light that arrived along the direction l. As always in this course, l points toward the light, away from the surface (Lesson 3.6 fixed that convention and gave the negation a name so it could not be skipped).

You have already derived this. Lesson 1.8 needed a ball to bounce off a wall, and the argument was: split the vector into the part along the normal and the part along the surface, keep the sideways part, flip the other one. Flipping a part means subtracting it twice — once to cancel it, once more to send it back the other way.

Run it on l. Its part along the normal is its shadow, dot(n, l) · n (no division by |n|², because n is unit). The leftover is everything else:

\[ \mathbf{l} = (\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{l})\,\mathbf{n} \;+\; \underbrace{\bigl[\mathbf{l} - (\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{l})\,\mathbf{n}\bigr]}_{\text{along the surface}} \] l = (n·l)·n + [l − (n·l)·n], the normal part plus the sideways part

A mirror keeps the normal part and flips the sideways part:

\[ \mathbf{R} = (\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{l})\,\mathbf{n} - \bigl[\mathbf{l} - (\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{l})\,\mathbf{n}\bigr] = 2(\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{l})\,\mathbf{n} - \mathbf{l} \] R = 2·(n·l)·n − l

In prose, so the page survives a dead CDN: the mirror direction is twice the shadow of the light direction on the normal, minus the light direction itself.

…and why the sign differs from Pong's

Lesson 1.8's function was reflect(v, n) = v − 2·dot(v, n)·n — note the subtraction where this one has an addition. The two are not different formulas. A ball's velocity points into the wall; a light direction points out of the surface. Feed one an arrow pointing the other way and you get the negation:

\[ \operatorname{mirror}(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{l}) = -\operatorname{reflect}(\mathbf{l},\mathbf{n}) \] mirror_direction(n, l) == −reflect(l, n)

Verified over 20,000 random unit pairs: worst |mirror_direction(n,l) + reflect(l,n)| is 0.000000. Both live in the codebase, both are documented as the other's negation, and picking the wrong one puts the highlight on the opposite side of the object — a bug that looks like a lighting problem and is a sign problem.

Two properties are worth proving rather than assuming, because both get used.

R is unit length. Write c = dot(n,l). Then R·R = 4c²(n·n) − 4c(n·l) + l·l = 4c² − 4c² + 1 = 1, using n·n = l·l = 1. So no renormalisation is needed after mirroring, which is why mirror_direction can be constexpr and free of a square root. Measured over the same 20,000 pairs: worst | |R| − 1 | is 0.000000.

R makes the same angle with the normal that l does. dot(n, R) = 2c(n·n) − dot(n,l) = 2c − c = c, which is exactly dot(n, l). Equal cosines, equal angles — the schoolbook "angle of incidence equals angle of reflection", falling out of the algebra rather than being imposed on it. Measured: worst disagreement 0.0006°.

Worked example — real numbers, pushed through by hand

Take a floor, n = (0, 1, 0), and a light up and to the right, l = (0.6, 0.8, 0) — already unit, since 0.36 + 0.64 = 1.

  • dot(n, l) = 0·0.6 + 1·0.8 + 0·0 = 0.8
  • 2·0.8·n = (0, 1.6, 0)
  • R = (0, 1.6, 0) − (0.6, 0.8, 0) = (−0.6, 0.8, 0)

The x component flipped and the y component did not — which is precisely what a horizontal mirror should do. Check the length: 0.36 + 0.64 = 1. ✓ Check the angles: acos(0.8) = 36.8699° for both l and R. ✓ Both confirmed by verify_37 §A to the last printed digit.

3.2 Phong's term, and what the exponent does

We have R. Now we need "how near is the eye to lying along it, and how much does near count for?" — a function that is 1 when the eye is exactly on R, falls to 0 as it moves away, and whose rate of falling is adjustable.

The cosine of the angle between R and v (the unit direction toward the eye) already does the first two: it is dot(R, v), it is 1 when they coincide, and it reaches 0 at 90°. What it does not do is fall off fast enough — a cosine is a very broad curve, and a real highlight is small.

Raising it to a power fixes that, and it is worth being clear about why this is a choice rather than a derivation. Phong picked cosp in 1975 because it is the cheapest available family of curves that (a) is exactly 1 at the peak, (b) decreases monotonically away from it, and (c) narrows as one parameter grows. It is not derived from any physics. Module 6 replaces it with distributions that are — GGX and Beckmann — and the honest summary is: this shape is a plausible guess that turned out to look good enough for thirty years.

\[ \text{specular}_{\text{Phong}} = \max(0,\ \mathbf{R}\cdot\mathbf{v})^{\,p} \] specular = max(0, dot(R, v)) raised to the power p

The exponent p is the shininess. Its effect is easiest to see through the half-width of the lobe — the angle at which the term has fallen to one half:

Worked example — one pixel, by hand

Continue the floor from §3.1: n = (0,1,0), l = (0.6, 0.8, 0), R = (−0.6, 0.8, 0). Put the eye up and to the left, at v = (−0.8, 0.6, 0) (unit: 0.64 + 0.36 = 1).

  • dot(R, v) = (−0.6)(−0.8) + (0.8)(0.6) = 0.48 + 0.48 = 0.96
  • The angle: acos(0.96) = 16.26° — a near miss.
  • At p = 8: 0.968 = 0.7214. Still a strong highlight.
  • At p = 32: 0.9632 = 0.2708.
  • At p = 128: 0.96128 = 0.00538 — gone.

One geometry, three materials, three completely different pictures. The same 16° miss is a bright highlight on a soft plastic and nothing at all on a varnished one, and that is the entire perceptual content of the shininess parameter.

3.3 The halfway vector, derived properly

§2.2 claimed that the facet which would send the light into your eye has its normal exactly between l and v. Claiming is not deriving, and this one derives in three lines.

We want a unit vector h such that mirroring l about it lands exactly on v. Using §3.1's formula with h in the normal's place:

\[ 2(\mathbf{h}\cdot\mathbf{l})\,\mathbf{h} - \mathbf{l} = \mathbf{v} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 2(\mathbf{h}\cdot\mathbf{l})\,\mathbf{h} = \mathbf{l} + \mathbf{v} \] 2·(h·l)·h − l = v, so 2·(h·l)·h = l + v

The left-hand side is h times some scalar. So h must point along l + v — and since it must also be unit length, there is exactly one answer:

\[ \mathbf{h} = \frac{\mathbf{l} + \mathbf{v}}{\|\mathbf{l} + \mathbf{v}\|} \] h = normalise(l + v)

Nothing was approximated. h is not "roughly" the required normal; it is the required normal, uniquely. Verified by feeding it back in: over 20,000 random (l, v) pairs, the worst |mirror_direction(h, l) − v| is 0.000006 — float rounding, and nothing else.

Why this reframing is worth a whole section

dot(R, v) and dot(n, h) are both cheap, and if speed were the only difference this would be a footnote. It is not. The two ask different questions:

  • dot(R, v) asks about a ray: where did the light go, and am I standing there?
  • dot(n, h) asks about the surface: what fraction of it is already oriented to send light at me?

The second question is the one microfacet theory answers, and answering it well is what Module 6's normal distribution function is. So Blinn-Phong is not a cheaper Phong; it is a crude member of a family Phong does not belong to at all. Meeting the question now, in its simplest form, is what makes GGX read as a better answer to a familiar question rather than as a new incantation.

There is one configuration with no answer: if the eye is exactly opposite the light (v = −l), then l + v is the zero vector and no facet orientation could do the job — the light would have to turn through 180°. Our halfway returns the zero vector there (Lesson 1.7's rule: no direction means no direction, not NaN), and dot(n, 0) = 0 kills the term. That is the correct answer arriving free. Returning n instead — the tempting "safe" fallback — would produce a maximal highlight in the one configuration that cannot have one.

Worked example — the same pixel, Blinn's way

Same floor and light: n = (0,1,0), l = (0.6, 0.8, 0), v = (−0.8, 0.6, 0).

  • l + v = (−0.2, 1.4, 0)
  • |l + v| = sqrt(0.04 + 1.96) = sqrt(2.0) = 1.414214
  • h = (−0.141421, 0.989949, 0)
  • dot(n, h) = 0.989949 — the angle is acos(0.989949) = 8.13°

Compare with §3.2's Phong angle of 16.26°. Exactly half, to the digit — which is not a coincidence and is the subject of the next section. Note what it means for the exponent: at p = 8 Phong gave 0.7214, while 0.9899498 = 0.9224. Same geometry, same exponent, visibly different answers. The two exponents are not interchangeable.

3.4 Why Blinn's exponent is four times Phong's

The worked example above is a single instance of an exact relationship. Set it up cleanly: put n, l and v in one plane (they always span at most a plane through the surface point, and the general case adds nothing) and measure signed angles from the normal. Let the light sit at a and the eye at b.

  • R is l mirrored about n, so it sits at −a. Therefore α = angle(R, v) = |a + b|.
  • h bisects l and v, so it sits at (a + b)/2. Therefore β = angle(n, h) = |a + b| / 2.
\[ \beta = \tfrac{1}{2}\,\alpha \qquad\text{exactly} \] beta = alpha / 2, exactly — not approximately

Figure 3 is that statement drawn to scale, with the arrows placed from the arithmetic rather than by eye.

@@FIG3@@

Confirmed numerically over a 33×33 sweep of light and eye angles: the worst |β − α/2| is 0.000018°.

So the halfway vector moves at half the rate of the mirror direction. Move the eye by one degree and R stays put while h swings half a degree — which means dot(n, h) falls off half as fast as dot(R, v), and to make the two lobes the same width you must sharpen Blinn's exponent.

By how much? Near the peak, cos x ≈ 1 − x²/2, so coskx ≈ exp(−k x² / 2). Matching Phong's exp(−p α² / 2) against Blinn's exp(−q (α/2)² / 2) = exp(−q α² / 8) needs p/2 = q/8, that is:

\[ q \approx 4p \] Blinn's exponent q is about four times Phong's exponent p

Fitted rather than assumed. For each p, verify_37 §C searches for the q minimising the worst difference between the two lobes across the whole range:

Where the equivalence stops

Read the last column: at p = 128 the two lobes agree to 0.0005, and at p = 4 they differ by 0.019 — nearly forty times worse. The 4× rule is a near-peak match, and a broad lobe spends most of its area away from the peak. So the two models converge as surfaces get shinier and diverge as they get rougher, which is exactly backwards from the intuition that "the difference is a detail". Measured on the rendered torus, matched exponents throughout:

  • p = 2 vs q = 8: 5,428 pixels differ — 85.5% of the object — worst delta 30/255.
  • p = 8 vs q = 32: 2,545 pixels, 40.1%, worst delta 9.
  • p = 64 vs q = 256: 210 pixels, 3.3%, worst delta 6.

3.5 Phong's cut-off, stated precisely

Now the difference that is not a matter of degree.

Folklore says Phong "breaks at grazing angles". That is vague enough to be unusable, and §3.4's angle bookkeeping gives the exact condition in one line. Phong clamps to zero when dot(R, v) ≤ 0, that is when α ≥ 90°, that is:

\[ |a + b| \ge 90^\circ \] the light's angle from the normal plus the eye's angle from the normal reaches 90 degrees

Both a and b are individually under 90° whenever the surface is lit and visible at all. So the sum can only reach 90° if they have the same sign — if the light and the eye are on the same side of the normal. That is the precise statement, and it is much more useful than "grazing": on a floor it means the sun is behind you.

Now — why? The tempting explanation, and the one you will find repeated all over the internet, is that the mirror ray points into the ground. It does not, and it cannot. R is l mirrored about n, so it makes the same angle with the normal that l does (§3.1); if the light is above the surface then R is above it too, always.

The real reason is sharper and more useful. cosp only answers over the hemisphere around R — directions within 90° of it. The visible hemisphere is the one within 90° of the normal. Those are two different half-spaces, and with the light at a degrees off the normal they fail to overlap in a wedge running from 90° − a out to 90°. An eye anywhere in that wedge sees nothing at all — and the wedge is exactly as wide as the light's own angle from the normal.

Figure 4 draws it. Both l and v are comfortably above the surface, R is comfortably above it too, and v still lands in the dead wedge.

@@FIG4@@

Blinn's term cannot do this, and the reason is one sentence: h lies between l and v, so if both are above the surface then so is h, so dot(n, h) is positive. Measured over all 7,921 (light, eye) pairs with both strictly above the surface:

Half of all valid configurations. Now, how much of that is visible? Being cut off where the term had already fallen to 0.0001 costs nothing. The honest way to answer is to render a plane, sweep the sun, and count pixels — a plane rather than the torus, because a torus presents every incidence angle at once and always contains grazing patches whatever the light does. On a plane the incidence angle is the sun's elevation.

That is not a subtle shading difference. At a sun elevation of 35° with the light behind you, Phong's model says a wet floor has no sheen whatsoever — and the boundary where it switches off is a hard curve across the surface, because the condition α = 90° is a sharp one. Real floors do not have that curve on them.

The same experiment, arranged the other way

Turn the sun to face you — the sunset-on-water arrangement — and the light and eye are now on opposite sides of the normal, α is small everywhere in view, and Phong shows a highlight on all 30,806 pixels too. The cut-off disappears entirely. The two models still differ (up to 81/255 at 18° elevation) but only in the shape of the tail, which is §3.4's story rather than this one.

Both halves are in render_37 §6 on purpose. An experiment that only ever confirms the claim is not an experiment, and "Phong breaks at grazing angles" is exactly the sort of half-truth that survives because nobody arranged the case where it does not.

Figure 5 puts the two lobes on one picture, at matched exponents, so the agreement near the peak and the divergence in the tail can be seen at once.

@@FIG5@@

This is the honest reason Blinn's version won, and it is worth saying plainly because the usual reason given — "it avoids computing the reflection vector" — is the least important one. Blinn-Phong is the model that Ngan, Durand and Matusik found fits measured reflectance data better, and this elongation of the highlight at grazing incidence is a large part of why.

3.6 The incoming cosine belongs on both terms

Lesson 3.6 derived Lambert's law from a spreading beam: a fixed bundle of light striking a surface tilted by θ covers 1/cos θ more area, so the power arriving per unit of surface falls by cos θ.

Read that argument again and notice what it does not mention: what the surface does with the light afterwards. It is a statement about arrival. A surface tilted away from a lamp receives less light per square centimetre whether it scatters that light diffusely, mirrors it, or eats it. So the same n·l factor multiplies the highlight exactly as it multiplies the diffuse term:

\[ \text{out} = \underbrace{\text{albedo}\cdot(I\,(\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{l}) + \text{ambient})}_{\text{scattered}} \;+\; \underbrace{\text{specular}\cdot I\,(\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{l})\cdot \text{term}}_{\text{mirrored}} \] outgoing = albedo · (light · n·l + ambient) + specular_colour · light · n·l · term

Classic Phong shading, as published, leaves that factor off the specular term, and it is worth knowing what that costs — partly because you will meet code that does it, and partly because the artifact is distinctive.

The geometry that exposes it is one meridian of a sphere: light from the left, eye from the right, and a normal sweeping between them. Here the terminator — where n·l crosses zero — is at 20°, and everything past it is unlit:

0.608 of full strength on geometry the light cannot reach, spread over a band covering 400 of the 701 unlit-but-visible directions. On screen that is a bright crescent hanging on the dark side of an object, brightest right at the terminator and fading into the shadow — a glow with no light source, which is a very odd thing to look at and a very hard thing to diagnose if you do not know to suspect it.

A clamp you do not have to write

Including the factor has a second benefit worth naming. Many implementations carry an explicit "and if the surface faces away from the light, zero the specular" guard. With the cosine in place, that guard is already there: lambert() clamps to zero past the terminator, and zero times anything is zero. A rule that falls out of a correct derivation is a rule that cannot be forgotten in one of the two places it was needed.

3.7 Energy, honestly

Blinn-Phong does not conserve energy, and it is worth being precise about what that sentence means rather than repeating it as a slogan.

A physical surface reflects some fraction of the light that arrives and absorbs the rest; it can never send out more than it received. Our specular term does not know that. Crank the exponent and watch what happens to the two quantities that matter. Integrating the lobe over the hemisphere gives a closed form, confirmed by quadrature to six places:

\[ \int_{\Omega} \cos^{p}\!\alpha \; \mathrm{d}\omega = \frac{2\pi}{p+1}, \qquad \int_{\Omega} \cos^{p}\!\alpha \, \cos\theta_o \; \mathrm{d}\omega = \frac{2\pi}{p+2} \] the lobe integrated over the hemisphere is 2·pi/(p+1); weighted by the outgoing cosine, as a BRDF requires, it is 2·pi/(p+2)

Both were checked against numerical integration at eight exponents from 1 to 128, agreeing to better than 1e−4 every time (verify_37 §H). The second is the one that matters, since a reflectance function has to be integrated against the outgoing cosine — and it is where the familiar (p+2)/(2π) Phong normalisation comes from.

Now the problem, in one line:

Doubling the exponent halves the light the surface sends out, while the peak brightness stays at exactly 1.0. Physically that is backwards. Polishing a surface does not destroy light; it concentrates the same light into a narrower cone, so a smoother surface should have a highlight that is both tighter and brighter. Ours gets tighter and no brighter, which means the total energy quietly drains away as you make things shinier. In practice artists compensate by raising the specular colour as they raise the exponent, which works and is exactly the kind of manual coupling a physically based model removes.

The standard patch is a normalisation factor in front of the term. For the Blinn form the commonly used approximation is:

\[ \frac{m+8}{8\pi}\,(\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{h})^{m} \] (m + 8) / (8·pi) times dot(n,h) to the power m

…which is 0.4775 at m = 4, 1.5915 at m = 32 and 10.5042 at m = 256 — so a mirror-like surface gets a peak ten times brighter than white, which is the physically correct answer and a completely unusable one until there is somewhere to put values above 1.0.

What is being deferred, and to where

We are not shipping the normalisation, and the reason is honest rather than lazy: with an 8-bit framebuffer, to_encoded clamps at 1.0, so a factor of 10.5 would produce a large flat white blob with its shape clipped off and no information about how bright it really was. The normalisation only pays once there is an HDR pipeline and a tonemapper to bring the range back down, and those are Module 6. Exercise 3.7.3 has you implement it and watch precisely that failure.

The (m+8)/(8π) form is an approximation from Hoffman's SIGGRAPH course notes, reproduced in Real-Time Rendering; the exact integral for the Blinn lobe is messier than Phong's because h moves at half the rate of R and the substitution does not stay clean. This is the ninety-percent picture, and Module 6 finishes it properly with a normalised microfacet BRDF.

3.8 Where the highlight will be, in closed form

A claim you cannot check is not worth much, so here is one that can be checked exactly. On a flat surface under a directional light, the position of the highlight's peak has a closed form, and it is short.

The peak is where dot(n, h) is largest, which is where h equals n. By §3.3, h = n exactly when mirroring l about n lands on v — that is, when the direction to the eye is the mirror direction R. And R depends only on the light and the surface: it is the same vector everywhere on the plane.

So the peak is at the point where a ray leaving the eye along −R strikes the plane. For a horizontal plane at y = 0:

\[ \mathbf{p} = \mathbf{e} - \mathbf{R}\,\frac{e_y}{R_y} \] peak position = eye − R · (eye.y / R.y)
Worked example — predicted, then found

Light l = (0.400, 0.799, 0.449) on a floor, so R = (−0.400, 0.799, −0.449) — the horizontal components flip, the vertical one does not.

Eye at (0, 3, 6). Then t = 3 / 0.799 = 3.755, and p = (0,3,6) − 3.755·R = (1.500, 0, 7.688).

Now find it the dumb way: evaluate the Blinn term at every point of a 1601×1601 grid over the plane and take the largest. The argmax is at (1.500, 7.680). The grid step is 0.02, so the two agree to well within one cell. Repeated for four eye positions, the worst disagreement is 0.0107 — and the grid itself cannot resolve better than 0.014.

Move the eye 4 units to the right and the prediction moves 4 units to the right with it ((5.500, 7.688), argmax (5.500, 7.680)): the highlight tracks the viewer, which is the entire thing this lesson set out to build.

Two things fall out of this that are worth carrying. First, on a flat surface the highlight moves with you and a diffuse pattern does not, so the two are trivially distinguishable in a screenshot — useful when debugging someone else's renderer. Second, the peak can easily be behind the camera: with the sun behind you, e − R·t lands further away than the eye, and the visible floor shows only the far tail of the lobe. That is the same configuration §3.5 used, and it explains why the cut-off matters exactly there.

3.9 Why the highlight has a colour of its own

One more parameter needs justifying before we write any code. The specular term is multiplied by a colour that is not the albedo, and the reason is a genuine physical distinction that Module 6 will lean on hard.

A dielectric — plastic, paint, glass, ceramic, wood, skin — is a clear substance with pigment suspended inside it. Light hitting it splits: a few percent bounces straight off the clear outer boundary without ever meeting the pigment, and the rest goes in, scatters around among the pigment particles, and comes back out having been coloured by them. The first part is the highlight and the second is the diffuse term. So a red plastic ball under a white lamp has a white highlight and a red body, and you have seen this ten thousand times without naming it.

A metal has no clear outer layer and no interior to scatter in — it is reflection all the way down. Its reflectivity varies by wavelength, so it tints what it reflects: gold's highlight is yellow, copper's is orange. And because nothing gets inside to scatter back out, a metal has essentially no diffuse term at all.

That is why the demo's three objects are set up the way they are. The icosahedron is an amber body with a white highlight — plastic. The slab is teal with a teal highlight — metal. Put them side by side under one white light and the difference in material is unmistakable, with no change to the geometry and no texture involved.

The number this becomes

Module 6 gives this a name (Fresnel reflectance at normal incidence, written F₀) and a value: about 0.04 for almost every dielectric, and the metal's own tinted reflectance for metals. That single fact is why physically based material systems have a "metallic" switch rather than independent diffuse and specular colours — the two are not independent, and letting an artist set them separately mostly lets them set them wrongly. We are setting them separately here because the constraint has not been derived yet, and inventing it now would be decree rather than derivation.
