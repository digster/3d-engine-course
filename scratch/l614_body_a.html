Module 6 — Advanced Rendering · Lesson 6.14

Antialiasing: Geometric and Shading

Two different problems share this name, and the cure everyone reaches for fixes exactly one of them. Geometric aliasing is the staircase on a silhouette — coverage, a step function with infinite bandwidth, answered yes or no. Shading aliasing is the sparkle on a polished curve, where what is undersampled is the lighting: a specular lobe narrower than the pixel looking for it. We can say exactly where the second begins, because the GGX lobe has a closed-form width — sin t = α·√((√2−1)/(1−α²)), which is 0.6436 α and not the α everyone quotes — and on a sphere 40 px in radius that crosses the normal's per-pixel variation at roughness 0.2468. The demo's 0.49 sits safely above it; every polished metal Lesson 6.12 introduced sits below. Then the measurement that reframes the whole subject: filtering the NDF improves per-pixel accuracy by 2.4× and at some roughnesses makes it worse, while improving stability by 2,034×. Antialiasing was never about getting a more correct pixel.

1The problem: one name, two failures

Render the demo with a polished material and two things look wrong. They look wrong in different places, they respond to different settings, and almost every treatment of antialiasing runs them together.

The first is the one everybody names: the silhouette is a staircase. A triangle's edge cuts across the pixel grid at some angle, each pixel is either inside or outside, and the boundary comes out as a flight of steps.

The second only appears once the camera moves: the highlight sparkles. Isolated pixels on a curved shiny surface flash on and off as the view pans, and they do it at a spatial frequency that has nothing to do with the geometry. Hold the camera still and the frame looks fine. That is the tell.

Why they are not the same problem

Both are aliasing — high-frequency content masquerading as low-frequency content, because the sample grid cannot tell them apart. But what is undersampled differs, and that decides which cures are even available:

geometricshading
what is undersampledcoverage — is this pixel inside the triangle? the lighting — how bright is this surface here?
where it appearssilhouettes and intersections anywhere the surface curves and is shiny
visible when still?yesbarely — it is a motion artefact
fixed by MSAAyesno, by construction

That last row is the reason this lesson separates them before building anything. MSAA is the default answer to "how do I antialias this", and it is a coverage technique: it evaluates coverage and depth at several positions inside each pixel while running the fragment shader once per primitive per pixel. That asymmetry is not a shortcut — it is what MSAA is, and it is why 4× MSAA costs about 1.3× rather than 4×.

You cannot buy shading samples with a coverage feature.

And we can say exactly where the second one starts

This is where the module's own arc pays off. Lesson 6.12 put a value of 55,917 in the frame buffer by polishing a metal; Lesson 6.13 measured what a display could do with it. The same polishing is what makes a surface sparkle, and by §3.4 we will have the crossover as a number rather than a feeling: on a sphere 40 pixels in radius, shading aliasing begins at roughness 0.2468.

The demo's own roughness is 0.49 — above it. Every polished material 6.12 introduced (0.20, 0.10, 0.05) is below it. The module has been walking toward this number for three lessons.

2Building intuition: a pixel is an area

Almost every bug in this lesson comes from one habit of thought, so it is worth attacking directly.

A pixel is not a point. It is a little square region of the image plane, and the value it should hold is the average of the image over that region. The rasterizer we built in Module 2 decides coverage by testing whether the pixel's centre is inside the triangle — one point sample standing in for an integral over an area.

That substitution is exact only when the thing being sampled does not vary over the pixel. When it does — and at a silhouette it varies from "entirely covered" to "entirely uncovered" — the point sample is not an approximation of the average. It is a different number that happens to share a location.

Why more samples is not the whole answer

The obvious fix is more sample points. It helps, and it is what supersampling does, but it is worth understanding why it cannot solve the problem before relying on it.

The sampling theorem says a signal can be reconstructed exactly from point samples only if it contains no frequencies above half the sample rate. Coverage is a step function — it jumps from 0 to 1 at the triangle's edge — and a step's Fourier transform has energy at every frequency, falling off only as 1/f. There is no sample rate high enough.

So no amount of supersampling makes a silhouette correct. What it does is push the error down and, crucially, make the error smooth: instead of a pixel that is fully black or fully white depending on which side of the edge its centre happens to fall, you get one that changes gradually as the edge moves across it.

Worked example. A checkerboard of period 0.37 pixels — far above the Nyquist limit. Its true local mean is exactly 0.5 whatever the phase, which is what makes it a ground truth rather than another rendering.

factor samples/px per-pixel RMS deviation worst pixel 1 1 0.5000 0.5000 2 4 0.1719 0.5000 4 16 0.0794 0.1240 8 64 0.0231 0.0333

At 1× every pixel is black or white — the deviation is a full 0.5, which is as wrong as it is possible to be. At 8× it is 0.0231. But the checker is never recovered, at any factor, because it is not representable on this grid. What changes is that the image stops claiming to be something it is not.

A measurement that measured nothing. The first version of that table reported the mean over the whole image, and found it already correct at 1×. Of course it did: across 4,096 pixels whose phases differ, errors of opposite sign cancel. The mean was blind to the thing it was pointed at. Aliasing is a per-pixel phenomenon, so the statistic has to be a per-pixel one — and a check that passes while measuring nothing is worse than no check at all.

Antialiasing does not recover detail the grid cannot hold. It removes the lie.

Hold on to that sentence — §4 turns it into a measurement, and the measurement is the most surprising result in this module.
