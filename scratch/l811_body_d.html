9. The hinge

A hinge is a ball-socket that also refuses to let its two bodies tilt relative to each other about anything but its axis. The socket takes three degrees of freedom away; the hinge takes two more, and leaves one rotation. The two new rows are pure angular rows — they involve no linear velocity at all — and they say: the relative angular velocity ω_b − ω_a may point along the axis and nowhere else. Pick two unit vectors p₀, p₁ perpendicular to the axis and the rows are

\[ J_i \;=\; \bigl[\;0 \;\big|\; -p_i \;\big|\; 0 \;\big|\; p_i\;\bigr], \qquad J_i V \;=\; p_i\cdot(\omega_b - \omega_a) \;=\; 0 \] J_i = [0, -p_i, 0, p_i], so J_i V = p_i . (w_b - w_a) = 0

and their position error comes from the two copies of the axis the joint stores — one in each body's frame, a₁ as body a carries it and b₁ as body b does. When the hinge is healthy they coincide. When they drift apart, their cross product a₁ × b₁ is (for a small misalignment) the rotation vector that carries one onto the other, and its components along p₀ and p₁ are the two angles the hinge must not allow: C_i = (a₁ × b₁)·p_i. Differentiate and you get J_i·V back, which is the check that the error and the row agree. The two rows share a body and are coupled through its inertia tensor just as a socket's three are, so the engine solves them as a 2×2 block, K_ij = p_i·(I_a⁻¹ + I_b⁻¹)·p_j, with no linear terms at all: turning a body about its centre moves its centre nowhere. 

     joint              worst anchor gap    worst misalignment    worst tilt
     hinge                    0.088 mm           0.0001 deg         0.00 deg
     ball-socket only           4.694 mm           0.0000 deg       175.98 deg

A door given 2 rad/s about its vertical hinge stays within 0.0001° of true for five seconds of swinging. The same door on the socket alone — the three rows, without the two — falls over, 176°. That is the control and it is also the definition: the socket holds the point, and gravity's torque about the point is exactly what the two angular rows exist to answer.

The frame the angular impulse is cached in

The two rows' accumulated impulses are warm-started like everything else, and 8.10 §4 is the reason to be careful about how. There, two cached friction impulses turned out to be coordinates in a tangent basis that tangent_basis rebuilt every frame from a world normal, choosing its seed from the normal's smallest component. On a near-vertical normal two of its components are both near zero and cross constantly, the basis flips by 90°, and last frame's friction was applied sideways. A hinge's two perpendicular impulses have exactly the same shape, and a hinge axis along a coordinate axis has exactly the same near-zero components.

The engine avoids it twice over. The basis is chosen from the axis in body a's own frame — a constant, so the choice is a constant — and rotated into the world with the body, so it turns exactly as smoothly as the body does. And the cached impulse is stored as a world vector (joint::angular_impulse), which carries its own frame and is simply re-measured in this step's basis. Either would have been enough. The harness probes what the obvious way would have done, on a twelve-plank bridge hinged about z, sagging under a bouncing ball:

     the world axis (8.10's way)     127 of 7787            90.000 deg
     body a's own axis (8.11)          0 of 7787             9.767 deg

A plank moving in the x–y plane carries a z axis whose x and y components are rounding noise either side of zero — the precise condition 8.10 found on a crate's normal. The body-chosen basis's worst step-to-step turn is 9.8°, which is the plank itself turning under an impact. 10. Limits: a contact that is always detected

A hinge limit is a one-sided angular row about the axis. For the upper stop, C = θ_max − θ ≥ 0, with θ the hinge angle, and the row pushes only one way, λ ≥ 0. It is a contact on an angle, and it would inherit a contact's defect: 8.10 §6 measured that a contact is only noticed on the step after the bodies cross, so it arrives with an overlap that is uniform on [0, v·h]. A limit that exists only once it is violated arrives with an overshoot uniform on [0, ω·h], for the same reason.

Except that a limit has one thing a contact does not: a hinge always knows its angle. So the row can exist before the stop is reached, with a target of −C/h — "you may close this gap, but no faster than it would take one step to close it" — and the [0, ∞) clamp makes it do nothing at all unless the door is approaching fast enough to overshoot this step. That is a speculative constraint, and for a contact it would need the narrow phase to run on pairs that are not touching yet, which is why 8.10 left speculative contacts as an open problem and this lesson gets speculative limits for free.

The hinge angle itself is 7.4's quaternion read in the one way that ignores everything a limit should not see. The joint stores the relative orientation it was authored in, rest = conj(q_a)·q_b; the deviation from it is q = conj(q_a)·q_b·conj(rest), expressed in a's frame, and a pure turn about the axis by θ gives exactly q = (cos θ/2, sin θ/2 · axis). So θ = 2·atan2(q.v·axis, q.w), with q first moved to the hemisphere w ≥ 0 — the double cover again — and any swing of the axes, which the alignment rows prevent anyway, drops out of the dot product. 

     limit            mean        largest      smallest
     reactive           0.4985      0.9960      1.02e-03
     speculative        0.0000      0.0000      0.00e+00

Overshoot past the stop, in units of ω·h, over 200 starting angles spaced evenly across one step's travel. Reactive: uniform on [0, 1], mean 0.4985 — 8.10 §6's arrival depth, transplanted onto an angle. Speculative: zero. (The first draft of this fixture swept the speed from 2.0 to 2.2 rad/s instead, which moves the arrival phase through 1.36 cycles rather than a whole number of them, and read a mean of 0.42. What has to be sampled uniformly is the phase.)

The door, and the second appearance of ρ

That was a turnstile, hinged through its centre. Hinge the same slab at its edge — a door — and the speculative limit is no longer exact, for a reason that is worth deriving because it is not a defect of limits or of doors. It is a property of every angular row that shares a body with a pin.

The limit row is angular, J = [0, a, 0, −a], so its effective mass is the inertia about the body's centre, I_cm. It computes the change in ω that would stop the door at the stop if the door turned about its centre. But a door does not turn about its centre; it turns about the pin. Having changed ω, the row has left the door's centre moving at a velocity that no longer matches ω × r about the pin, so the pin row pulls it back into line — and in doing so gives back part of the change in ω. Work it through for a body on a fixed pin at distance d from its centre and the fraction given back is

\[ \rho \;=\; \frac{m\,d^2}{I_{\text{cm}} + m\,d^2} \] rho = m d^2 / (I_cm + m d^2), the fraction of the limit's change the pin undoes

so Gauss–Seidel between the limit and the pin contracts by ρ per sweep. It is §5's ρ — the parallel-axis ratio — arriving the second time, as a rate rather than a loss. For any uniform slab hinged at its edge, I_cm = m·W²/12 and d = W/2, so ρ = (1/4)/(1/12 + 1/4) = 3/4 whatever the door's size or mass. The harness puts the slab one step from its stop turning at 2 rad/s into it, and measures what fraction of the required change is still missing after n sweeps: 

     hinge offset   rho predicted   n=1       n=2       n=4       n=8       per sweep
     d = 0.00 W       0.0000    -0.0000   -0.0000   -0.0000   -0.0000    0.0000
     d = 0.25 W       0.4261     0.4261    0.1816    0.0330    0.0011    0.4261
     d = 0.50 W       0.7481     0.7481    0.5597    0.3133    0.0981    0.7481
     d = 1.00 W       0.9224     0.9224    0.8508    0.7238    0.5239    0.9224

To four figures, on every shape: exact through the centre, 0.4261 a quarter of the way out, 0.7481 at the edge (the slab's 0.1 m thickness moves it off 3/4), and 0.9224 on a bracket a full width out. Not "roughly geometric" — the measured error after n sweeps is ρⁿ. It is the cleanest agreement between a derivation and a measurement in the module.

And the consequence, which is visible: at eight sweeps a door with restitution zero bounces off its stop at 7.5% of the speed it arrived at, and an arm on a bracket at 27%. That is not restitution. It is the ρ⁸ of the correction that eight sweeps left undone, stored in the accumulated impulse, handed to the next step by the warm start, and unwound as a separating velocity. The knob that removes it is the sweep count — at 32 sweeps a door's ρ³² is 9 × 10⁻⁵ — and §14 prices sweeps. The fix that would remove it at any sweep count is to solve the limit row and the pin as one 4×4 block, with the limit's clamp handled by trying both cases; that is the "block with one clamp" 8.10 wondered about, and it is named here rather than built.

11. Motors, and what friction was all along

A motor is the axis row with a target that is not zero — the speed it wants — and a symmetric clamp, |λ| ≤ τ_max·h, which is how much impulse its torque can supply in a step. That clamp is what makes it a motor and not a hinge welded to a spinning frame: a load heavier than the motor can turn simply stalls. While the motor is short of its speed the clamp is saturated, it applies exactly τ_max·h every step, and the spin-up should take

\[ t \;=\; \frac{I_{\text{pivot}}\,\omega}{\tau_{\max}} \] t = I_pivot * w / tau_max

with I_pivot the inertia about the pin — because once the clamp is deciding the impulse, the row's own I_cm-based effective mass never enters. That was the claim. 

     slab             I about pin   rho      I w / tau    with §5's loss   measured
     through centre    0.8417 kg m2  0.000     0.6733 s       0.6733 s       0.6833 s
     door, at edge     3.3417 kg m2  0.748     2.6733 s       2.8847 s       2.8833 s

Through the centre, right to the step. At the edge, 7.9% slow — the fourth measurement in this lesson to refuse its section. The clamp really does apply τ_max·h per step. What the claim forgot is that the joint is also losing energy the whole time, at §5's rate: ρ(ωh)² of its kinetic energy per step, which is ½·ρ·h·ω² of its angular momentum per second. So the motor is fighting

\[ \frac{d\omega}{dt} \;=\; \frac{\tau_{\max}}{I_{\text{pivot}}} \;-\; \frac{\rho\,h}{2}\,\omega^3 \] dw/dt = tau_max / I_pivot - (rho h / 2) w^3

and integrating that predicts 2.8847 s against a measured 2.8833 — within a step. That is ρ's third appearance, and the one that ties the other two together: the loss a motor has to overcome is the same parallel-axis fraction that set the pendulum's period and the limit's convergence.

The stall is the cleaner test. A 1 m, 2 kg arm hinged at its end about a horizontal axis, held level, motor asked to lift it: gravity's torque about the pin is m·g·d = 9.8100 N·m, and bisecting the motor's torque over eighteen halvings for the smallest that lifts the arm finds 9.8100 N·m, to 4.8 × 10⁻⁶. The clamp is a torque, exactly.

Friction was this row all along

8.9's friction rows clip the tangential impulse to μ times the normal impulse. A motor's row clips to τ_max·h. Set a motor's target speed to zero and it is a brake with a fixed maximum torque — which is Coulomb friction in the hinge: a constant decelerating torque while the door slides, and exactly zero relative speed held once the required impulse falls inside the clamp, which is static friction from the same line of code. 

     tau          predicted stop    measured stop    speed after 5 s
      2.0 N m        3.2752 s        3.2833 s     7.29e-43 rad/s
      4.0 N m        1.6538 s        1.6833 s     1.40e-45 rad/s
      0.0 N m         never           never       1.7904 rad/s   (control: no brake)

The prediction includes §5's loss, which here helps the brake. The door stops within two steps of it and then stays stopped to 10⁻⁴³ rad/s. What lateness there is — under two steps at 4 N·m — is the last step, where the door is slower than τ·h can stop, so the brake is not saturated, and an unsaturated axis row on a door converges at §10's ρ per sweep. The control has no brake and is still turning at 1.7904 rad/s after five seconds. The missing speed is §5 once more, and with the motor's term deleted the equation above has a closed form, ω(t) = ω₀ / √(1 + ρ·h·ω₀²·t), which says 1.7894.

That is why contact_material — a restitution and a friction coefficient — stops being the right shape for "what a constraint may do". Friction is a bound derived from another row's impulse; a motor is a bound from a torque; a limit is a bound at zero on one side. They are all jacobian_row::lower and upper.

12. Islands, sleeping and joints

8.10 built islands on one rule: a contact is an edge of the island graph when both of its bodies can move, because a fixed body cannot propagate an impulse and would otherwise weld the level into one island. A joint is an edge by exactly the same rule — and in the code it is literally the same function, union_edge, lifted out of the contact loop so that joints could call it and the two could never disagree. 

     islands with joint edges       2   (the solver reports 2)
     islands without them          16

With joint edges, two chains hung from one ceiling are two islands: each chain is joined along its length, and the fixed ceiling joins nothing. Without them, every link is an island of one, because the links of a chain never touch — their joints hold them apart.

What that would cost is sleeping, and a chain is the case where it is guaranteed to bite. 8.10 §10 showed that a body slept on its own becomes, to everything else, an immovable body, and hangs in the air when its support is knocked away. A chain's links go quiet together, so they would sleep together under either rule — the difference only appears when something wakes one of them. 

     rule                 asleep before   awake after   moved > 1 mm   bottom swung
     per island (engine)        8 of 8          8 of 8         8 of 8     0.329 m
     per link                  8 of 8          1 of 8         1 of 8     0.129 m

Under the island rule the whole chain wakes and swings. Under a per-link rule — emulated, as 8.10 §10 did it, by making the other seven links fixed, which is not an approximation but the same statement in the type system — one link swings as a 0.25 m pendulum hung from a frozen chain in mid-air. (The first draft counted links that had moved more than a centimetre and reported seven of eight under the island rule: the top link, pinned to the ceiling, moves less than that. The direct instrument is whether each link is awake.)
