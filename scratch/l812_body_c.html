6. A skeleton with mass

With the angles settled, a ragdoll is a description and a build step. The description is written against the skeleton's bind pose, in model space, because that is where a person authoring one is looking: the character stands in its T-pose, capsules are drawn over it, and the numbers are read off the picture. For each part, ragdoll_part_desc names the skeleton joint the body follows (called bone, because this file has two kinds of joint in it and every part has one of each at the same place), the capsule's segment and radius, its share of the total mass, and how it hangs from its parent: a hinge axis and range, or a twist axis, a cone axis, a cone half-angle and a twist range.

build_ragdoll does four passes over that. It finds each part's parent part — the nearest ancestor skeleton joint that is also a part, so the upper arm hangs from the torso through a clavicle that is only a passenger. It stands each capsule along its segment (8.5's capsules run along body +y, so the body's orientation is 7.4's two-mirror rotor from +y to the bone) and records where the body sits in its bone's frame — the joint_from_body transform, constant, and the only link between the two representations. It authors each joint with 8.11's make_hinge or this lesson's make_cone_twist, at the bind pose, and keeps only the body-local result, which is valid at every pose. And it audits every pair of capsules for self-collision.

How good is a capsule as a limb?

A capsule puts a body's centre of mass at its middle and spreads its mass uniformly. A real limb does neither: a thigh is heavier at the hip. Dempster measured both on cadavers, and Winter's Table 4.1 gives each segment's centre of mass as a fraction of its length from the proximal joint and its radius of gyration about that centre. The number that matters to the solver is neither of those directly — it is 8.11's ρ = m·d²/(I_cm + m·d²) about the proximal joint, which sets how fast the limb's limits converge against its pin, how much energy a velocity joint takes out of it per step, and how hard it bounces off a stop. Computed both ways: 

     segment          L m    CoM: capsule  Dempster   k: capsule  Dempster   rho: capsule  Dempster
     upper arm        0.280   0.500        0.436      0.282      0.322      0.759         0.647
     forearm + hand   0.250   0.650        0.682      0.344      0.468      0.781         0.680
     thigh            0.420   0.500        0.433      0.272      0.323      0.772         0.642
     shin + foot      0.410   0.500        0.606      0.273      0.416      0.770         0.680

Every capsule's ρ comes out about 0.1 high — 0.76 to 0.78 against Dempster's 0.64 to 0.68 — and the cause is the second column more than the first: a capsule carries too little of its mass far from its own centre (k 0.27–0.34 of the length against 0.32–0.47), so too much of the inertia about the joint is the orbit. The engine keeps capsules anyway. They are what the narrow phase is fast on, the error is in the direction of more dissipation and slower-bouncing stops — a ragdoll that settles a little faster than a person would — and the honest move is to say so here rather than to hide a correction in the inertia tensor that the collision shape disagrees with.

Which pairs may collide

8.11's rule was that two bodies a joint connects must not collide: they overlap at the joint by construction, and a contact solver that sees the overlap fights the joint every step. The handover to this lesson predicted that a ragdoll would need more than that — "a forearm and a torso overlap too, two joints apart" — and the engine grew a rule for it: exclude every pair whose capsules overlap, within a centimetre, at rest. The harness then audited the humanoid. The prediction was wrong. In the bind pose, and over a whole cycle of both motions this lesson hands over, no unjointed pair comes within 4 cm of another — the closest is the two thighs, a constant 40 mm apart. So the default rule excludes exactly the ten jointed pairs and nothing else.

What the prediction was reaching for is real, but it is not about rest. It is about falls. Over twelve trips from the jog, three seconds each, the narrow phase's own manifolds record every pair of parts that touched:

The torso and a forearm touch in nine falls of twelve, more than any other pair; the two thighs in six. Both are two links apart. That matters because the obvious rule a ragdoll author writes first — "exclude anything within two links of each other", which is what the handover's sentence suggests — excludes thirteen pairs beyond the jointed ten, and those three are among them. Under it a forearm swung across the chest does not stop at the chest. The same twelve falls, each rule: 

     rule                     deepest instant   frames > 20 mm   deepest at rest
     two links                   189.9 mm          1503             179.6 mm
     overlapping (default)       106.6 mm           509              61.4 mm

The first column is not the instrument, and the section's first draft used it: a forearm swung at several metres a second arrives up to v·h deep in one step whatever the rule — 8.10 §6's arrival depth, and this engine's missing speculative contacts — so both rules reach a tenth of a metre for a frame. What separates them is whether the overlap persists. Under the two-links rule limbs spend three times as many frames more than 2 cm inside the body, and come to rest there: 179.6 mm, which is a forearm lying inside a chest whose capsule is 150 mm in radius. The default's 61.4 is something else, and §8 finds exactly the same number as the torso-on-forearm overlap of the one fall that ends face down on its own arm: a chest lying on a forearm, pressed into it from above. That is an iteration problem, not the filter's.

The control is to exclude nothing, not even the jointed pairs. After a trip and four seconds the default ragdoll holds 0.0015 J and its worst joint gap is 0.01 mm; with every jointed pair colliding it holds 0.0429 J, its worst joint has been pushed 67.51 mm open, and it is still moving — each joint's two capsules shoving apart what the joint pulls together, 8.11's pitfall at the scale of a body.

7. The first fall, and a bug 8.11 shipped

The first ragdoll this lesson dropped — tripped out of the jog, three seconds on the floor — lay still, which looked right, and then a probe printed each joint's angle against its limits. The right knee was 7.9° past its stop, bent backward, and a shoulder 6.4° past its twist range. Run for longer, the knee went on creeping: 3.9° after one second, 7.9° after three, 9.0° after ten, and the ragdoll never fell asleep. Those numbers were measured on 8.11's engine, before the fix below, and they are the one thing in this lesson the harness cannot reproduce — it links the fixed engine. What it reproduces is the mechanism.

A limit held under load is 8.11's machinery doing exactly what it was built for, so the first suspects were the ones 8.11 had already measured: the limit converging against the pin at ρ per sweep, or the contacts pushing the leg. Neither survives an isolated test. The harness puts a shin on a fixed thigh, lying straight out behind the knee so gravity drives it into its hyperextension stop, and holds it there for ten seconds under a harness-local copy of the solver's joint path, once with 8.11's position pass (kept verbatim in the harness, as 8.10 kept a private copy of the step it split) and once with the engine's: 

     position pass       warm start   2 s        4 s        6 s        8 s        10 s
     8.11's              on            -0.1691    -0.1691    -0.1691    -0.1691    -0.1691
     8.12's              on            -0.0000    -0.0000    -0.0000    -0.0000    -0.0000
     8.11's              off          -19.8455   -37.5492   -51.8791   -62.7588   -70.7129
     8.12's              off           -1.8915    -1.8915    -1.8915    -1.8915    -1.8915
     none (Baumgarte)    on            -0.0000    -0.0000    -0.0000    -0.0000    -0.0000

The first row is the whole bug in one line. A violation of 0.1691° that does not change in the fourth decimal for ten seconds is not a limit converging slowly; it is a limit that is not being corrected at all. Split impulse's position pass removes a fifth of a violation every step, and here it removes none. The third row confirms it from the other side: without warm starting, the velocity pass leaves a little approach velocity every step, and with no correction to hold it the stop gives way without bound, 70° in ten seconds. Baumgarte, which corrects in the velocity pass, never had the problem — which is why the obvious diagnosis, "split impulse is too weak for a loaded stop", would have been exactly wrong.

What the position pass was doing is in the prepared rows, and the harness prints them five seconds in: 

     lower stop    C = -0.002951 rad   bias 0.03541   pseudo impulse +0.006513
     upper stop    C = +2.620945 rad   bias 0.00000   pseudo impulse +0.006513
     the knee's pseudo spin about the hinge after the pass: +3.648e-09 rad/s

The upper stop — 150° away, nowhere near violated — has accumulated exactly as much pseudo impulse as the lower one, in the opposite direction, and the knee's net pseudo spin is 3.6 × 10⁻⁹ rad/s. It cancels the correction to seven figures. And the reason is a line 8.11 wrote with a correct comment above it. Every one-sided row gets a speculative target −C/h in the velocity pass — "you may approach this stop, but not faster than a step allows" — and bias = 0, because it is not violated. 8.11's position pass solved every row against its bias. For a satisfied row that made the target zero: a hard "no pseudo-velocity toward me", from a stop the joint could not reach in a hundred steps. A hinge with both ends of its range enabled always has both rows, speculation being on, so the far stop undid every correction of the near one, and under split impulse a violated two-ended limit was never repaired. 8.11's comment on the function said "a position correction only repairs error that already exists", which is the right intent; the code skipped motors and nothing else.

@@FIG_BUG@@

The fix is one line in solve_joint_positions and a helper that takes the goal explicitly: a one-sided row that is not violated gets its own speculative target, −C/h, in the position pass too — a correction may carry the joint up to the far stop and no further. Violated rows keep their bias; a rod's bilateral row has no speculative side. The fixed pass holds the loaded knee at 0.0000°, and cold it settles at a steady lag of 1.8915° instead of giving way. On the whole ragdoll the knee now lies inside its range, and §6's default row — a trip and four seconds, measured by the harness on the fixed engine — reads a worst joint gap of 0.01 mm.

Why 8.11 never saw it, and what the fix moved

8.11 measured limits at the velocity level: a turnstile's overshoot, a door's rebound, a motor's stall. Each of those is decided in the step the limb arrives, before a violation has had time to be frozen; the position pass is barely involved. A rope has one row and a rod's row is bilateral, so neither ever had a far stop. It took a limit held against a steady load — a knee under a leg's weight, which is every ragdoll's normal state — for a violation to sit there long enough to notice that nothing was repairing it.

Fixing a shipped lesson's engine code raises the question 8.10's tangent-basis fix settled: what happens to the lesson that shipped the bug? Its page is an archive of its own code — the listings are pinned, and 8.11's page still shows the pass that has the bug, with the comment that was right about the intent. What the fix must not do is quietly change 8.11's measurements, so the harness that published them was rerun against the fixed engine: 58 checks of 58, and one table moved. It is §10's rebound table, the one place 8.11 held a limb against a stop for a second: 

     hinge offset   rho^8      overshoot     rebound        (8.11's page)
     d = 0.25 W    0.00109    0.00003 rad     0.0011
     d = 0.50 W    0.09813    0.00318 rad     0.0753
     d = 1.00 W    0.52388    0.01744 rad     0.2706

     hinge offset   rho^8      overshoot     rebound        (with 8.12's fix)
     d = 0.25 W    0.00109    0.00001 rad     0.0011
     d = 0.50 W    0.09813    0.00316 rad     0.0750

The overshoot past each stop is now repaired a little, so it is a little smaller, and a door's rebound moves from 7.53% to 7.50%. 8.11's prose says "7.5%", which is still true. Every other number in 8.11's harness is byte-identical except its timings. The lesson learned is 8.10's again, in a new place: what found the bug was a probe, not a test. No check in 8.11 was wrong. Printing one joint's angle against its limits, on an object the check had never been run on, was what disagreed.

8. What eight sweeps do to a ragdoll

8.11 §14 left a warning for this lesson: a chain's stretch grows with its mass ratio and halves with each doubling of sweeps, and eight sub-steps of one sweep beat one step of eight sweeps by 19× on a 100:1 chain, for the same number of joint visits. A ragdoll is a tree of short chains with a 16:1 ratio in it. The harness measures four scenes against five budgets — 8, 16 and 32 sweeps, eight sub-steps of one sweep (the same visits as the shipped eight), and a control of 32 sub-steps of four sweeps each, sixteen times the shipped work: 

     budget               fall: peak   rest     hanging by a hand   chest on arm
     8 sweeps (shipped)      92.47    12.860         4.128  (KE 6.5e-05)     61.4
     16 sweeps               54.88     7.204         3.319  (KE 4.6e-05)     28.8
     32 sweeps               32.75     0.488         2.524  (KE 2.7e-05)     20.3
     8 sub-steps x 1         30.03     6.741         0.001  (KE 1.2e-08)      3.3
     CONTROL 32 x 4           3.69     0.002         0.001  (KE 1.4e-07)      0.0

A fall opens the joints for a moment. The worst joint gap anywhere in six falls peaks at 92 mm at eight sweeps — the instant a shin or a head strikes the floor and the impulse has to cross the tree — and closes again (12.9 mm at rest, in the worst of the six). More sweeps help, halving it roughly per doubling as 8.11 found. Eight sub-steps do as well as 32 sweeps, at a quarter of the visits.

A hanging character refuses 8.11's rule. Hang the whole 80 kg from one hand — 1.76 kg of forearm carrying everything, a 45:1 load — damp every part so it comes to rest, and read the gap after eight seconds. More sweeps barely help: 4.13, 3.32, 2.52 mm for 8, 16 and 32. Eight sub-steps of one sweep: 0.001 mm, four thousand times less for the same visits, and the sixteen-times-the-work control does no better. This section's first draft hung the body undamped, and the measurement was nonsense — the body swings like a pendulum for a long time after release, with tens of joules still in it five seconds later, and a gap sampled mid-swing measures the phase of the swing, not the solver. A load test needs the load at rest.

And there is a stack nobody built. §6's resting overlap turned out to be one fall that ends face down on the right forearm: 28.4 kg of chest on 1.76 kg of arm on the floor. That is 8.10's heavy-on-light tower, the case eight sweeps cannot hold, formed by accident, and the chest sinks 61.4 mm into the arm and stays there. It is an iteration problem — 28.8 at sixteen sweeps, 20.3 at thirty-two, 3.3 with eight sub-steps, nothing at the control — and it comes back in §13, because a crushed arm is a ragdoll that never stops fighting.

So the handover's first limit is confirmed and sharpened: a ragdoll's mass ratios cost it centimetres at eight sweeps, and the answer 8.11 measured for chains — small steps rather than many sweeps — is better still for a body, by orders of magnitude on a static load. The engine does not sub-step yet; the harness's scene does it by running the whole pipeline substeps times at h/substeps, and the demo's U does the same. Pricing that for a whole scene, contacts and broadphase included, is Module 9's.
