Module 8 — Physics · Lesson 8.13

A Character Controller

Twelve lessons built a simulation that is correct, and the player's character is the one body in a game for which that is the problem. A rigid capsule on 8.10's solver, rotation locked, friction 0.6, stands on a 30° ramp and hangs on a wall mid-jump at any stick speed above 27 cm/s — one friction coefficient, two wishes. It slides 2.91 m after the stick is released, climbs a 20 cm ledge only at 8 m/s, and passes through a 10 cm wall half the time at 42 m/s. So the character leaves the simulation. It becomes a capsule with a position and no mass that asks the world one question, how far can I move before I touch something?, and answers it with a cast. A cast is Newton's method on a convex distance, and it cannot overshoot because each step stops at a separating plane. That holds only if the step is taken from GJK's certified lower bound: stepped from its distance, 377 of 6,049 casts finished inside the skin. Everything else is policy, and each policy has a number. Clip the original motion against every surface, or walk back and forth in an obtuse corner at 58 cm/s. A round bottom climbs a 90.8 mm curb for free and stands 219 mm past an edge. A step-up must carry the capsule up to 90.8 mm across before it lands on a ledge, and with the curb it climbs 0.39 m when told 0.30. A rider carried by velocity spirals off a turntable at eπωh per revolution. A kinematic proxy, steered and never teleported, lets the character push a crate at its own speed. Teleported, the proxy sinks 138 mm into the crate, or walks straight through it once it is asleep. The controller tunnels through nothing at any speed up to 10,000 m/s. Module 8 ends here.

1. The problem: a character that obeys Newton

Every lesson in this module has made something more physically correct. Bodies keep their momentum (8.2), spin the way their mass is spread (8.3), meet at the right points (8.7), push each other exactly as hard as they must (8.9), settle into stacks (8.10), and hang from joints (8.11). A ragdoll falls down a staircase the way a body would, because nothing about it is authored (8.12). The obvious next step is to make the player's character one more body of the same kind: a capsule standing upright, which is what 8.5 said a capsule was for, with its rotation locked so that it cannot tip over. The stick sets its velocity, and the solver does the rest.

So build it, on exactly 8.10's solver: a dynamic capsule 1.8 m tall and 0.3 m in radius, 80 kg, a zero inverse inertia (every engine's "freeze rotation" box), friction 0.6. Then ask it for the five things any designer asks of a character, and measure what it does. §A of this lesson's harness does exactly that, and Figure 1 is the result.

Stand on a 30° ramp. At μ = 0.5 it slides 1.98 m in two seconds, against a predicted 1.97 from g(sin α − μ cos α). That is correct: 8.9 measured the critical slope at atan(μ), 26.6°, and 30° is steeper. Raise μ to 0.6 (tan 30° = 0.577) and it holds.

Jump at a wall and keep pushing into it. Now μ = 0.6 is the problem, because the capsule hangs there. Each step the stick sets its horizontal velocity to v_push and the contact cancels it, so the normal impulse is m·v_push. Coulomb friction may then hold up to μ·m·v_push of vertical momentum, and gravity only adds m·g·h a step. The capsule hangs whenever μ·v_push ≥ g·h: a stick speed of g·h/μ, 0.2725 m/s at 60 Hz. The harness bisects it at 0.2757. Any character pushing into a wall at walking pace sticks to it. Setting μ to zero fixes the wall (it falls at exactly −9.81 m/s after one second) and breaks the ramp. Friction is one number for a pair of materials, and the design wants two: grip on the floor, none on the wall. The same material is floor at one angle and wall at another, so no material table can hold both.

Let go of the stick at 6 m/s. It slides 2.91 m. The continuous v²/(2μg) is 3.06 m, and the sum of semi-implicit Euler's steps, taken from where the stick let go, is 2.9098 m to four decimals. Momentum is conserved, and the design wants a stop.

Walk into a ledge. The round bottom meets a ledge's edge with a tilted normal, and the solver turns part of the stick into lift. How much depends on how hard the capsule is driven. A 10 cm ledge goes up at 1 m/s but not at 0.5. A 20 cm ledge goes up at 8 m/s and at nothing slower, and 30 cm never does. Whether a staircase can be climbed depends on speed, which no level designer can work with.

Dash into a wall. 8.10 §6 measured that a body arriving at a surface is first sampled up to one step's travel deep, uniformly. At 42 m/s a step is 0.7 m, and a 10 cm wall is passed exactly half the time (§9 derives the odds and measures them).

None of those red cells is a bug in 8.10's solver; each is the solver being right. A physics engine exists to conserve momentum, to make friction one number, and to answer questions about instants, and those are the three properties the design needs to be false. The fix is not a better solver. It is to take the character out of the simulation entirely, and that is the rest of the lesson.

2. Building intuition: a question, not a body

A controller does not simulate a character. It keeps a position and, every step, asks the world one question: if this capsule moved by d, how far would it get before it came within a centimetre of something, and what would it be touching? That question has a name, a shape cast (a sweep), and a precise answer: a fraction t of the move, a surface normal, and a body. Everything else a controller does is policy, a decision about what to do with the answer.

  • If it hit a wall, keep the part of the move that runs along the wall (collide and slide, §4).
  • If it hit ground, which is a normal within 45° of up, lay the move along it rather than stopping; and never let a steeper surface lift the character (slopes, §5).
  • After the move, look a little way down. If there is ground, the character is grounded, and its normal and its body are worth keeping (§6).
  • If it was grounded last step and the ground has dropped away, snap down onto it rather than stepping off into the air (§7).
  • If a wall is shorter than a stair, step up it: up, across, down (§8).
  • If the ground is moving, move with it, by exactly what it did (§10).

Figure 2 is that list as move_character runs it: five stages, each labelled with the section that measures it, over the one query they all share. The move is cut into a sideways pass and a vertical pass because walls and floors want opposite treatment. A wall should stop a sideways move and never a fall. A floor should stop a fall and never a sideways move. Doing both in one collide-and-slide is the classic way a character ends up creeping downhill while standing still, and §5 measures the creep at g·h·sin α.

Who owns what. 8.12's rule was that every body has exactly one owner at a time. The controller owns the character's position, and the solver owns every other body. The two have to meet, or the character would walk through crates and crates would fall through the character, and they meet through a proxy. The proxy is a kinematic capsule in the body_world, placed where the character is and steered there by velocity, the chord of 8.12, never teleported. To the solver it is an infinitely heavy moving body. A crate it walks into is pushed at the speed the character is really moving, and keeps that speed when the character stops. Light dynamic bodies (40 kg or less) are pushable: the controller's sideways moves ignore them, and the proxy shoves them. Heavier ones are walls. §11 measures what teleporting the proxy does instead, and it is worse than the obvious answer.

When. Each fixed step runs in three parts, in this order. Steer the proxy to where the controller left the character. Run the physics step. Then move the character against the world as the step left it. Running the controller after the step means it sees every crate and platform where it now is. It also means the one thing it cannot see in the world, how far the platform under it just travelled, is exactly readable from that platform's velocity and the integrator that moved it (§10).

Design, not Newton. The displacement handed to move_character is the game's, and so is everything that produces it: acceleration curves, air control, and the rule that a grounded character does not fall. A jump is the clearest example. A designer names a height, not a force, and the launch speed is arithmetic: v₀ = √(2gH). Asked for 1.20 m, though, the character rises 1.1599 m. Semi-implicit Euler (8.1) moves each step at the velocity it ends with, so the first step already moves at v₀ − g·h, and the apex is the discrete sum v₀²/2g − v₀h/2 = 1.1596. Four centimetres is not a physics error. It is a design parameter measured in the units the integrator actually works in, and a game that cares aims for the height the steps give.
