Three files change, and one of the three changes in a way worth dwelling on. As always this section is for understanding — every file appears whole in §Complete Code Listings.

4.1 reflect grows a third component

Lesson 1.8 wrote reflect for vec2, to bounce a ball off a wall. The 3-D version is the same line, and it is worth noticing that the derivation needed no changes either — the shadow argument never counted components. That is the third time this course has moved something from 2-D to 3-D for free (the dot product in 2.6, the whole of linear algebra in 2.6, and now this), and it is not luck: arguments phrased geometrically tend not to depend on the dimension.

And in light.hpp, the same operation with the arrow the other way round:

Both exist, and both are documented as the other's negation. That may look like duplication, and the alternative is worse: a single function whose caller has to remember which way the argument points is a function whose caller will eventually get it wrong, and the symptom — the highlight appearing on the far side of the object — reads as a lighting bug rather than a sign bug. Two names, each honest about its own convention, cost one line.

4.2 The two terms, and one clamp that earns its keep

Both terms have the same shape: measure an alignment, discard it if it has gone negative, raise it to the shininess.

Two details in three lines, and neither is decoration.

The early return is not just the §3.5 clamp. It also keeps std::pow away from 00, which is 1 — so a surface at exactly the cut-off boundary with a shininess of zero would return a full-strength highlight instead of none. Shininess is documented as positive, but a guard that costs a branch we already needed is cheaper than a documented precondition.

The upper clamp is not paranoia; it was measured. The dot product of two unit vectors is a cosine and cannot exceed 1 — but it can round above it, and pow then amplifies the excess rather than absorbing it. Before the clamp was added, verify_37's ground-truth peak on the torus printed 1.00000 as 1.00001. Invisible on screen, and still worth fixing: the function advertises a range of [0,1], and a clamp is what makes that a fact rather than an intention. GPUs spell the same thing saturate and charge nothing for it.

Blinn's version is the same shape with a different question:

Both are kept, and Phong is kept specifically so it can be watched failing. That is the eighth time this engine has made that bargain — draw_line_naive (2.1), swept_collision (1.8), blend_space::encoded (2.4), the w toggles (2.7), trs_order (2.8), interpolation::affine (3.2), near_mode (3.3) and cull_choice::back_by_forward (3.4). A bug you can summon with a keypress is worth more than one you can only describe.

4.3 specular: two fields, and a name withheld

A highlight needs two numbers that the engine has nowhere to put: how tight it is, and what colour it comes out. Both belong to the surface — not to the light, not to the rasterizer, and not to fill_style, whose job is the state the fill needs.

This is a material, and it is deliberately not called one. A material is also the albedo, the cull mode, the blend mode, the textures and eventually the shader itself, and inventing four fifths of that here would be guessing at a design three lessons before the course has earned it. What this struct is is the pull: watch it try to draw the other fields in. Lesson 3.2 predicted the shading enum would strain; 3.4 noted that closed belongs on a material and that two cull modes means two batches; 3.6 noted that fill_style is the wrong home for lighting. This is the fourth pull in the same direction, and Module 6 is where it is answered — with arguments, rather than by accretion.

Note the default. A black specular colour means "no highlight", which makes specular{} reproduce Lesson 3.6 exactly — and "exactly" is checked rather than hoped: verify_37 §I shades 100,000 random configurations both through shade() with a default specular and through 3.6's arithmetic written out longhand, and 0 of 100,000 differ. Bit for bit. A new feature that cannot change the old pictures is a feature you can ship without re-verifying the previous lesson.

C++ in place — default member initialisers and aggregate init

specular has no constructors, so it is an aggregate: it can be built with braces, {{0.85f, 0.85f, 0.85f}, 64.0f}, and the members are initialised in declaration order. The = 32.0f and {0,0,0} are default member initialisers, used for any member the braces do not reach — so specular{} is fully initialised and specular{{1,1,1}} gets a shininess of 32 without saying so.

This is why the engine prefers plain aggregates for parameter bundles over constructors: every field is named at the point of use, adding a field does not break existing call sites that used designated initialisers, and there is no way to accidentally leave one uninitialised. It is the same reasoning that gave fill_style its shape in 3.2.

4.4 A parameter with no default

shade() gains three parameters. Two of them default; one deliberately does not.

to_eye has no default, and that is the entire point of its position in the list. Every call site written before this lesson is now a compile error. Given a default it would have been a silent behaviour change — a highlight computed against a viewer who is not there, which produces a picture that looks plausible and is wrong. This is the same bargain Lesson 3.1 made when it inserted z ahead of colour in vertex: when a change must be noticed, make the compiler notice it.

The surface parameters do default, because there the safe answer and the correct answer coincide: nothing to say about shininess means no highlight means 3.6's picture.

The body is where §3.6's argument becomes arithmetic:

Read the last three lines against §3.6's equation. ir is the light arriving, already carrying the cosine; the diffuse part multiplies it by the albedo and the mirrored part by the highlight colour. They cannot simply be added before multiplying, because they are multiplied by different things — which is the structural reason a material needs two colours rather than one.

Notice also that the ambient term is added to the diffuse path only. Ambient is a stand-in for light bouncing in from everywhere; it has no direction, so there is no mirror direction for it to have come from, and giving it a highlight would be inventing a reflection of a light that does not exist. (A real answer to "what does the whole environment reflect off this surface?" is image-based lighting, Module 6.)

The early-out is worth one sentence of justification: a black highlight skips std::pow entirely, so every scene from Lessons 3.1 through 3.6 pays nothing at all for this lesson existing. A feature that taxes the code paths that do not use it is a feature that gets removed later.

4.5 The world position comes back

Here is the change that is genuinely structural rather than additive, and it is easy to miss because it is three lines.

Through Lesson 3.6, collect_triangles composed the two transforms once per object and sent each vertex straight from model space to view space:

It could do that because nothing in the shading needed to know where the vertex was. A directional light is a direction; Lambert compares two directions; the world position never appeared. Composing the matrices was a real optimisation — one multiply per vertex instead of two — and it was available precisely because the shading was view-independent.

A highlight needs eye − position. That is a question about places, not directions, so the world position has to exist, and the composition has to come apart:

Same destination, one more matrix multiply per vertex. That is what view dependence costs, and it is worth naming rather than absorbing: the optimisation was not lost to carelessness, it was bought out by a feature. Being able to say which of your fast paths depend on which of your simplifying assumptions is most of what performance work actually is.

The usual dodge, and why we are not taking it

There is a well-known alternative: instead of moving every vertex into world space, move the eye into model space once per object (and the light with it), and shade entirely in model coordinates. That trades one matrix inverse per object against one matrix multiply per vertex, which is an excellent trade for any object with more than a handful of vertices.

We are not doing it here because it makes the normal-matrix story from 3.6 considerably harder to follow — you would be shading with model-space normals and a model-space light, and the inverse transpose would appear in a different place. Exercise 3.7.4 works it out. This is the honest shape of a lot of optimisation: the fast version is fine, and it is the version you write second.

The other half of the change is the eye itself. cam.eye() has existed since Lesson 2.9, and it is worth noticing that the view matrix contains that position but getting it back out would take an inverse. The general rule: when a transform is built from meaningful inputs, keep the inputs. That is why Module 5's camera stores a transform rather than a matrix.

Rendering Phong and Blinn at the same exponent and counting differences mostly measures that one lobe is wider than the other — which is true, is §3.4's whole point, and swamps the effect under test. 4.7 What did not change, for the third lesson running

raster.cpp gained nothing. fill_style gained no field, fill_triangle gained no branch, and the rasterizer still has no idea that light exists.

That is the same observation Lesson 3.6 made, and it is now starting to look less like a happy accident and more like a structural fact: lighting produces a vertex colour, and interpolating vertex colours is Lesson 2.4's job. The vertex stage and the fragment stage are separating themselves without being asked, which is exactly what Module 4 will make literal by giving each its own shader.

But there is a difference this time, and it is the reason Lesson 3.8 exists. Diffuse shading survives that split: Lambert varies smoothly and slowly across a triangle, so evaluating it at the corners and interpolating is a decent approximation. A highlight does not vary smoothly and slowly. It is the sharpest feature in the whole image, deliberately — that is what the exponent is for — and evaluating a sharp feature at three points and drawing straight lines between them is the chord-under-a-curve error this course has now met three times (2.4's sub-pixel bias, 3.2's affine texturing, and here).

7Common Pitfalls & Debugging

The highlight is on the wrong side of the object — everything else looks right
Symptom
The diffuse shading is correct — the lit side is the side facing the lamp — but the bright patch sits on the opposite side of the object from where it should. Orbiting moves it, so the view dependence is working; it just moves to the wrong place.
Cause
The mirror direction's sign. reflect(l, n) and mirror_direction(n, l) are exact negations (§3.1), and if you reach for the wrong one — or write 2·dot(n,l)·n + l, an easy slip — the highlight appears where the light came from rather than where it is going.
Fix
Use mirror_direction, and test it on the §3.1 worked example: n = (0,1,0), l = (0.6, 0.8, 0) must give (−0.6, 0.8, 0). The horizontal component flips and the vertical one does not. If both flip, you negated the whole thing; if neither flips, you added instead of subtracting. Switching to Blinn also makes it vanish as a class of bug, since halfway has no sign to get wrong.
A bright crescent glows on the dark side — light with no source
Symptom
A curved band of light sits on the unlit half of an object, brightest right at the terminator and fading into the shadow. It looks a little like a rim light, which is why it sometimes survives review.
Cause
The specular term is not multiplied by n·l. Classic Phong shading as published leaves it off (§3.6), and the halfway vector can point well above a surface whose normal has already turned away from the light. Measured: 0.608 of full strength on geometry the light cannot reach, over a band covering 400 of 701 unlit-but-visible normals.
Fix
Multiply the specular by the same clamped cosine the diffuse term uses. The derivation in §3.6 is the justification — the cosine law is about how much light arrives, and it does not care what the surface does with it. As a bonus this removes the need for a separate "zero the specular past the terminator" guard, because lambert() already returns zero there.
Switching Phong → Blinn makes everything look matte — or the reverse, chalky and blown out
Symptom
You change one enum and the whole scene's materials change character. Blinn looks washed out and over-broad, or Phong looks pinched and dull.
Cause
The exponent was not converted. dot(n,h) falls off at half the rate of dot(R,v) (§3.4), so the same number means a lobe about twice as wide in angle. The two exponents are not the same parameter wearing the same name.
Fix
Multiply by 4 going to Blinn, divide by 4 going to Phong — the demo's matched_shininess. Then re-tune, because 4× is a near-peak match and gets worse the broader the lobe: measured 4.38× at p = 4 converging to 4.01× by p = 128.
A hard curved edge cuts the highlight off — only on floors and walls, only sometimes
Symptom
The sheen on a large flat surface ends along a crisp arc with nothing beyond it. It comes and goes as the camera moves, and it never happens when you are looking toward the light.
Cause
Phong's cut-off (§3.5). When the light and the eye are on the same side of the normal — on a floor, when the sun is behind you — their angles sum past 90° and the eye falls outside the hemisphere Phong answers in, so dot(R, v) goes negative. (Not because the mirror ray points into the ground: it never does. §3.5.) The arc is the α = 90° boundary drawn across the surface. Measured on a plane: at a sun elevation of 35°, Phong puts a highlight on 0 of 30,806 lit pixels while Blinn covers all of them.
Fix
Use the halfway vector. This is not a tuning problem and cannot be tuned away — the cut-off is exact and the boundary is sharp. It is also the honest reason Blinn's model won, ahead of the arithmetic saving it is usually credited with.
The highlight flickers or vanishes as the object turns — worse on simple shapes
Symptom
A highlight pops in and out, or slides in jerky steps, or is a hard-edged polygon rather than a round spot. Making the surface shinier makes it worse. It looks fine on dense meshes and terrible on cubes.
Cause
Not a bug in the shading equation — a bug in where it is evaluated. Per-vertex evaluation samples a very sharp function at a few points and draws straight lines between them, so whether the peak is found at all is luck about vertex placement, and that luck changes as the object turns. Measured: cube.obj has no highlight at all in 157 of 180 frames of a rotation; the icosahedron in 107 of 180; the 48×24 torus in none.
Fix
Evaluate per pixel — interpolate the normal across the triangle and shade each fragment — which is Lesson 3.8. Tessellating harder is the wrong fix and Table 6 shows why: going from 1,225 to 4,753 vertices improved nothing at all.
The highlight is a flat white blob with no shape — and gets bigger, not brighter
Symptom
The centre of the highlight is a featureless patch of pure white with a hard boundary. Turning up the light makes the patch grow rather than intensify.
Cause
Clipping at 1.0. to_encoded clamps, and a specular term is the first thing in this engine that routinely produces values above 1.0 — measured up to 1.86 over a random sweep. Everything above white becomes white, so the shape of the peak is thrown away and only its footprint remains.
Fix
Nothing today; this is genuinely correct behaviour for an 8-bit framebuffer. The real answer is to render into a float target, keep the values above 1.0, and tonemap at the end — Module 6's HDR pipeline, which exists precisely for this. In the meantime, lower the specular colour or the light intensity so the peak lands near 1.0 rather than well above it.
The highlight does not move when the camera does — but the code has a to_eye in it
Symptom
There is a specular term, it produces a highlight, and orbiting the camera slides it not at all — or slides it much less than it should.
Cause
Three candidates, in decreasing order of likelihood. (1) to_eye is computed from a constant — the "infinite viewer" approximation, where v is taken to be the camera's forward axis rather than the direction to each point. It is a real and legitimate optimisation, but it removes exactly the variation you are looking for on nearby objects. (2) The eye position and the surface position are in different spaces: a world-space eye minus a model-space vertex is a vector with no meaning. (3) You are shading per vertex on a mesh too coarse to show the motion — check peak in the HUD, and re-read Table 7.
Fix
For (2), which is the one that hides best: put an assertion on the space. In this codebase the naming convention does the work — eye_world - world_pos[v] reads correctly and eye_world - view_pos[v] reads wrong, which is the whole point of the a_from_b discipline from Lesson 2.8.

8Exercises

3.7.1Predict the controlwarm-up

The violet plinth has specular{} — a black highlight. Before running anything, predict what the demo's vs other pixel count would do if you gave the plinth the icosahedron's {{0.85f, 0.85f, 0.85f}, shininess}. Then do it and check.

Second part, and the interesting one: the plinth is the only object in the scene with identity rotation and a non-uniform scale. Predict whether its highlight moves when you orbit. Predict whether it moves when you swing the light with A/D.

Hint

The plinth is a flat-topped box. Which of its faces can carry a highlight at all, given the light's fixed 40° elevation? And what did §3.8 say about where a highlight sits on a flat surface?

Solution

The count goes up — the plinth's faces now differ between the two models like every other surface. The highlight does move under both controls, because §3.8's formula p = e − R·(ey/Ry) contains both the eye and (through R) the light. But it moves in a very particular way on the flat top: as a single smooth patch sliding across, rather than wrapping around a curve. And it will be badly sampled, because the top face is two triangles with four corners between them and §4.7's Table 7 is about exactly this — the cube is the worst subject in the demo.

3.7.2Ship the classic bug, on purposeguided

Add a fourth specular_model — call it phong_classic — that is Phong's term without the incoming n·l factor, exactly as published in 1975. Put it on the H cycle, then find a camera and light arrangement where the crescent from §3.6 is unmistakable, and use the demo's comparison counter to measure how many pixels it costs.

This is the engine's ninth "keep the wrong thing behind a key" bargain. Write the comment that says why it is there.

Hint

The factor is applied in shade(), not in phong_term, so the new model needs the multiplication to be conditional. The cleanest change is to compute spec as now and then choose whether to multiply it by ir or by the un-cosined light. Watch out: the existing early-out is guarded on n_dot_l > 0, which is precisely the guard the bug needs to not have.

Solution sketch

The guard is the interesting part. To reproduce the bug faithfully you must let the specular through when n_dot_l ≤ 0, which means the early-out has to become model-dependent — and noticing that is the exercise. It is a small demonstration of something general: a correct implementation often has its correctness expressed as a control-flow shortcut, and reintroducing a bug means unpicking an optimisation that was only valid because the code was right.

Expect the crescent to be most visible with a low light and a camera roughly opposite it, on the torus (a smooth mesh has a long terminator). A broad exponent, p = 4 or less, spreads the leak over more of the surface.

3.7.3Normalise the lobe, and watch it failguided

Multiply the Blinn term by (m + 8) / (8π) (§3.7) and run the demo through the whole E range. Then answer three questions with numbers rather than impressions:

  1. At which exponent does the peak first clip to 255?
  2. How large is the clipped region at m = 256, as a fraction of the object?
  3. Is the picture at m = 4 better or worse than the unnormalised one, and why?
Hint

The factor is 0.4775 at m = 4 and 10.5042 at m = 256. One of those makes a rough surface dimmer, which is the correct behaviour and may not be the one you expected. The demo already reports the brightest channel on screen; a count of pixels at exactly 255 is a three-line addition to brightest_channel.

Solution sketch

The answer to (3) is "better, and it will not feel like it" — the normalised rough surface is dimmer because a broad lobe genuinely spreads its energy over more directions, and the unnormalised version was over-bright all along. The answer to (1) and (2) is that the high-exponent end becomes unusable: with a factor of 10.5 the peak is ten times white and the highlight is a flat clipped disc whose size, rather than whose brightness, encodes the material. That is the concrete argument for HDR: the normalisation is correct and an 8-bit target cannot carry it. Keep the change behind a flag and revisit it in Module 6.

3.7.4Shade in model spaceengine

§4.5 gave up a matrix multiply per vertex to get world positions. Take it back: transform the eye and the light direction into model space once per object, shade there, and keep the composed view_from_model for the position pipeline. Measure the per-frame cost both ways on the 1,225-vertex torus, and check that the rendered images are identical.

Hint

A position goes into model space through inverse(world_from_model). A direction does too. But a normal does not — and you already have that machinery from 3.6, so ask yourself what the model-space normal actually is before transforming anything. (It is the one the mesh stored. That is the whole saving.)

Solution sketch

The saving is real and larger than it looks: you also stop needing the normal matrix per vertex, because model-space normals are what the mesh already holds. The cost is one 4×4 inverse per object per frame, plus a genuine conceptual burden — every quantity in the shading is now in a per-object space, so a second light means transforming that one too, and a light count of n costs n transforms per object rather than none.

That last point is why real engines mostly do not do this any more: it is a win for one light and a loss for many, and it made more sense when vertex transforms were the bottleneck than it does now. Worth implementing once to feel the trade, and worth knowing why it fell out of fashion.

3.7.5A second lightopen

lighting holds one directional_light and an ambient constant. Give it several. Decide: a fixed array with a count, or a span? Does shade() loop over them, or does the caller call it once per light and sum? What happens to the ambient term — is it added once or once per light?

Then render a scene with a warm key from one side and a cool fill from the other, and look carefully at the two highlights.

Hint

The ambient question has an unambiguous answer once you say what ambient is. And for the shape: which choice would still work when Module 6 wants to sort draws by light count, and which one bakes an assumption into the shading function?

Solution sketch

Ambient is added once — it is a stand-in for the whole environment, not a property of any particular lamp, so adding it per light would scale a fudge by a number that has nothing to do with it.

For the structure: summing inside shade() over a span is the better shape, because the diffuse and specular terms both need the albedo and the surface parameters, and splitting the loop outward would recompute normalised(normal) and normalised(to_eye) for every light. Note what you have just built, though — a loop over lights inside the shading function is a forward renderer, and its cost is objects × lights. Module 6 names that and weighs it against the alternatives.

The two highlights are the payoff: a surface with a warm and a cool light shows two distinctly coloured specular spots, and it is the most convincing "this is a real material" cue the engine has produced so far.

9Recap & Next

What the engine can do now that it could not before

  • Draw a view-dependent highlight — the first shading term in this course that changes when you move your head, and the reason a surface reads as smooth and hard rather than as plaster.
  • Compute the mirror direction and the halfway vector, both derived rather than quoted, and both verified against each other over tens of thousands of random configurations.
  • Evaluate either Phong or Blinn-Phong, compare them at matched visual tightness, and say precisely where and why they differ.
  • Carry per-surface material parameters — a specular colour and a shininess — which is the first time this engine has had a parameter that belongs to neither the geometry, the light, nor the rasterizer.
  • Predict, in closed form, where a highlight will land on a flat surface — and confirm it against a brute-force search to within one grid cell.

Three ideas here will keep paying. The halfway vector as a question about the surface is what Module 6's microfacet theory is built on, and meeting it now means GGX arrives as a better answer rather than a new mystery. The incoming cosine belonging to every outgoing path is what makes a BRDF a BRDF. And the observation that a fast path can be bought out by a feature — the composed matrix that view dependence cost us — is the shape of most real performance work.

Next: 3.8 — Flat, Gouraud and Per-Pixel Shading. Everything in §4.7 was a measurement of the same defect: the shading equation is right and we are evaluating it in the wrong places. Three tables and 157 blank frames out of 180 are a more convincing argument than any assertion, and they are the argument 3.8 answers. It separates where the normal comes from from where the equation is evaluated — two axes that have been quietly tangled since 3.6 — and puts the highlight where it belongs: on the pixel.
