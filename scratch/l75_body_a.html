Module 7 — Motion: Rotation, Animation, Audio · Lesson 7.5

Slerp, and the Storage Swap

Three lessons have now derived the same three steps in three different algebras, and this one writes them down for the fourth time in three lines — because a·(a⁻¹b)ᵗ is not a fact about quaternions, it is the definition of a geodesic on any group that has an inverse, a product and a power. What is genuinely new is the half that has no analogue in the plane. The double cover means every pair of orientations has two arcs between it, one of them wrong, and half of all pairs name the wrong one; the fix is one comparison and the bug without it is a character spinning 359° between two keyframes a degree apart. Then the same fact pays a dividend nobody advertises: because that comparison forces the arc into one hemisphere, nlerp's schedule error — unbounded in the plane, where Lesson 7.3 measured it at a factor of 13,131 — is capped here at exactly 2, and at 8.1491° of pose. The thing that looked like a nuisance is what bounds the error. Everything is measured, and two of the measurements refused the claim they were written to confirm. The lesson's own metric had to be rewritten before anything could be measured at all: angle_between shipped in 7.4 as 2·acos|a·b|, which is the formula every reference gives and is blind near zero, and it scored slerp's own geodesic at −41.10% of its own arc. And a table of small-arc errors printed a clean 0.0000e+00 for a function that was returning NaN, because std::max(x, NaN) returns x. Then the swap this engine has promised since Module 2: transform::rotation becomes a quat, twenty-five call sites move, and the narrower type finds — as it found one in 7.4 — two more places that had never put a rotation in a field called rotation, one of them a published demo whose camera boom had been storing a sheared basis since Lesson 5.12.

1. The problem: a rotation you cannot blend

Here is a thing an engine has to do several thousand times a frame, and which this engine currently cannot do at all. A character's left elbow is at one orientation in keyframe 41 and a different one in keyframe 42. The clock says you are 37% of the way between them. Where is the elbow?

Try it with what transform holds today. Its rotation field is a mat3, so you have two matrices and a number, and the obvious thing to do with two matrices and a number is to average their entries:

\[ M(t) \;=\; (1-t)\,A \;+\; t\,B \]

That is not a rotation. It is not nearly a rotation, either — it is a matrix that shears and shrinks, and you can see how badly in one number. The determinant of a rotation is exactly 1, because a rotation preserves volume; the determinant of the entrywise average of the two poses this lesson's harness uses is 0.00007. Feed that to a vertex shader and the character does not merely bend oddly. It collapses to a plane.

Why this is not a fixable defect of matrices

You could Gram-Schmidt the average back into a rotation, and people do. The result is not the halfway pose — it is a pose, somewhere near the halfway one, arrived at by a procedure with no geometric meaning, whose error grows with the angle between the endpoints and which has no closed form you can reason about. The problem is not that the average is wrong. It is that the set of rotation matrices is not closed under addition, so there is nothing between two of them to find. Nine numbers is six too many, and the six extra are constraints, not freedom.

This is the last of the four costs Lesson 7.1 opened the module by listing, and it is the one that decided the storage. Lesson 7.2 could compose but not store cheaply; Lesson 7.3 proved in the plane that a number system does the job and that four components are forced in space; Lesson 7.4 built the four components and measured everything about them except the one operation the animation system actually needs. This lesson does that operation, and then makes the engine store the thing that can do it.

2. Building intuition: the shortest way round a sphere

Forget quaternions for a paragraph and think about the Earth. You are in London and you want to be in Tokyo, and you want to fly the shortest route and arrive at a steady speed. Two questions, and they are different: which way do I go, and where am I at each moment.

The first has a famous answer. The shortest path between two points on a sphere is the arc of the great circle through them — the circle you get by slicing the sphere with the plane that contains both points and the centre. Any other route is longer, and the reason is worth one sentence because the same sentence is going to do all the work later: the great circle is the only path that never turns. Every other route is curving within the surface as well as with it.

The second question is the one people skip. Having chosen the arc, you still have to say how far along it you are at time t, and "37% of the way" can mean 37% of the angle or 37% of something else. Those are different points, and telling them apart is most of §5.

2.1 Why a rotation is a point on a sphere

Lesson 7.4 gave us the object: a rotation of θ about the unit axis n̂ is the quaternion

\[ q \;=\; \cos(\theta/2) \;+\; \sin(\theta/2)\,\hat n \]

and its norm is 1, because cos²+sin²=1 and n̂ is a unit vector. So every rotation is a point at distance 1 from the origin in four-dimensional space — a point on the unit 3-sphere, exactly as every rotation of the plane was a point on the unit circle in Lesson 7.3. The London-to-Tokyo problem is not an analogy for what we are about to do. It is the same problem with one more coordinate.

And with one wrinkle that the Earth does not have, which §6 is entirely about: every orientation is two points on this sphere, q and −q, at opposite ends of a diameter. Imagine arriving in Tokyo and being told there is a second Tokyo on the far side of the planet that is also Tokyo. Which one you aim at changes your flight plan completely, and nothing in the destination's name tells you.

2.2 One formula, now in its third algebra

Lesson 7.2 built rotation_slerp for matrices and Lesson 7.3 built complex_slerp for the plane, and 7.3 pointed out — without cashing it in — that the two are the same three steps. Here they are for the last time, and the third column is this lesson's whole implementation:

\[ \operatorname{slerp}(a, b, t) \;=\; a \cdot \big(a^{-1} b\big)^{t} \]

Undo a; take t of the difference; redo a. Nothing in that sentence mentions a dimension, a representation, or a number of components. It needs an inverse, a product, and a way to raise something to a real power — and a unit quaternion has all three.

Lesson 7.2's rotation_slerp works on mat3 in a notation that has never heard of a quaternion, and 7.3's complex_slerp works in two dimensions on two floats. Run all three over the same journey and they agree to 4.172e−07 of a matrix entry and 2.384e−07 radians respectively — which is float resolution, three times over. Those two numbers are §7's instruments as much as they are its results: the new function is checked against two older ones that share no code with it and no code with each other.

The word "geodesic", earned

A geodesic is the straightest path a curved space allows. Lesson 7.2 introduced the word with matrices and measured what it buys: 0.00% of excess turning, against Euler-angle interpolation's +14% on a generic pair and +209% near gimbal lock. §7.2 of this lesson re-measures the same pose pair with quaternions and gets +14.10% for the Euler route — the same number, from a completely different representation, with a completely different metric. That agreement is not a coincidence and it is not a tautology: it is two implementations of "how much turning did this path actually do?" landing on the same answer.
