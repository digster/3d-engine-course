Module 8 — Physics · Lesson 8.2

Forces, Gravity, and Linear Rigid Bodies

Lesson 8.1 taught this engine to advance a state through time, and left it unable to tell a crate from a pebble. You hand integrate an acceleration and it believes you — which is enough to drop a cube and enough for nothing else, because an acceleration cannot be added up and does not know what anything weighs. Four systems that each want to push the same body will overwrite one another, and a thruster that shoves a pebble will shove a truck exactly as hard. The fix is one equation old enough to be furniture, F = ma, and the interesting part is everything the engine has to decide to make it usable: an accumulator rather than a setter, because forces add and Newton's second law is linear in F; the reciprocal of the mass rather than the mass, because the common case in a real scene is immovable and that is a clean exact zero where infinity is a NaN waiting to happen; and an impulse as a separate entry point, because a jump written as a force for one step is 23× higher at 30 Hz than at 144 while the same jump written as an impulse varies by 6%. Then two questions that look like pedantry until they cost a week. Where “one unit is one metre” stops being a convention: 9.81 is in metres per second squared, a world modelled at scale s runs every duration in it by √s, and that single square root is why miniatures look like miniatures, why film crews overcrank by 2.83× for a 1/8 model, and why “the jumping feels floaty” is a measurement rather than an opinion. And where a scaled parent quietly stops meaning it: a body integrated under a parent scaled by 2 falls at 2 g, a body merely turned under a non-uniformly scaled one falls at 1.5811 g, 63.435° off vertical, and a spinning parent passes every test a matrix is capable of answering while putting a force-free body 3.7963 m from the straight line it was obliged to travel. Along the way: why a sweep of a million masses finds 16% of them fail a round trip that seven hand-picked ones all passed, and what that costs; why damping and drag sit next to each other in the header with different names and produce identical pictures for three masses 1000× apart until one of them does not; and a budget in which the honest arrangement of gravity turns out to cost a float divide worth 15% of the whole update, more than 8.1's entire integrator step.

1. The problem: an engine that cannot tell a crate from a pebble

Lesson 8.1 ended with a working stepper and a deliberately small type. A motion is a position and a velocity, and integrate advances one under an acceleration you supply. That is genuinely the heart of a physics engine, and everything in this module runs through it. It is also, right now, unusable for anything but a demo — and the reason is not that it is missing features. It is that the interface is wrong in two specific ways, and both of them are interesting.

An acceleration cannot be added up. Picture a crate in a real scene for one frame. Gravity wants to pull it down. A conveyor belt under it wants to drag it sideways. Wind from a designer-placed volume wants to push it east. A player has just shot it. Air resistance wants to slow it. Each of those is a separate system that knows nothing whatever about the others, written by different people, running in whatever order the frame happens to run them. Every one of them wants to tell the crate what its acceleration is.

With a single acceleration field, the last one to write it wins and the rest of the physics silently does not happen. You will not get a crash, or a warning, or a wrong-looking number in a debugger — you will get a crate that ignores gravity on the frames the wind system happened to run last. That is a bug with no symptom except “it feels wrong sometimes”, which is the worst kind there is.

And an acceleration does not know about mass. Put the same thruster on a pebble and on a truck and, in 8.1's world, both accelerate identically, because the only thing the stepper was told is how fast the velocity should change. That is not a small inaccuracy; it is the absence of the entire concept a player is using when they decide whether something looks heavy.

You might reasonably propose to fix the second one at the call site — have the wind system pass F/m instead of F — and that proposal is exactly what makes the first problem visible. The division can only happen once, after everything has been added, or it is not the same arithmetic. So the two problems have one answer, and it is the oldest equation in the subject.

Sum the forces. Divide by the mass. Once. Everything in this lesson is a consequence of taking that sentence literally.

And then two questions that sound like pedantry and are not. 9.81 is a number in metres per second squared, and conventions.html §3 has said “one unit is one metre” since Module 2, back when nothing in the engine could tell the difference. This is the lesson where something can. §8 derives what a mis-scaled world actually does — it is a square root, and it is why miniatures look like miniatures — and §9 asks where a body's position lives, which turns out to be a restriction rather than a feature and to have three separate ways of going wrong.

2. Building intuition: a shove is not an acceleration

Stand behind a shopping trolley and push it. Push exactly as hard on an empty one and on one full of bricks. What is the same in those two pushes, and what is different?

What is the same is you. Your arms are doing the same thing; the sensation in your shoulders is the same; if there were a scale between your hands and the handle it would read the same number. What is different is everything that happens next. The empty trolley leaps away; the full one grudgingly creeps. The quantity that was the same is the force, and the quantity that differed is the acceleration, and the thing that converted one into the other is the mass.

That is genuinely all Newton's second law says, and stated this way it is almost a tautology: we define force as the thing that is the same in the two pushes. What makes it a law rather than a definition is the second half — that forces add. If a friend pushes the trolley from the side while you push from behind, the trolley does not choose one of you. It goes in the direction of the sum of the two pushes, and the sum is the ordinary vector sum you would draw with a ruler. Nothing about that is obvious in advance; a universe in which the second push had half its effect while the first was already acting would be perfectly imaginable, and would make physics engines far harder to write.

It is worth sitting with how much the engine gets for free from that one property. Because forces add, gravity does not need to know that wind exists. Because forces add, the order of the four systems above does not matter. Because forces add, there is no interface to implement, no registry to sign up for, no visitor to write — the crate has a vec3, and everything that wants to push it does +=. An engine's simplest subsystems are the ones whose physics is linear, and this is the cleanest example in the whole course.

Now the other half of the picture, and it is the one people find genuinely strange the first time. Drop a hammer and a feather. On Earth the hammer wins, which everybody knows; on the Moon, famously, they land together, which David Scott demonstrated on camera in 1971 for exactly this reason. If mass converts force into acceleration, and the hammer has more mass, why does more mass not mean less acceleration under gravity?

Because gravity is the one force that scales with the mass it is pulling on. The hammer is a hundred times heavier, so gravity pulls on it a hundred times harder, so the two hundreds cancel and every object falls at the same rate. It is the only force in this lesson with that property, and it means gravity is simultaneously the most familiar force and the least representative one — the intuition you have built over a lifetime of watching things fall is intuition about the one case where mass does not matter. §5 makes that a design decision and §6 shows what happens when a second force turns up and the cancellation stops.

3. F = ma, and the accumulator that makes it usable

Write the law down, in the direction the engine actually needs it:

\[ \mathbf{F} = m\,\mathbf{a} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \mathbf{a} = \frac{\mathbf{F}}{m} = \mathbf{F}\cdot\frac{1}{m} \]

Force equals mass times acceleration; so acceleration is the total force divided by the mass, or — as the engine will actually spell it — the total force multiplied by the reciprocal of the mass.

The total force. That word is doing all the work, and it is what turns one equation into a piece of architecture. A body carries a vec3 that starts each step at zero; every system that wants to push adds to it; and at the moment the step runs, that vector is divided by the mass exactly once. Nothing else in the design has to exist.

3.1 A worked sum, with four real systems

Take a 2 kg body and let four systems have their say, in whatever order the frame runs them. Gravity contributes its weight, m·g — 2 kg at 9.81 m/s² is 19.62 N, downward. A thruster pushes 14 N along +x. A wind volume contributes (−4.5, 1.25, 3.0) N. A crude drag model contributes (−2.25, 0.5, −1.5) N. Add them componentwise:

\[ \mathbf{F} = (0, -19.62, 0) + (14, 0, 0) + (-4.5, 1.25, 3) + (-2.25, 0.5, -1.5) = (7.25,\; -17.87,\; 1.5)\ \text{N} \]

and divide once by 2 kg:

\[ \mathbf{a} = (7.25,\; -17.87,\; 1.5) \times 0.5 = (3.625,\; -8.935,\; 0.75)\ \text{m/s}^2 \]

The four forces sum to 7.25 newtons east, 17.87 down and 1.5 forward; dividing by the two-kilogram mass gives an acceleration of 3.625, −8.935 and 0.75 metres per second squared.

Notice the y component: −8.935, not −9.81. The wind and the drag both had a small upward contribution, and the body is now falling slightly slower than gravity alone would have it. That number is the one thing you could not have got from a design where each system sets the acceleration, and it is what the accumulator buys.

Just a body that this frame has decided is in space.

3.2 Why the accumulator is cleared after the step, not before

A force is an input to a step, not a property of a body. Next frame every one of those systems will speak again; what the crate “has” is a velocity and a position, and what it was handed was a force. So the accumulator has to be emptied once per step, and the single most common bug in a first physics engine is forgetting — which presents as objects that accelerate away forever after being touched once, because a thruster fired for one frame keeps firing for the rest of the level.

Less obvious is when. Clearing at the start of the step is the arrangement that first occurs to you and it is wrong in a way you will not notice for months: every tool that wants to ask “what was pushing on this body?” then reads zero. A debug overlay drawing force arrows draws nothing. An assertion checking that nothing exceeded a sane magnitude has nothing to check. Lesson 8.10's solver, which reads what the contact generator asked for, reads an empty vector. step therefore clears the accumulator last, after the integration it fed, so that anything looking at a body between two steps sees the forces that produced the state it is looking at.

3.3 The measurement: a is proportional to 1/m, exactly

Section A of the harness pushes three bodies with the same 50 N and steps them once at 60 Hz: 

    mass      inv_mass        a       v after 1 step
      1.0 kg  1.000000   50.0000      0.833333 m/s
     10.0 kg  0.100000    5.0000      0.083333 m/s
   1000.0 kg  0.001000    0.0500      0.000833 m/s

  A.1  m*a recovers the force at every mass:
           1.0 kg -> m*a =    50.0000 N   (F = 50 N)
          10.0 kg -> m*a =    50.0000 N   (F = 50 N)
        1000.0 kg -> m*a =    50.0000 N   (F = 50 N)

  A.2  v(1 kg) / v(1000 kg) = 999.9999  (expect 1000)

  A.3  CONTROL same mass, same push: identical  yes

A.1 is the check worth having: multiplying the acceleration back by the mass must return the force you put in, at every mass, and it does. A.2 is the same statement as a ratio, and it reads 999.9999 rather than 1000 for the reason §5 is about — 1/1000 is not exactly representable in float, so neither is the number you get back by dividing into it. That is a one-part-in-ten-million discrepancy and it is not nothing; hold on to it.

3.4 …and the one way in which forces do not commute

Vector addition is commutative and associative. Floating-point addition is commutative and not associative, and since an accumulator is a running sum, the order in which systems call add_force can in principle change the answer. Section B tries all 24 orderings of four forces chosen to be awkward: 

  B.2  same four forces, 24 orders, x component only
         [1e6, -1e6, 0.125, 0.0037] N
    24 orders   lo 0.125000000   hi 0.128700003
    spread 3.7000e-03 = 2.9% of the answer
    the smallest of the four forces is 3.7000e-03

  B.3  CONTROL exactly representable, partial sums exact
         [4, 2, 1, 0.5] N
    24 orders   lo 7.500000000   hi 7.500000000
    spread 0.0000e+00 = 0.0% of the answer

Read the two numbers in B.2 together: the spread across orderings is 3.7000e−03, and the smallest of the four forces is 3.7000e−03. They are not similar, they are the same number. What happened is not a gradual loss of precision; it is that in some orderings the fourth force was added to a partial sum of a million, where one ulp is 0.0625, and it vanished completely. Whether a whole force applies at all came down to which system ran first.

B.3 is the control, and it is written against the second half of 7.8's rule — ask what the check would say if the thing were completely fine. Four forces that are exact powers of two have exact partial sums in every order, and the spread is exactly zero. So B.2's spread is a fact about those particular values and not a fact about addition, which is the distinction that makes it actionable.

Two honest conclusions, and they point in different directions. For a normal game this is a non-issue: real forces on a real body are within a few orders of magnitude of each other and the spread is a last-bit affair. For a deterministic game — a replay, a lockstep multiplayer session, a golden-image test — it is fatal, because bit-identical is the requirement and a differing last bit compounds. The mitigation is not a smarter sum; it is that the accumulator is a field written by systems in a fixed order fixed by the code, not a list summed in arrival order. Our design already has that property, and it is worth knowing that it is a property rather than an accident.

4. Inverse mass, and the question it does not answer

Every physics engine you will ever read stores 1/m rather than m, and the reason usually given — “it turns a divide into a multiply” — is the least of it. A divide is about four times a multiply on modern hardware and it happens once per body per step; nobody restructures a public API for that. There are three real reasons, and they get better as they go.

@@FIG_INVMASS@@

4.1 Immovable is representable, and it is the common case

Count the bodies in a real level. The floor is immovable. So is every wall, every staircase, every rock, every piece of static geometry an artist placed — which is to say, most of the bodies. Their mass is not large. It is infinite, in exactly the sense that no force you can apply will accelerate them, and inv_mass = 0 says so precisely, in a float, with no sentinel value and no branch anywhere.

Store the mass instead and you need either a magic value or a parallel flag. The magic value is HUGE_VALF, and it works right up until two of them meet: 

  F.2  two immovable bodies:
       mass_of  = inf and inf
       m_a - m_b        = nan   (NaN: yes)
       inv_a + inv_b    = 0.000000   (the solver's term)
       1/(inv_a+inv_b)  = inf   (reduced mass)

inf − inf is NaN, and a NaN in a physics engine is not a local problem — it propagates through every subsequent operation, out through the position, into the transform, and onto the screen as an object that has disappeared. The inverse masses subtract to a clean zero.

4.2 The solver wants this quantity, and not mass

This is the reason that will look like foresight in eight lessons' time. Every contact in 8.9 and every joint in 8.11 computes an effective mass for the pair, and it is built from the sum of the inverse masses:

\[ m_{\text{eff}} = \frac{1}{\frac{1}{m_a} + \frac{1}{m_b}} = \frac{m_a m_b}{m_a + m_b} \]

The effective mass of a colliding pair is one over the sum of their inverse masses, which is also the product of the masses over their sum.

That quantity is the reduced mass, and it has a physical meaning: it is the mass a single body would need to have for the pair's relative motion to look the same. Written in terms of masses it needs a special case at every infinity; written in terms of inverse masses it is 1/(w_a + w_b) and a fixed body simply contributes nothing to the sum, which gives exactly the right answer — a crate bouncing off a wall behaves as though only the crate has a mass, which is what happens. The storage is chosen to match the arithmetic the hot loop will actually perform, and the hot loop has not been written yet.

4.3 The degenerate case moves somewhere harmless

With mass stored, mass = 0 is a divide by zero on the hot path. With the reciprocal stored, a massless body is inv_mass = ∞: still nonsense, but nonsense that does not divide, and nonsense that can be refused at the one place it can be created. 

  F.3  set_mass(0) refused   set_mass(-2) refused
       set_mass(inf) accepted
       inv_mass after inf = 0.0

set_mass is written as if (!(kilograms > 0.0f)) rather than if (kilograms <= 0.0f), and that is the one place in the file where the negated comparison is load-bearing: NaN <= 0 is false, so the second form lets a NaN mass straight through, while !(NaN > 0) is true and catches it. A negative mass is refused too, and not out of fastidiousness — a body with negative mass accelerates towards a push, which breaks assumptions the solver will make from 8.9 onward in ways that are essentially impossible to trace back to here.

4.4 …and now the question inverse mass does not answer

“Can a force move it?” is answered by inv_mass, completely. There is a second question that looks like the same one and is not: “does it move at all?”

A lift is immovable by forces — a crate landing on it must not push it down, however heavy the crate is — and it also moves, because that is its entire job. A floor is immovable by forces and does not move. Both have inv_mass = 0 and they are not the same kind of object, so the bottom half of figure 2 is a 2×2 rather than a switch, and three of its four cells are the three values of body_kind:

does not movemoves
a force cannot move it fixed — the ground, a wall kinematic — a lift, a door
a force can move it nothing is this dynamic — a crate, a ragdoll

Conflate the two and you get the two classic bugs, one from each direction. Make the lift dynamic and very heavy, and it sags under load and — as we are about to measure — drifts downward under gravity anyway. Make it static and teleport it every frame, and anything standing on it is left behind, because a body with no velocity imparts none to whatever it is carrying.

A C++ note in passing, because the enumerator name is odd. Every other engine calls the first of those static, and static is a keyword — it cannot be an enumerator even inside an enum class, where the name is otherwise scoped and cannot collide with anything. Ours is fixed, and the doc comment says the other word so that somebody searching for it lands in the right place.

4.5 “Just make the floor very heavy”

This is the proposal that every engine receives, and it deserves a measurement rather than an argument. Section F drops a dynamic body of one million kilograms beside a ten-gram pebble for one second: 

  F.4  CONTROL a 1,000,000 kg DYNAMIC 'floor', 1 s:
       y = -4.986749 m   a 10 g pebble: y = -4.986749 m

  F.1  fixed body, 10 s of gravity + 10 kN:
       position 0.0  velocity 0.0   moved: no
@@FIG_HEAVY@@

The two numbers are identical to the last decimal place, and figure 3 draws them as two arrows of exactly the same length. A hundred million times the mass buys precisely nothing, because gravity does not ask what anything weighs — which is §2's hammer and feather arriving as a line in a test log. Making something heavy makes it hard to push, and the floor's problem was never that somebody was pushing it.

inv_mass = 0 is not a very large number. It is a different kind of answer, and F.1 is what it buys: ten seconds of gravity plus a ten-kilonewton shove, and the position and velocity are still exactly zero. Not “small”. Zero, because x · 0 is zero and there is nothing for the arithmetic to round.
