# Linux / macOS
cmake -S . -B build -DCMAKE_BUILD_TYPE=RelWithDebInfo
cmake --build build -j

# Windows (MSVC)
cmake -S . -B build
cmake --build build --config RelWithDebInfo

Then the harness:

sh scratch/build_verify_617b.sh                                # as configured
ENGINE_CFLAGS="-O2 -DNDEBUG" sh scratch/build_verify_617b.sh   # for §J's timing

§J's timing line means something only when both the harness and libengine.a are optimised. ENGINE_CFLAGS optimises the harness; the library is optimised by the CMAKE_BUILD_TYPE above. Configure without a build type and the library is built unoptimised: the same run then prints about 3.9 ms and 2.7 ms where a release library gives 0.72 and 0.35.

./gltf_view --lights --lamp-shadows 0  # the same light, with no occlusion

15Common pitfalls & debugging

A spot light lights the ceiling instead of the floor

Symptom: the pool of light is on the wrong side of the lamp, or nowhere at all.
Cause: direction is the direction the light travels — from the lamp toward what it lights — the same convention as directional_light since 3.6. Computing it as position − target points it backwards.
Fix: normalised(target − position). The demo's spot does exactly this.

An imported lamp is a million times too bright, or invisible

Symptom: a glTF scene's lamps blow the image to white, or contribute nothing.
Cause: glTF gives punctual intensity in candela and directional in lux, and the engine's units are exposure-normalised so that the sun's default is π.
Fix: one exposure factor, applied to both kinds (Exercise 6.17b.2). Because candela at one metre is lux, the same factor keeps a scene's lamps and sun in proportion.

A faint ring on the floor at each lamp's range

Symptom: a circle, barely visible, where the light ends.
Cause: a hard cutoff, or the unsquared window, whose slope jumps from twice the inverse square's to zero at the edge (§4.4).
Fix: square the window. If the ring persists with the squared window, the range is too short for the intensity: the light is still visibly bright where it is being forced to zero.

A point light's shadows fall on the wrong side of their casters

Symptom: shadows that look plausible but are mirrored about the centre line of each cube face; worse near face centres than you would expect from any bias problem.
Cause: the face cameras were built with look_at, which can only build rotations. The face the hardware reads is a reflection (§7.2).
Fix: fit_cube_face, rows (u, −v, −major). Test it the way §E does: push a direction through the camera and through direction_to_cube and require the same texel.

Shadows detach from thin casters, and only far from the lamp

Symptom: a fence or a sign has a lit gap at its base that widens with distance from the lamp; solid objects look fine.
Cause: 6.8's constant device-depth bias reused under a perspective map. It is right at n·f/1 m and too large beyond it, in proportion to distance (§6.3).
Fix: convert the bias through the depth curve per fragment. And test with a thin caster — a solid one will not show it (§6.6).

A square of acne directly under a point light

Symptom: speckled self-shadowing in a patch under the lamp, nowhere else.
Cause: the −Y face sees the floor at constant axial depth, the derived slope is exactly zero, and float rounding decides the comparison (§6.5).
Fix: the rounding floor, k_perspective_depth_floor. Not a larger constant bias: that brings back §6.3's problem everywhere else.

Acne on the GPU only, across both pools, away from any edge

Symptom: the CPU image is clean and the GPU image has stripes.
Cause: the bias reach was sized for a nearest-texel comparison, and the GPU's linear comparison sampler reads four texels (§9.1).
Fix: (r + 1)√2 on the GPU. If you change the sampler to nearest, change the reach back — the comment says which filter it is sized for.

The CPU's lamp shadow map is wrong, and no bias fixes it

Symptom: acne over most of a large floor under a spot; reading the map shows depths that do not match the geometry.
Cause: near-clipped vertices landing beyond ±8,000 px and being clamped (§8) — or, on a guard-clipped floor, the half-texel slide from vertex snapping (§9.2).
Fix: guard-band clipping, and the extra half texel of reach under perspective. clip_stats::guard_clipped tells you whether the first is happening.

Light leaks in thin lines along a point light's 45° seams

Symptom: hairline lit streaks through a shadow, radiating from the lamp.
Cause: the lookup rejects points that project a hair outside the face that owns them, calling them lit (§7.4).
Fix: under perspective, clamp to the face instead of rejecting.

Culling front faces in the shadow pass makes a point light's acne worse

Symptom: 6.8's third acne cure works for the sun and spots, and makes cube shadows worse.
Cause: a cube face's camera is a mirror, so screen-space winding reverses, and "front" and "back" swap.
Fix: flip the cull mode for cube faces. The engine does not do this for you: both shadow passes cull nothing by default, which is why the engine never needed to.

SDL asserts "Missing fragment storage buffer binding", or the shader reads garbage lamps

Symptom: an assertion at draw time, or lamps with random positions and colours.
Cause: the storage buffer bound at slot 8 because the shader says t8; or no buffer bound at all because there were no lamps; or a count that describes a different list from the buffer.
Fix: slot 0 — each resource kind counts from zero on the C++ side (§10.6). Let the renderer bind its one-record fallback when there are no lamps, and let it set the count.

The spot's shadow matrix is transposed on one backend

Symptom: the spot shadow is right on one API and garbage on another.
Cause: a float4x4 member in a structured buffer, whose packing is exactly where compilers and cross-compilers disagree.
Fix: store the matrix as four float4 rows and take four dot products, as gpu_local_light does.

16Exercises

1 — From a bulb's lumens to an intensity (guided)

A 60 W incandescent bulb emits about 800 lumens. Compute its intensity as a point light in candela; the illuminance it delivers 2 m away, square-on; its intensity as a spot light under Frostbite's masking convention, I = Φ/π; and its intensity as a focusing spot with a 30° cone. Then write intensity_from_flux(flux, kind, outer_angle, focusing) and check it against your hand arithmetic.

Solution sketch: as a point, 800/(4π) = 63.7 cd, and 63.7/2² = 15.9 lux at two metres. As a masking spot, 800/π = 254.6 cd — four times the point's, and independent of the cone. As a focusing spot, 800 over §5.3's 0.8418 sr = 950 cd, and it doubles if you halve the cone's solid angle. Which convention an editor's "lumens" slider uses is a choice, and the engine stores I either way — so the conversion belongs in the importer or the editor, never in the shader. Watch for: lumens and candela are photometric; converting them into the engine's exposure-normalised units needs Exercise 2's factor too.

2 — Import KHR_lights_punctual (engine challenge)

The engine's glTF loader (engine/src/gfx/gltf.cpp, 6.6) ignores lights. cgltf already parses them: cgltf_data::lights holds cgltf_light records with color[3], intensity, type (cgltf_light_type_directional, _point, _spot), range, spot_inner_cone_angle and spot_outer_cone_angle, and a node that carries one has a non-null cgltf_node::light. Produce a std::vector<local_light> and an optional directional_light from a file, with one exposure factor k applied to every intensity.

Hint: position and direction come from the node's world transform (cgltf_node_transform_world), and a glTF light shines down its node's local −Z. Solution sketch: position is the matrix's translation column; direction is the matrix applied to (0, 0, −1, 0), normalised — already a direction of travel, which is the engine's convention. glTF's undefined range means infinite, which is the engine's range ≤ 0; the cone angles map across unchanged, including their defaults. For k, pick the value that maps the file's sun (in lux) to π, or 1/1000 if there is none, and log it — the same factor then keeps candela lamps in proportion, because a candela at one metre is a lux. Watch for: a scaled node scales the direction vector too; normalise after transforming.

3 — Bind the frame constants once (guided)

§10.8's corrected rule says a partial SDL_BindGPUFragmentSamplers writes only the slots it names, and bindings reset only when a pass ends. Change the renderer to bind slots 2–7 once per pass, after begin_frame, and only slots 0–1 per draw. Count the binding events in the frame log before and after, and prove the picture did not change.

Solution sketch: SDL_BindGPUFragmentSamplers(pass, 2, binds + 2, 6) once, SDL_BindGPUFragmentSamplers(pass, 0, binds, 2) per material change. On the harness scenes that saves six descriptor writes per draw — a few dozen a frame, which is honest about how little it buys here and how much it would buy with a thousand draws. The proof is §I's comparison: 0 of 196,608 channels. Watch for: every pass that draws must bind its own frame constants — a shadow pass, then the scene pass, is two passes and two binds — and a stale binding from a previous pass is exactly what the old, wrong explanation feared.

4 — PCF across cube seams on the CPU (engine challenge)

Point lights default to PCF radius 0 because the CPU's six maps cannot filter across a face edge. Make a radius-1 kernel work on the cube: take each tap as a direction, not a texel, and let direction_to_cube choose the face for it. Then compare against the GPU in §G's instrument.

Hint: for the fragment's face, a tap one texel over is the direction major + (sc + 2dx/res)u + (tc + 2dy/res)v — which, past the edge, belongs to the neighbouring face, and the lookup will say so. Solution sketch: each tap then compares against the depth of its face, computed from its own major axis; the reach grows to (1 + 1)√2 plus the snap's half texel, and the GPU needs the same radius with the same direction-space taps, since its one tap is already 2×2. Expect the 259 edge disagreements of §G to change and not to vanish — the two filters are still different filters.

5 — A spot wider than its shadow map (engine challenge)

fit_spot caps a spot's shadow camera at 80° from the axis (k_max_spot_shadow_angle, §6.2). Give a spot an outer angle of 85° and look at the ring between 80° and 85°: the cone lights it, and it lies outside the map. Find out what each renderer does there, make them agree, and decide what is right.

Solution sketch: they disagree. The GPU's spot_shadow_visibility rejects anything outside the map as lit; the CPU's perspective branch clamps to the map's edge first — right for a cube face, which owns every direction it is asked about (§7.4), and wrong here, which is why the comment above that clamp, saying it only touches points the cone has already zeroed, holds only up to 80°. The quick fix makes the spot path reject, as the GPU does: a ring of light with no shadow in it. The right fix gives such a spot six faces, as a point light has, and lets its cone do the masking — a point light's shadow under a spot's light. Watch for: the budgets then count a wide spot as a point, and prepare has to say so.

17Recap & next

The engine has lamps. A local_light is a point or a spot with an intensity in the sun's own units, so a lamp of intensity π at one metre is the engine's sun; it falls with the inverse square — derived from a sphere and measured as the same power through three of them — to zero at its range through a window that meets zero without a crease, and a spot masks that light with a cone ramped in cosine. Both renderers draw them, through one BRDF lifted out of shade() without moving a pixel of the reference render.

Each lamp can cast a shadow: a perspective map for a spot, six mirrored cameras into a cube for a point. Carrying 6.8's bias to a camera with a position exposed the three things an orthographic derivation could not see — the depth curve, the axial slope sin α cos φ / cos θ, and the knife edge where that slope is exactly zero — and a pixel-by-pixel comparison of two renderers found each of them, plus two different reasons for one reach. Along the way a Module 3 clamp that moves geometry became guard-band clipping. The GPU hands the fragment stage a light list sized by the scene through its first storage buffer, and the frame graph schedules the shadow passes — or, for a frame that declares none, reuses last frame's maps.

Next: 6.18 — Text and 2D Overlay Rendering. The engine can light a room now and still cannot put a single character on the screen: every number this lesson measured went to a terminal. 6.18 rasterizes glyphs with stb_truetype, packs them into an atlas, and composites them over the frame — in linear light, which turns out to be the part that decides whether text looks thin or fat — so the picture and the measurement finally share a surface.
