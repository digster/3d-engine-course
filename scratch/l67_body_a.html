Module 6 — Advanced Rendering · Lesson 6.7

Normal Mapping and the TBN Derivation

Every surface this engine has ever drawn is exactly as flat as its triangles. The fix is not more geometry — it is to lie about the normal, per pixel, from an image. But a stored direction is meaningless without a frame to express it in, and finding that frame is the whole lesson: T and B are whatever two vectors make the triangle's edges and its uv deltas agree, which is two equations in two unknowns and nothing more mysterious. Along the way the engine finally answers a question Lesson 6.6 had to walk away from — is this image colour, or is it data? — and the answer turns out to have been sitting in the GPU path since Lesson 4.7, with no counterpart on the CPU side at all.

1The Problem: Flat Is All We Have

Run gltf_view --model torus.obj as Lesson 6.6 left it. The torus is smooth, lit, and — if you look at it for a moment — completely characterless. It is a shape, not a surface. There is no grain, no pitting, no weave, nothing that says what it is made of.

The engine has exactly one lever for that, and it is the wrong one: add triangles. Lesson 3.6 established that shading is a function of the normal, Lesson 3.8 made it per-pixel, and Lessons 6.2 through 6.4 built a physically-based BRDF on top — but every one of those consumes a normal that came from the geometry. To make a surface look bumpy, the geometry has to be bumpy.

Price that. The torus is 2,304 triangles and reads as smooth at arm's length. To give it visible surface detail — a fabric weave, say, at a millimetre scale — you would need triangles at a millimetre scale, which on an object this size is tens of millions. For one prop. The vertex buffer alone would be hundreds of megabytes, and the rasterizer would spend all of it on triangles smaller than a pixel, which is the worst case for every renderer ever built.

@@FIG_PROBLEM@@

So: do not add the geometry. Add the normal. Shading only ever consults the normal, so a surface that reports a bumpy normal will shade exactly as though it were bumpy — and reporting takes one texture lookup instead of ten million triangles.

What this trick cannot do, said up front

A normal map lies to the shading and to nothing else. The silhouette stays smooth, because the silhouette is geometry. Bumps do not occlude one another, because occlusion is geometry. Nothing casts a shadow onto anything else on the same surface, and at a grazing angle the illusion collapses entirely — you are looking along a flat plane that claims to be rough.

Those are real limits with real names, and each has a technique that addresses it: parallax mapping moves the sample point to fake the occlusion, and displacement mapping gives up and moves the vertices. Both are out of scope here. This is the ninety-percent picture, and the missing ten percent is the silhouette.

2The Idea, and Why It Needs a Frame

Storing a normal per texel sounds like the whole design, and it nearly is. The problem is which normal.

The obvious version — store the world-space normal directly — fails immediately, and it fails in a way worth understanding rather than just avoiding. A world-space normal map is correct for exactly one orientation of the object. Rotate the torus and every normal in the image is now wrong; the lighting slides around the surface as if the bumps were painted on the world rather than on the object. And the same map could never be shared between two objects, or tiled, or mirrored, because it encodes where the surface is rather than what it is like.

The insight the whole technique rests on

Store the normal relative to the surface, not relative to the world.

"Tilted a bit toward increasing u, and a bit toward increasing v" is a statement that stays true when the object rotates, that means the same thing on the left half of a model and the right, and that can be tiled across a wall. It is a statement about the material, which is exactly what a texture ought to be.

And now the whole difficulty of this lesson appears in one sentence. If the map says "tilted toward increasing u", the renderer has to know which way in world space u increases — and that is not something the geometry has ever been asked before. A mesh knows where its vertices are and which way its normals point. Nowhere does it record the direction, in metres, that you would walk to make u go up.

That direction is the tangent, conventionally T. Its partner, the direction of increasing v, is the bitangent, B. Together with the normal N they form an orthonormal frame — the TBN basis — and a normal map's three numbers are coordinates in it.

A naming annoyance worth clearing up once

You will see B called the binormal in older material, including some very good older material. It is a misnomer borrowed from differential geometry, where a curve's Frenet frame genuinely has a binormal. A surface does not have one; what it has is a second tangent. Bitangent is the correct word and the one glTF uses, and the only reason to know "binormal" is so that you recognise it in somebody else's code.

So the plan for the rest of this lesson has three parts, and they are genuinely independent: work out what the numbers in the image mean (§3), work out what the frame is (§4–§6), and get the frame from the vertex to the fragment through two renderers (§8–§9).

3Colour, or Data? The Question 6.6 Deferred

Lesson 6.6 read a glTF material's normalTexture slot, counted it, and did not load it. The reason it gave was one sentence, and it is time to pay for it:

Every texture in this engine before now has been an albedo — an authored colour, sRGB-encoded like every stored colour since Lesson 1.6 — so sample could decode unconditionally and be right every single time. A normal map breaks that. Its three channels are a direction, packed into bytes. Running a direction through the sRGB transfer function is arithmetic on numbers that were never a colour.

How wrong? One number settles it, and it is the number the whole section turns on.

@@FIG_SPACE@@

The byte 128 is what a flat normal map stores for x and y — "no tilt in either direction". Read as data it means 0.50196, which maps to a tilt of essentially zero. Read as a colour it means 0.21586, which maps to −0.568 — so the "flat" direction becomes (−0.568, −0.568, 1), and every surface in the scene is tilted 38.8°, in the same direction, everywhere.

And it does not look like a bug

38.8° of uniform tilt does not produce a black screen or a crash. It produces lighting that is subtly, consistently wrong — every surface behaving as though the light were somewhere slightly different from where it is.

The usual diagnosis is "this normal map was authored too strong", and the usual fix is to reduce its strength, which makes the picture less wrong without making it right. That is why this is worth a type rather than a comment.

Where the colour space belongs

Three places it could live, and this decision has to be made once, because both normal maps and the still-deferred metallic-roughness maps read through it. A per-call answer is two answers.

Three candidate homes for a texture's colour space, and the argument against two of them

Two halves of one idea, and no path between them

The GPU has had a colour space since Lesson 4.7 and the software renderer has never had the concept at all. In SDL_GPU the decode is declared by the texture's format — _UNORM against _UNORM_SRGB — and performed by the sampler. One image cannot be sRGB in one binding and linear in another, because the format is a property of the memory.

This is the same shape of gap Lesson 6.6 found between load_image and sample: one half complete, the other absent, and no code path crossing between them until a feature needed both. It is worth writing down as a habit — look for pairs of subsystems that ought to compose and have never been asked to.

And it settles the design without a preference: Lesson 3.9 built these types to mirror SDL_GPU's "so that when Module 4 replaces this file with a GPU sampler object, the change is a rename and not a re-education". Mirroring includes mirroring where a decision lives. It goes on the texture.

The whole cost of it is one branch, in the one function every read already goes through — which is the argument for putting it there rather than at the call site. There is exactly one fetch, so there is exactly one place the question can be asked, and no caller can forget to ask it:

The branch is on a value that is constant for an entire draw, so it predicts perfectly. And the default is srgb, which keeps every texture written before this lesson meaning exactly what it meant — a default that changed the answer would have made this lesson a re-baseline instead of an addition.

The space is part of the asset's identity

One consequence that falls straight out of Lesson 5.5. The same PNG read as colour and read as data are two different textures with two different texel meanings; they cannot share a cache entry, and a store keyed on the filename alone would hand the second requester the first one's decode and be quietly wrong.

That is exactly what mesh_import established for geometry — "these are part of an asset's IDENTITY, not arguments to a function" — arriving for a second type. So load_texture takes the space, the key carries it, and 5.5's rule that the default serialises to nothing applies unchanged:
