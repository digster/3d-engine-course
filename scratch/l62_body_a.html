Module 6 — Advanced Rendering · Lesson 6.2

Radiometry-Lite: What a BRDF Is

Lesson 6.1 settled what the numbers leaving the renderer mean at the output stage. This one asks what they are on the way there — and the honest answer, today, is that nobody knows, because shade() multiplies an albedo by a light and calls the product "the light leaving the surface" without a single unit anywhere in the sentence. We fix that, and the fix has a sting in it: there has been a π hiding inside the light's intensity field since Lesson 3.6, unlabelled, doing exactly the job the Lambert BRDF's π is supposed to do. Moving it into the open changes 154,240 floating-point results and not one pixel — and that empty diff is the proof, not the anticlimax.

1The Problem

Here is the line the engine has been shading with since Lesson 3.7. It is in light.hpp, it is correct, and it is about to be a problem:

Now answer three questions about it.

What are the units of the returned value? It is called "the light leaving the surface", which is not a unit. What are the units of intensity? The field's own documentation says a light's "brightness", which is not a unit either. Is this shading model energy-conserving? There is no way to even ask that question of the code above, because energy conservation is a statement about a ratio of two physical quantities and this expression contains none.

You might reasonably think this is pedantry. It is not, and the rest of Module 6 is the proof. Every remaining lesson in this module is about replacing the guess in that return statement with something better — microfacets in 6.3, Cook–Torrance in 6.4, image-based lighting in 6.15 — and every one of those is a claim about how much of the arriving light leaves in a given direction. You cannot check such a claim against an expression that does not know what arriving light is.

And there is something concrete hiding in there

By the end of §5 you will be able to show that intensity was never a free parameter. It was an irradiance divided by π — the exact constant the Lambert BRDF is supposed to carry, sitting on the wrong side of the multiplication with no name and no comment, since Lesson 3.6. This is not an unusual bug. It is the single most common thing to find when you first put units on a renderer someone else wrote, including your own from six months ago.

So this lesson buys three things. A vocabulary: four words for light, each with a unit, which is what lets the rest of the module be precise. A definition of a BRDF as a ratio, which is what makes "is this model physically plausible?" a question with an answer. And a test — a numerical integral over the hemisphere — that every BRDF from here on gets run through, starting with the one the engine already ships, which turns out to fail it in a way worth knowing about.

Almost nothing on screen changes. That is deliberate, and §8 makes the case that it is the strongest possible outcome rather than a disappointing one.

2Four Words for Light

Radiometry has a reputation for being a wall of Greek letters guarding three ideas. The reputation is deserved, and the way through it is to notice that the quantities are built from one another by division, in a chain, and that each division is answering a question the previous quantity could not.

2.1 Flux, irradiance, radiance

Start with a lamp. The most obvious thing to measure about it is how much light it puts out in total — every photon, in every direction, added up. That is radiant flux, written Φ, and its unit is the watt, because light is energy and flux is energy per second. A 40-watt bulb is a statement about flux.

Flux is useless for shading, and for a reason worth stating plainly: it is a property of the lamp, and shading is a question about a surface. A surface does not care how much light the lamp emits; it cares how much lands on it. So divide by area. How much flux arrives per square metre of surface is irradiance, written E, unit W/m². That is a property of the surface's situation rather than of the lamp, which is progress.

Irradiance is also not enough, and this is where most people's intuition stalls, so here is the failure that makes the next division necessary. Consider a white wall lit by a window. Every square centimetre of that wall has some irradiance — light from the window lands on it. Now put your eye somewhere and ask what colour you see. The wall sends light in every direction; your eye catches an extremely narrow slice of those directions. Irradiance has already thrown away which direction the light came from and which direction it leaves in, and those are exactly the two things shading is about.

So divide again, this time by solid angle — the amount of "directional space" a bundle of rays occupies. What you get is radiance, written L, unit W/(m²·sr): the light travelling in one direction, through one square metre of surface measured perpendicular to that direction, per steradian of directional spread.

@@FIG_QUANTITIES@@

Four words, then, and the fourth — radiant intensity, W/sr, flux per solid angle without the area division — is the one this course will barely use, because it describes point sources rather than surfaces. It is named here only so that when you meet it in a paper you know it is not radiance with a typo.

Worked example — the chain, with numbers

A lamp emits 12 W. All of it falls on a patch of floor 3 m² in area. The irradiance there is

E = Φ / A = 12 / 3 = 4 W/m²

Now suppose that light all arrives through a cone of 0.25 sr — a skylight, say, rather than the whole sky. The radiance along that cone is

L = E / ω = 4 / 0.25 = 16 W/(m²·sr)

Note what happened to the number: narrowing the cone made the radiance larger, with no more light involved. Radiance is a density in direction, and densities go up when you squeeze. Hold onto that; it is the same fact that lets a BRDF exceed 1 in §4.1. (This chain is asserted in verify_62 §A, with these numbers.)

2.2 The steradian, from the geometry

A steradian is to a sphere what a radian is to a circle, and if that sentence has ever felt like a definition rather than a restatement, here is the geometry it comes from.

An angle in the plane is measured by taking a unit circle around the vertex and asking how much arc the angle cuts off. The whole circle has circumference 2π, so a full turn is 2π radians. Nothing more mysterious than that. In three dimensions, take a unit sphere around the vertex and ask how much area the cone of directions cuts off. The whole sphere has area 4π, so all of directional space is 4π steradians, and a hemisphere is 2π.

Two consequences we need immediately. First, the differential element. Parameterise a direction by its polar angle θ from the normal and its azimuth φ around it. A small patch on the unit sphere spans dθ in one direction and, because a circle of constant θ has radius sin θ, only sin θ · dφ in the other. So

\[ d\omega = \sin\theta \, d\theta \, d\varphi \]

In prose, so this survives the CDN being unreachable: the solid-angle element is sine theta, times d-theta, times d-phi. The sine is there because the circles of constant θ shrink toward the pole; it is the same factor that makes lines of longitude converge at the north pole.

Second — and this is the one that matters for the π — light-transport integrals are almost never over plain solid angle. They are over solid angle weighted by cos θ, because of §3's projected-area argument. And the cosine-weighted hemisphere does not measure 2π. It measures π:

\[ \int_{\Omega} \cos\theta \, d\omega = \int_{0}^{2\pi}\!\!\int_{0}^{\pi/2} \cos\theta \sin\theta \, d\theta \, d\varphi = 2\pi \cdot \tfrac{1}{2} = \pi \]

In prose: integrating cos θ over the hemisphere gives 2π (from the φ integral) times one half (from the θ integral of sin θ cos θ), which is π. verify_62 §A computes this numerically and gets 3.1415939 on a 1024×2048 grid.

Keep that number. It is not a coincidence that it is π and it is not a coincidence that the Lambert BRDF has a π in it; §4.2 shows they are the same π, arriving by the same route, and Figure 5 draws the disc whose area it is.

2.3 Why a pixel holds radiance

Of the three quantities, exactly one can be stored in a pixel, and the reason is a small miracle of cancellation that is worth doing slowly, because it is the justification for the entire architecture of a renderer.

Put a small emitter of area A in front of a receiver, a distance d away, both facing each other. Move the receiver twice as far and ask what happens to what it measures.

The irradiance falls by four. That is the inverse-square law, and it is not mysterious either: the same flux is now spread over a sphere with four times the area. But the solid angle the emitter subtends at the receiver also falls by four — it is A/d², and d has doubled. Radiance is irradiance divided by solid angle, so the two factors of four cancel exactly and the radiance is unchanged.

@@FIG_INVARIANCE@@

That is radiance invariance along a ray, and here is what it buys. It means that "the radiance arriving at this pixel from that direction" equals "the radiance leaving that surface toward this pixel", with no distance term anywhere. A ray carries its own answer. Store irradiance in your framebuffer instead and every single shading operation would need to know how far away things are, and every compositing operation would be wrong.

The nine-tenths picture

Radiance is invariant in a vacuum, or near enough. Participating media — fog, smoke, atmosphere, skin, milk — absorb and scatter along the ray, and there radiance does change with distance. That is volumetric rendering, it is a large subject, and this course does not reach it. Everything in Module 6 assumes empty space between surfaces, which is the standard assumption of real-time rendering and is why fog in games is a hack applied afterwards rather than a consequence of the lighting model.

3The Cosine, Re-derived

Lesson 3.6 derived Lambert's cosine law from a spreading beam and left it there. This is the spiral's second turn, and it goes one step further in a direction that turns out to matter a great deal for how the code is organised: the cosine is a fact about projected area, and therefore it belongs to the light, not to the surface's material.

Take a beam with a fixed cross-section — a square metre of sunlight, say — and let it strike a surface. If the surface faces the beam square-on, that square metre of light lands on a square metre of surface. Tilt the surface by θ and the same beam now lands on a patch that is stretched in one direction, by exactly 1/cos θ. Same light, more surface, so each square metre gets less: a factor of cos θ less.

@@FIG_PROJECTED@@
Worked example — sixty degrees, both ways round

Tilt the surface to θ = 60°. The beam's footprint stretches by

1 / cos 60° = 1 / 0.5 = 2.0000

so the patch has twice the area and each square metre of it receives half the power. Now compute the same thing from the vectors, which is what the code does. With the surface normal n = (0, cos 60°, sin 60°) = (0, 0.5000, 0.8660) and the beam arriving straight down so that the direction toward the light is l = (0, 1, 0):

dot(n, l) = 0×0 + 0.5000×1 + 0.8660×0 = 0.5000

The two routes agree, and they are not the same computation: one measured an area with a cross product, the other took a dot product of two unit vectors. verify_62 §B runs both over a range of angles and reports agreement to 1e−6 — deliberately computing the area from actual vertices, so the check owes nothing to the formula it is checking.

So far this is Lesson 3.6 with better arithmetic. Here is the part that is new, and it is a statement about where code goes rather than about geometry:

The cosine is on the light's side

Nothing in the argument above mentioned what the surface is made of. A beam striking chalk at 60° and a beam striking chrome at 60° both deliver half the power per square metre. The cosine describes how much light arrives; what the surface does with it afterwards is a completely separate question, and it is the question a BRDF answers.

Which is why albedo * n_dot_l, written as one expression the way every introduction writes it, is a small act of vandalism. It fuses two independent facts into a product, and the fusion is precisely why the π has nowhere natural to live and ends up inside the light. Lesson 6.2's whole implementation is the un-fusing.

In the engine this shows up as a new function on the light rather than a new function on the material — directional_light::irradiance_on(normal), which returns E⊥ · cos θ and nothing else. It computes what the old code computed. Its value is that it names it.
