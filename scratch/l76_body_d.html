7. Implementation: four multiply-adds

Everything so far runs once per joint. This runs once per vertex, and on real content those differ by two to three orders of magnitude. That asymmetry is the whole reason the palette exists: it is a place to hoist work to where it is nearly free. 

vec3 sum{0.0f, 0.0f, 0.0f};
for (int k = 0; k < k_max_influences; ++k)
{
    sum = sum + blend_point(palette[inf.joints[k]],
                            v, inf.weights[k]);
}
out[i] = sum;

No branch on the weight being zero, and that is deliberate rather than lazy. A zero weight costs one multiply; a predictable branch costs about the same, and an unpredictable one costs far more. The index is always valid by construction (§6), so there is nothing to guard and the loop body is straight-line code a compiler can vectorise.

7.1 A point is not a direction, and w is why

Lesson 2.7 introduced the fourth coordinate as the answer to "is this a position or a direction", and this is the place it earns its keep for the second time. Positions get the translation column; directions do not: 

vec3 blend_point(const mat4& m, vec3 v, float w)      // …+ m.c3
vec3 blend_direction(const mat4& m, vec3 v, float w)  // …no m.c3

Skip that distinction and every normal is offset by wherever its joint happens to be standing. The harness's control does exactly that — skins the normals with skin_positions instead of skin_directions on a posed skeleton — and measures the worst tilt at 156.108°. That is not a subtly wrong surface; it is lighting computed from numbers that are not normals.

A control that could not fail, and how it announced itself

The first version of that control ran at the bind pose and reported 0.020°, which reads as "the two rules basically agree". They do agree there, necessarily: at the bind pose every palette matrix is the identity (§3.1), so its translation column is zero and the wrong rule gives the right answer. The check was passing because its fixture could not fail.

That is the second instance in two lessons of the same shape — 7.5's §G table printed a clean zero for a function returning NaN — and the two together sharpen into one question worth asking of every control you write: what would this fixture do if the thing being tested were completely broken? If the answer is "pass", the fixture is the bug.

7.2 Only two arrays change

A skinned mesh has five parallel arrays and a pose moves two of them. Texture coordinates do not care where the elbow is; a triangle list does not change when a character walks. So skin_into copies uvs, tangents and indices exactly once — when the destination is the wrong shape — and never again.

That is not a micro-optimisation, it is the observation the entire GPU path is built on. The static arrays live in a vertex buffer uploaded once; the pose arrives as a hundred matrices. §10 puts the ratio at 28.4× on this lesson's own fixture, and it grows with the mesh.

8. The candy wrapper, derived and measured

Now the artifact, and it is the reason linear blend skinning has a reputation. Twist a forearm against its elbow and the skin pinches. Twist it far enough and the limb becomes a wasp waist. Twist it 180° and it vanishes to a line.

That is not a bug in an implementation and it is not fixable by weighting. It is what averaging two rotations does, and it has an exact closed form you can derive with a ruler.

8.1 The derivation is one isoceles triangle

Two joints at the same place, one rotated θ from the other about the limb's own axis. A vertex weighted half to each. The blend sends it to the midpoint of R₀v and R₁v:

\[ v' \;=\; \tfrac{1}{2}\big(R_0 v + R_1 v\big) \]

Both R₀v and R₁v have length |v| — rotations preserve length — and they are θ apart. So the origin, R₀v and R₁v form an isoceles triangle with apex angle θ. The midpoint of the base lies on the bisector of that apex, and the bisector of an isoceles triangle meets its base at a right angle. That right triangle gives the answer immediately:

\[ |v'| \;=\; |v|\,\cos(\theta/2) \]
@@FIG_CANDY@@

Nothing in that derivation mentions skinning, a joint, or a matrix. It is a fact about averaging two points on a circle, and it is the same half-angle Lesson 7.3 found between two mirrors and Lesson 7.4 found inside a quaternion — arrived at here for a third independent reason.

The numbers you can check in your head: at 60° the radius is cos 30° = 0.866; at 120° it is cos 60° = 0.500 exactly; at 180° it is cos 90° = 0. Not "small". Zero. The limb collapses to a mathematical point, because the average of two antipodal points is the origin. 

   twist   measured   cos(t/2)    gap
     0.0   1.000000   1.000000   0.00e+00
    30.0   0.965926   0.965926   0.00e+00
    60.0   0.866025   0.866025   0.00e+00
    90.0   0.707107   0.707107   0.00e+00
   120.0   0.500000   0.500000   0.00e+00
   150.0   0.258819   0.258819   0.00e+00
   179.0   0.008727   0.008727   0.00e+00
   180.0   0.000000  -0.000000   8.74e-08
   worst gap over the sweep                   8.742e-08

The control is the same vertex weighted 1.0 to a single joint, which comes back at 1.000000 at the same 180° twist. A rigid vertex cannot collapse — so the artifact belongs to the blend and not to the twist, which is the distinction the whole section rests on.

8.2 What a forearm twist bone is

The formula also contains its own remedy, and it is the one every rigger already uses without necessarily having seen the cosine.

The collapse depends on the angle between two adjacent joints, not on the total twist. Spread a total twist θ over n joints and each adjacent pair differs by θ/n, so the pinch opens to

\[ \frac{|v'|}{|v|} \;=\; \cos\!\big(\theta / 2n\big) \]

At a 180° twist: 0.000 with one joint, 0.707 with two, 0.866 with three, 0.924 with four. That is what a forearm twist bone is — not a modelling nicety but a direct attack on a half-angle — and it is why a production rig carries considerably more joints than a skeleton does.

8.3 A bend obeys the same cosine, on an ellipse

A twist rotates about the limb's own axis, so the whole ring of vertices lies in the plane the collapse happens in and every one of them moves inward by the same factor. A bend rotates about an axis across the limb — and the vertices that lie on that axis do not move at all, because a rotation fixes its own axis.

So a bent ring does not shrink. It flattens: it becomes an ellipse with semi-minor axis r cos(θ/2) and semi-major axis r. Same cosine, spent on one direction instead of two.

@@FIG_BEND_ELLIPSE@@ 
   bend    min radius   r cos(d/2)   max radius
     0.0     1.000000     1.000000     1.000000
    30.0     0.965926     0.965926     1.000000
    60.0     0.866025     0.866025     1.000000
    90.0     0.707107     0.707107     1.000000
   120.0     0.500000     0.500000     1.000000
   worst gap on the minor axis                1.192e-07

That is why the two artifacts look so different in practice and are so often described as unrelated. A twisted forearm loses its whole cross-section — the candy wrapper. A bent elbow loses volume on one axis and keeps it on the other, which reads as a crease and a flat patch rather than as a pinch. One cosine, two geometries.

The demo prints both numbers, and they agree in both modes

rig's panel shows the smallest ring radius it can find alongside r cos(δ/2), where δ is the angle between the two joints that ring is weighted between. At a 90° bend spread over five joints: 0.37532 measured against 0.37532 predicted. At a 180° twist over two joints: 0.26870 against 0.26870. A prediction that survives being pointed at two different deformations is a prediction about the blend, which is what it claimed to be.

Two instruments that both reported a pinch that was not there

Measuring "how much did this ring shrink" looks trivial and took three attempts. The first two measured the radius perpendicular to the chain, which sounds obviously right: a ring on a bent limb is not perpendicular to anything else, so measure against the bone and the foreshortening cancels.

Version one had no bone to measure against at the tip ring — it is weighted entirely to the last joint, so there is no segment through it — and the axis fell back to world +y. On a limb bent 90° that ring is edge-on to +y, and the perpendicular radius read 0.241 instead of 0.380: a pinch reported at the one place on the tube where there are not two joints to pinch between. Version two clamped the axis to the last real segment and left a smaller copy of the same error — the tip ring is rigidly rotated one joint angle further than the segment below it, so its plane is tilted against the measuring axis, and at 120° it read 0.364.

The fix is to stop needing an axis. A rigid rotation does not change the distance between two points, so a distance from the ring's own centroid is invariant under every rotation the skeleton can apply and drops only when the blend actually pulls the vertices in. Both wrong versions reported a smaller number — which is the direction this measurement is hunting in, so both of them looked like a discovery.

9. Normals, and the rule we are not using

Lesson 3.6 established that a normal is not transformed by the matrix that transforms positions. It is transformed by the inverse transpose of the linear part, because a normal is defined by being perpendicular to the surface and a non-uniform scale does not preserve perpendicularity.

skin_directions uses the linear part anyway, and it is worth being exact about when that is correct and when it is not.

  • For a rotation the two rules are the same rule. R is orthonormal, so (R⁻¹)ᵀ = (Rᵀ)ᵀ = R. Measured over 4,000 random rotations, the worst chord between the two answers is 3.817e−07.
  • For a uniform scale they differ by a positive scalar, which a renormalise removes. Measured at a scale of 2.5: 0.000e+00.
  • For a non-uniform scale they diverge, and not gently.
   joint scale        normal error   d(N.L)
   (1.00, 1.00, 1.00)     0.0000 deg   0.0000
   (1.10, 1.00, 1.00)     5.4526 deg   0.0202
   (1.25, 1.00, 1.00)    12.6804 deg   0.0469
   (1.50, 1.00, 1.00)    22.6199 deg   0.0833
   (2.00, 1.00, 1.00)    36.8699 deg   0.1342

The last column is what the error costs rather than how big it is — the change in Lambert brightness for a light 30° off the surface — which is Lesson 7.5's rule about reporting consequences applied to a lighting quantity. A tenth of the shading range is not a subtlety.

The engine keeps the cheap rule, and the justification is not that the error is small: it is that non-uniform scale on a skinning joint is a rig defect rather than a renderer's problem to absorb. Joints rotate and translate; a rig that squashes one is describing a deformation that the four-influence linear blend cannot represent honestly anyway. So skeleton_report::nonuniform_binds counts the authored half of the problem at load time — the half a check can see — and §12's fourth exercise builds the second palette that would fix the other half, and measures what it costs.

10.1 The measurement that nearly did not mean anything

This is the first harness in Module 7 that has to link something. 7.1 through 7.5 link nothing — everything they test is inline in a header — so the -O2 on the harness's own command line covered every instruction they timed. Skinning is not header-only: anim/skeleton.cpp and anim/skin.cpp are real loops over arrays and live in libengine.a.

And cmake -S . -B build leaves CMAKE_BUILD_TYPE empty, which means the library is compiled with no -O flag at all. The first version of these numbers was: 

   palette for 64 joints      26.32 us
   skin 4840 vertices        744.86 us  (153.90 ns/vertex)

…which is 18.1× slower per vertex and 26.6× slower per palette, on the same machine in the same minute. 153.9 ns is about five hundred cycles to do four multiply-adds, and that implausibility is the tell: ARCHITECTURE.md §6's measuring rule says to ask whether a number is physically possible before believing it, and this one is not.

A flag reaches the translation units it is on, and no further

The rule in ARCHITECTURE.md — "-O2, never a debug build" — was written about harnesses, and for five lessons a harness was the whole program. The moment one links a library, the flag on its command line stops covering the code being measured, and nothing in the build says so: an unoptimised static library links exactly as quietly as an optimised one.

So build_verify_76.sh now refuses rather than measuring. It reads CMAKE_BUILD_TYPE out of the cache, prints which library it is about to link and at what build type, and exits with an error if there is no build type set — ENGINE_ALLOW_UNOPTIMISED=1 is how the slow row above was produced, deliberately. A measurement tool that cannot describe its own configuration is a measurement tool you have to remember things about.

10.2 Linear blend skinning is not nlerp without the normalise

There is a one-line summary of linear blend skinning that circulates widely and that this lesson was expecting to repeat: it is nlerp with the normalise deleted. Lesson 7.5 built quat_nlerp, which takes the straight chord between two quaternions and pushes it back onto the sphere; linear blend skinning takes the straight chord and does not. Same operation, one step missing.

Measured, that summary is wrong in both directions.

@@FIG_LBS_VS@@

It is worse than nlerp. Both get the schedule wrong — they move too fast through the middle of the blend — but they are wrong by the same function evaluated at different arguments. nlerp's error is governed by the arc between the two quaternions, which is half the rotation angle (and then doubled on the way back out to a pose); the matrix blend's is governed by the whole rotation angle directly. Lesson 7.3 showed that schedule error grows much faster than linearly, so halving the argument buys more than the doubling gives back — measured, a factor of about four at every arc. At a 120° twist: 10.95° of pose for the matrix blend against 2.23° for nlerp, and at 179° it is 76.63° against 8.00°.

And it is better than nlerp, at the one thing Lesson 7.5 spent half a lesson on. A rotation matrix is unique: there is exactly one matrix per rotation, so there is no double cover, no long way round, and no nearest to forget. The 359° spin that 7.5's figure 4 is about cannot be written here. The harness checks it over 20,000 random pairs and finds the blended point never further from the endpoints than they are from each other: excess 1.192e−07.

The midpoint isolates the two failures from each other. At t = 0.5 the schedule error vanishes by symmetry for both, and what is left is only the collapse: the matrix blend puts the vertex at the right angle — 60.0000° against slerp's 60.0000° — and at 0.500000 of the radius, which is §8's cosine and nothing else.

So what would fix it, and why it is not here

The fix that works is to blend something that is closed under normalisation. Dual quaternion skinning blends the joints' rigid transforms as dual quaternions and normalises, which cannot collapse — it preserves the cross-section exactly — at the cost of a bulge on the outside of a bent joint, roughly twice the arithmetic per vertex, and a rig authored for one of the two methods looking wrong under the other. It is genuinely a different technique rather than a patch, and it is named here with a reference (§14) rather than built. This is the ninety-percent picture; the missing ten percent is Kavan's paper.

The other fix is the one in §8.2 — more joints — and it is the one shipped content actually uses, because it costs nothing at runtime and the rigger was going to be involved anyway.
