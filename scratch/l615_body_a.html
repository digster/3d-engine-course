Module 6 — Advanced Rendering · Lesson 6.15

Skybox and Image-Based Lighting

Since Lesson 3.6 the engine has carried an apology called ambient: one constant radiance standing in for the sky, the floor, the walls and every bounce off all of them. Two things are wrong with it. It has no direction, so a face turned to the sky and one turned to the floor receive identical fill and nothing in the scene is ever grounded. And it has no specular half at all — a sentence shipped in this engine since 6.4, naming this lesson by number as the fix. An environment map replaces it with radiance as a function of direction, and the honest part of the technique is that the sky you see and the sky that lights you are one texture. Getting there means two integrals, and only one of them is exact: the diffuse half factors perfectly and reproduces Lesson 6.2's albedo × ambient to six decimals on a uniform sky, while the specular half does not factor and we pretend it does — at a price this lesson measures rather than hedges. 0.08% at roughness 0.10, 29.0% dark at roughness 1 seen edge-on, 73.0% once the sky has a sun in it. And the mapping from roughness to mip level, which every engine picks by taste, turns out to be derivable from 6.14's closed-form lobe width — to within 0.77 of a level of what everybody ships.

1The problem: an apology called ambient

Open engine/include/engine/gfx/light.hpp and scroll to the bottom of shade(). The last statement in the function has looked like this since Lesson 3.6:

f_r * E is everything this module has built — a microfacet BRDF fed by a directional light, with Fresnel coupling the two lobes and a shadow map deciding whether the light arrives at all. And then there is albedo * ambient, which is a constant.

Lesson 6.2 did that term a real service. It took the word ambient, gave it a physical reading — a uniform radiance La arriving from every direction of the hemisphere — and pushed it through the Lambert BRDF:

\[ L_o = \int_\Omega \frac{\text{albedo}}{\pi} \, L_a \cos\theta \, d\omega = \frac{\text{albedo}}{\pi} L_a \pi = \text{albedo} \cdot L_a \]
(The integral of albedo/π · L_a · cos θ over the hemisphere is albedo/π · L_a · π, which is albedo · L_a. The π cancels exactly.)

That is a genuinely nice result and it is why the expression above is not a fudge: it is the right answer for a uniform environment. What 6.2 also said, and what we are here to cash, is that the arithmetic was never the problem. The assumption was.

1.1 What the assumption costs, twice

@@FIG_PROBLEM@@

The first failure is the one you can state in a sentence: a constant has no direction. Real bounced light does not arrive equally from everywhere. Outdoors, most of it comes from the sky, some from the ground, and the two are different colours and different brightnesses by an order of magnitude. Indoors it comes from whichever walls are lit. A surface facing up should receive more fill than one facing down, and in this engine, today, they receive exactly the same.

You have seen the consequence without necessarily naming it. Objects in our renders do not look placed. They look like they were cut out and laid on top of the scene, because nothing about their shading knows which way is up.

The second failure is worse and it has been on record for eleven lessons. Here is the comment that sits directly above that return statement, shipped in the engine since Lesson 6.4:

THE AMBIENT TERM IS STILL WRONG IN THE SAME WAY IT ALWAYS WAS, and Lesson 6.4 deliberately did not touch it: it has no specular counterpart, so a mirror in a bright uniform room still renders black except where the key light hits it. That missing term is the specular half of image-based lighting, and it is Lesson 6.15's subject.

Look at the expression again and you can see exactly why. albedo * ambient passes through no BRDF — it is the Lambert answer, with the π already cancelled. So the ambient fill is purely diffuse. And Lesson 6.4 established the metallic workflow: a conductor has no diffuse lobe at all, because it absorbs whatever crosses its interface within a few atoms. Its diffuse_albedo is zero by construction.

Put those two facts together. In this engine, a metal receives no ambient light whatsoever. Chrome standing in the middle of a brightly lit room is black except for one reflected lamp. That is not a subtle inaccuracy; it is the single largest error in the renderer, and Lesson 6.12 made it worse by introducing four polished metals to look at it with.

Why 6.4 left it alone on purpose. It would have been easy to give the ambient term a specular half by guessing — multiply f0 by ambient and move on. That would have produced a plausible picture and an unfalsifiable one. The reason to wait is that the specular half of ambient light is an integral over the environment, and until there is an environment to integrate, any expression you write is a number chosen to look right. 6.4 named the gap and left it; this lesson fills it with something that can be checked against a reference.

1.2 What we are going to build

Everything below follows from one change of representation. Instead of a single linear_rgb ambient, the engine will hold

\[ L_i(\mathbf{d}) \quad \text{— the radiance arriving from direction } \mathbf{d} \]
(a function from a direction to a quantity of light), sampled on the six faces of a cube.

That single object does two jobs, and the fact that it is the same object for both is the honest part of the whole technique:

  • It is the background. A ray through each pixel that hits no geometry reads the environment directly. That is the skybox.
  • It is the light. Every shaded point integrates it over the hemisphere. That is image-based lighting.

Because they are one texture, a reflection cannot disagree with its surroundings. You have seen games where a puddle reflects a sky that is not the sky above it; that happens when the two are authored separately. Here it is structurally impossible.

2Building intuition: light as a function of direction

Stand outside on an overcast day and look around slowly. In every direction you look, some amount of light is arriving at your eye. Straight up it is bright; toward the horizon it is brighter still and warmer; below the horizon it is whatever the ground is doing. If you could record the brightness in every direction, you would have a complete description of the light in that place — complete for the purpose of shading an object standing there, anyway.

That recording is an environment map. It is a function on the sphere of directions, and the entire subject is (a) how to store such a function and (b) how to integrate against it fast enough to do per pixel.

2.1 The integral we cannot afford

Lesson 6.2 wrote down what a shaded point owes. Here it is again, now with an environment rather than a single light on the right-hand side:

\[ L_o(\mathbf{v}) = \int_\Omega f_r(\mathbf{l}, \mathbf{v}) \; L_i(\mathbf{l}) \; (\mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{l}) \; d\omega \]
(The light leaving toward the eye is the integral, over the hemisphere above the surface, of the BRDF times the arriving radiance times the cosine.)

Put a number on that. A modest environment is 128×128 on each of six faces, which is 98,304 texels; half of them are in the hemisphere above any given normal. At 1920×1080 with one shaded fragment per pixel, evaluating this integral honestly is

2,073,600 pixels × 49,152 directions ≈ 1.0 × 1011 BRDF evaluations per frame.

At a generous ten per nanosecond that is about ten seconds a frame. This is not a per-frame budget problem; it is a per-frame impossibility, and it does not become possible on better hardware — it is five orders of magnitude away.

So the whole technique is two different ways of moving the integral offline. And the two halves of the BRDF permit that to very different degrees, which is the most important structural fact in this lesson and the thing most treatments blur.

2.2 A worked example before any formalism

Take the simplest environment there is: uniform, radiance 1 in every direction. A white Lambertian surface, albedo 0.8, facing anywhere at all.

What should come out? The surface reflects 0.8 of the light that hits it, diffusely. The light that hits it is the hemisphere's worth of radiance 1, projected by the cosine, which is the integral of cos θ over the hemisphere, which is exactly π. So the irradiance is π, the BRDF is 0.8/π, and the outgoing radiance is

\[ \frac{0.8}{\pi} \times \pi = 0.8 \]

Now notice what just happened: that is the number albedo × ambient has been producing since Lesson 3.6. The old term is not something the new machinery replaces; it is the special case the new machinery reduces to.

This gives us the single best test in the lesson, and it is the first one we will write: build a uniform environment, run the whole precomputation over it, shade a point, and the answer must be the old one. A new subsystem that reproduces the old one on the old one's own input is a subsystem you can trust with a new input. When we run it, the agreement is 1.3 × 10⁻⁵ relative.

This is the ninety-percent picture and here is the missing ten. An environment map records light arriving at one point in space — the point it was captured from. Using it everywhere assumes the environment is infinitely far away, which is exactly right for a sky and exactly wrong for a lamp two metres off. Real engines place many probes and blend between them, and add parallax correction so a reflection of a nearby wall moves as you do. Everything in this lesson is the single-distant-probe case; §11 points at the rest.

3The cube, and the texel that is worth five others

Before any integral, we need somewhere to put Li(d). The obvious answer is a rectangle: longitude across, latitude down, the way a world map works. That is called an equirectangular or lat-long map, it is easy to author, and most downloadable HDR environments come in that form.

It is also a bad container, for the same reason a Mercator projection makes Greenland the size of Africa. Every texel in the top row of a lat-long image covers the same point — the pole — so a 2:1 image spends an entire scanline on one direction, and roughly a quarter of its texels on the two polar caps. The distortion is unbounded: as you approach the pole, the solid angle per texel goes to zero.

A cube is better. Six flat squares wrapped around the sphere, each covering 90° by 90°. Its worst distortion is at a corner, and — as we are about to derive — that corner texel is about a fifth of a centre one. A factor of five is a bounded, knowable, correctable amount; an unbounded one is not. The hardware also samples cube maps natively, with filtering that works across face edges, which a lat-long lookup does not get.

3.1 Direction to face, and every sign in the table

The algorithm is one sentence: the largest component of the direction picks the face, and dividing the other two by its magnitude projects the direction onto that face.

Take d = (0.4, 0.3, −0.866). The magnitudes are 0.4, 0.3 and 0.866, so z is largest and it is negative: the −Z face. Dividing the other two by 0.866 gives (0.462, 0.346) on that face's plane — a point in [−1, 1]² — and mapping that to [0, 1] gives the texture coordinates.

Everything else is a sign convention, and the sign convention is the part that costs people a day. Here it is in full, exactly as the D3D and OpenGL specifications give it, where sc runs along the face's u axis, tc along its v axis, and ma is the major axis:

then u = 0.5·(sc/|ma| + 1) and v = 0.5·(tc/|ma| + 1).

Two things in that table are worth stopping on.

tc = −y on four of the six faces is where v grows downward comes from, and it is the same top-left texel origin every other image in this engine has used since Lesson 3.9 (texel_origin::top_left). That is not a coincidence — both conventions descend from the same lineage — but it is worth noticing that they agree, because it means a cube face and an ordinary texture are stored the same way round.

And the table was written for a LEFT-handed coordinate system, while conventions §2 pins this engine's world as right-handed, Y-up, −Z forward. This is exactly the class of silent bug §10 of the course spec warns about, so let us not assume — let us measure it. Look along +X in a right-handed Y-up world: your right hand points along forward × up = X × Y = +Z. The table puts the +X face's u axis along −z. The harness computes both and takes their dot product:

So: a cube map whose faces were photographed or rendered with a right-handed camera and then sampled by this table comes out mirrored. On a sky that is almost invisible; on anything with writing in it, unmistakable. The fix is not to "correct" the table — the hardware implements it and we cannot change it — but to generate each face with the axes the table names. Our procedural sky does that by construction, because it evaluates a function of whatever direction cube_to_direction hands it, so the bake and the lookup agree by construction rather than by agreement.

3.2 The solid angle of a texel, derived

@@FIG_CUBE@@

Here is the fact that every integral in this lesson depends on, and that is easy to skip: the texels of a cube face do not subtend equal solid angles.

Picture one face as a flat square at distance 1 from the origin, spanning [−1, 1] in each of its two axes. A texel at face coordinates (x, y) is at distance

\[ r = \sqrt{1 + x^2 + y^2} \]

from the origin — further away than the centre texel, which is at exactly 1. Two separate things follow, and they compound:

  1. Inverse square. Solid angle falls as 1/r², because that is what solid angle is.
  2. Foreshortening. The texel's own little patch of plane is tilted away from the line of sight. The face's normal is the major axis, and the angle between it and the direction has cos θ = 1/r — because the major-axis component of the un-normalised direction (x, y, 1) is 1 and its length is r.

Multiply them:

\[ d\omega = \frac{dA \cos\theta}{r^2} = \frac{dA}{r^3} \]
(the solid angle of a texel is its area divided by the cube of its distance)

Now the worked number. At the centre of a face, x = y = 0, so r = 1 and r³ = 1. At a corner, x = y = 1, so r = √3 = 1.7321 and

\[ r^3 = 3\sqrt{3} = 5.196 \]

The centre texel is worth 5.196 corner texels. That is the whole argument, and it is not a rounding detail — it is a factor of five sitting inside every sum you might write over a cube map.

The harness checks the asymptote directly, by measuring the ratio at increasing resolutions and watching it converge:

Two independent confirmations there. The ratio converges to 1/(3√3) = 0.19245, which is the number we derived on paper. And the six faces' solid angles sum to 4π — the whole sphere — to within double-precision round-off, which is the one check that says the implementation transcribes the formula correctly. A sum that came to 4.01π would mean a sign or an index was wrong somewhere, and nothing else in the lesson would catch it.

Why the code uses a closed form and not dA/r³. The derivation above is exact in the limit of a small texel, and a texel at 8×8 is 22.5° across, which is not small. The implementation uses Lambert's formula for the solid angle of an axis-aligned rectangle, f(x, y) = atan2(xy, √(x²+y²+1)), differenced at the four corners — which is what a two-dimensional integral's antiderivative looks like. That is why the 4π check passes at 8×8 and not merely at 512.

3.3 What ignoring it costs, and why that is a bug and not an error

Suppose you write the obvious loop: walk every texel of every face, multiply by the cosine, add it up, divide by the number of texels. What do you get?

The harness measures it against the solid-angle-weighted answer, whose true value is known exactly: the mean of cos θ over a hemisphere is the integral of the cosine divided by the hemisphere's area, π / 2π = 0.5.

160.505052990.500050041.010005

One per cent. That is small — smaller than you might fear from a factor of 5.196, because the over-weighted corners are relatively few and the cosine partly compensates. It is also worth being honest that one per cent of an ambient term is not going to be the thing you notice.

But look at the second row. Sixteen times as many texels, and the error did not move: 1.010005 became 1.010066. It got very slightly worse. That is the signature of a bias rather than a discretisation error, and the distinction is the whole point: a discretisation error is a number you can buy down with memory, and a bias is a number that will still be there when you have spent all the memory you own.

Which is exactly why cube_texel_solid_angle exists as a named public function rather than as an expression inside one loop. Every integral in the file calls it, and a reader can tell at a glance which ones do.
