15. Common pitfalls & debugging

15.1 The object spins all the way round between two adjacent keyframes

Cause: a metric or a blend that has not handled the double cover. The two poses are the same orientation and the two quaternions are antipodal, so the naive angle between them is 360° rather than 0°.
Fix: std::fabs on the dot product, or negate one input when the dot product is negative. §8.2, and Lesson 7.5 in full.

15.2 Everything is rotated backwards, and consistently

Cause: the sandwich is q̄ v q rather than q v q̄, or the product is spelled with the operands swapped. Both produce genuine rotations, so a single-object test passes.
Symptom that separates them: composition. M(qp) comes out equal to M(p)M(q) instead of M(q)M(p), so a parent-child hierarchy applies its transforms in the wrong order while each individual object looks fine. §C.6 is that test.

15.3 The scene slowly inflates

Cause: quaternions accumulating without repair. mat3_from_quat scales by |q|², so a norm that has drifted to 1.01 produces a matrix with determinant 1.01⁶ ≈ 1.06, and every object is 6% too big.
Fix: renormalised_fast wherever a rotation is stepped incrementally — it costs 1.06 ns. §10.4.

15.4 An object is wrong by a large angle and normalising does not help

Cause: rotate called on a non-unit quaternion. §6.4: the two-cross form equals q v q̄ only on the unit sphere, so off it you get neither a rotation nor a scaled one. At |q| = 1.5 the error is 34.7°.
Why it is hard to spot: it does not look like a scale problem, so nobody checks the norm.

15.5 quat::i() does not do what you expected

Cause: arriving from complex numbers, where i is a quarter turn. quat::i() has w = cos(θ/2) = 0, so it is a half turn. The quarter turn about x is (0.707107, 0.707107, 0, 0).

15.6 A quaternion from another library is a 180° error about a diagonal

Cause: component order. This engine stores w, x, y, z; a great deal of published code and most GPU packing uses x, y, z, w. A blind memcpy across that boundary rotates w into x and produces exactly that symptom. Conventions §8e.

15.7 An extraction is fine in testing and wrong in the field

Cause: the naive trace route (§9.1). It is indistinguishable from Shepperd's up to about 120°, which is where a hand-written test suite lives, and it is off by 180° at 179.99°. Rotations near a half-turn are not exotic — a character facing away from the camera is one.

15.8 A process pitfall, from writing this lesson

A figure that measures an angle with acos reports exactly zero. §9.4's first draft compared two recovered axes with acos(|a·b|) and printed 0.000e+00 in every row — not a pass, a blind instrument. An axis error of 5.7×10⁻⁵ rad puts the dot product at 1 − 1.6×10⁻⁹, which rounds to exactly 1.0f, and acos of exactly 1 is exactly 0. That is Lesson 7.1 §6.4's finding arriving from a third direction. Measure the chord |a − b|, which is of the size of the answer, and turn it back into an angle with 2 asin(chord/2).

15.9 And one about drawing

A render figure on the page's own pale panel loses the colours the program chose. Every screenshot figure in this course before now sat on .fill-soft, which is near-white in light mode. That is fine for a lit solid and destroys a thin bright line: §8.3's gold dial hand — the entire content of that figure — was at 1.8:1 against it. The fix is one CSS rule, .fill-shot, giving a render panel the program's own background. The alternative, changing the demo's palette to suit the page, would have been the page telling a lie about the program.

16. Exercises

Exercise 7.4.1 — The other convention guided

Write quat_from_xyzw(const float* p) and quat_to_xyzw(quat q, float* p), and a round-trip test. Then deliberately break it: read a w,x,y,z buffer as x,y,z,w and measure, in degrees, how far the resulting orientation is from the intended one for a 30° turn about ŷ.

Hint. Use angle_between_rotations on the two matrices. The answer is large, and whether it is exactly 180° depends on the axis — work out which axes give 180° before you run it.

Solution sketch. the stored buffer is (cos 15°, 0, sin 15°, 0); read as x, y, z, w it becomes the quaternion w = 0, (x, y, z) = (cos 15°, 0, sin 15°). The angle between two rotations is 2 arccos|q₁ · q₂|, and here the dot product is c·0 + 0·c + s·0 + 0·s = 0 — so for any turn about ŷ the misread is exactly 180° from the intended rotation. About x̂ or ẑ the misread keeps one product, cos · sin = 0.25 for 30°, and the answer is 2 arccos 0.25 = 151.04°. Either way the error is enormous and scene-shaped rather than noisy, which is why the helper pair and its round-trip test are worth their four lines: a swapped layout does not produce a slightly-wrong model, it produces a model facing somewhere else entirely.

Exercise 7.4.2 — is_rotation, finally guided

Lesson 7.1's Exercise 5 asked for a predicate that says whether a mat3 is a rotation, and it has been open ever since — §11 is the fourth place in this course to note its absence. Write it: bool is_rotation(const mat3& m, float tol), checking both MᵀM = I and det M = +1. Then answer the design question with a measurement rather than an opinion: what tolerance passes every matrix mat3_from_quat produces from a unit quaternion, over 20,000 random ones, and does it reject the 1.65-determinant matrix of §11.2?

Hint. Both halves are needed. MᵀM = I alone accepts a reflection, whose determinant is −1 — and a reflection composed into a scene graph turns a model inside out while every normal still looks unit.

Solution sketch. check every entry of MᵀM against the identity and the determinant against 1, both within tol. Measured in a float32 model of mat3_from_quat (three calls to rotate) over 20,000 random unit quaternions (scratch/isrot_74.py), the worst |MᵀM − I| is 5.3 × 10⁻⁷ and the worst |det − 1| is 4.3 × 10⁻⁷. So 10⁻⁶ passes with barely a factor of two to spare, and 10⁻⁵ passes with twenty — pick 10⁻⁵, because a quaternion that has been composed many times without renormalising drifts further than a fresh one. The 1.65-determinant matrix fails any tolerance below 0.65, so rejecting it says nothing about how tight the tolerance is; a better test of the predicate is a rotation scaled by 1.00001, which it should reject, and a reflection, which only the determinant catches.

Exercise 7.4.3 — The crossover on your machine measurement

§10.3 measured the break-even at 7.6 vectors. Reproduce it, then change the experiment: write the closed-form mat3_from_quat (the nine entries are in the header's doc comment) and measure it against the three-calls-to-rotate version. Does the crossover move? By how much, and is the closed form worth the loss of checkability?

Hint. Lesson 7.2 ran the identical experiment on rotation_from_axis_angle and found the closed form 1.3–1.6× faster, never the 3× its multiply count predicted, because both spellings paid for one sin. There is no trigonometry here at all, so predict the answer first.

Solution sketch. the break-even is cost(mat3_from_quat) / (cost(rotate) − cost(a matrix-vector product)), so any saving in building the matrix moves it down in proportion: a closed form twice as fast would put the crossover near 3.8 vectors. Predict before timing that the saving will be smaller than the multiply count says, for a new reason this time: rotate is being called on the basis vectors, whose zeros the compiler folds, so the "three general rotations" are cheaper than they look. On checkability, keep the three-rotate version as the reference: the closed form's nine entries are nine chances for a sign error that a symmetric test case hides, and the only honest way to ship it is a test comparing the two over random quaternions — at which point the slow one has become the oracle rather than the product.

Exercise 7.4.4 — Where does the gap grow fastest? derivation

§7.1 derived the gap between the two composition orders as 2 arccos|c⁴ + 2c²s² − s⁴| for two equal perpendicular turns of φ. Expand it for small φ and show the gap goes as φ². Then find the φ at which the gap is largest, and check it against the demo.

Hint. c⁴ + 2c²s² − s⁴ = (c² + s²)² − 2s⁴ = 1 − 2s⁴, which makes both questions easy. Mind the absolute value: the gap is largest where |1 − 2s⁴| is smallest, which is not where 1 − 2s⁴ is most negative.

Solution sketch. with s = sin(φ/2) ≈ φ/2, 1 − 2s⁴ ≈ 1 − φ⁴/8, and arccos(1 − ε) ≈ √(2ε), so the gap is 2√(φ⁴/4) = φ² — quadratic, which is why small rotations nearly commute (at 30° the exact gap is 15.36° against φ² = 15.71°). The maximum is where the dot product crosses zero: s⁴ = ½, s = 2−1/4, φ = 2 arcsin 2−1/4 = 114.47°, where the two orders are a full 180° apart. Past it the gap falls again — 84.4° at 150° — and at φ = 180° it is zero: two half-turns about perpendicular axes commute, both products being the half-turn about the third axis, with opposite signs that the double cover identifies. The "most negative" reading lands exactly there, on the one angle where there is no gap at all. Check with the demo: --commute 90 reports 120°, --commute 114.47 should report 180°, and --commute 180 0°.

Exercise 7.4.5 — A rotation from one vector to another engine challenge

Write quat quat_from_to(vec3 from, vec3 to): the shortest rotation carrying one unit vector onto another. The obvious construction is the axis from × to and the angle acos(from · to), which needs two transcendentals and dies when the vectors are nearly parallel or nearly opposite. There is a construction with no trigonometry at all that handles the parallel case gracefully — find it, and then handle the opposite case explicitly, because it genuinely has no shortest rotation and any perpendicular axis is correct.

Hint. §5 says a quaternion is built from two mirrors. What are the two mirror planes whose composite carries from to to? One of them is the plane bisecting the two vectors, which you can write down with an addition and a normalise. For the opposite case, from and any vector not parallel to it give you a perpendicular by one cross product — and choosing "any vector" robustly means choosing the coordinate axis from is least aligned with.

Solution sketch. reflect in the plane perpendicular to from, then in the plane perpendicular to the bisector h = normalised(from + to); the composite turns by twice the angle between the planes, which is the angle from from to to, about their common line. In quaternion form that is simply q = normalised(1 + from · to, from × to) — no trigonometry, and when the vectors are parallel it is (2, 0) → the identity, gracefully. The failure is the other end: as from · to → −1 both parts go to zero and the normalise divides nothing by nothing. So test 1 + from · to against a small threshold (10⁻⁶ is plenty) and, below it, return the half-turn (0, â), where â = normalised(from × eₖ) and eₖ is the coordinate axis with the smallest |fromₖ| — the least-aligned axis gives the best-conditioned cross product.

Exercise 7.4.6 — Integrate an angular velocity engine challenge

Module 8 will need this. Given an orientation q and an angular velocity ω in radians per second (a vector whose direction is the axis and whose length is the rate), advance the orientation by h seconds. Do it two ways — the exact way, with quat_from_axis_angle(normalised(ω), |ω|h), and the cheap way, using the derivative q̇ = ½ ω q as a Euler step followed by renormalised_fast — and measure how far apart they drift over ten seconds at 60 Hz for a few rates.

Hint. Derive q̇ = ½ ω q first, and the ½ should now be unsurprising: differentiate cos(θ/2) + sin(θ/2) n̂ with respect to time at θ = 0. The cheap way is what a physics engine actually does, and finding the rate at which it stops being acceptable is the point of the exercise. Guard |ω| = 0 in the exact version — normalised of a zero vector is the reason normalised_or exists.

Solution sketch. the Euler step is q ← q + ½h ω q = (1 + ½h ω) q, and after renormalising, (1, ½h ω) is a rotation about ω̂ by 2 arctan(θ/2) with θ = |ω|h — not by θ. So the cheap way turns too slowly by θ − 2 arctan(θ/2) ≈ θ³/12 every step, always in the same direction, and the error accumulates linearly while the axis stays exact. Over ten seconds at 60 Hz: 0.013° at 1 rad/s, 3.3° at one revolution a second, 13.2° at 10 rad/s and 104° at 20 rad/s. The total goes as ω³/(12 f²) × T, so 1° in ten seconds is reached at about 4.2 rad/s at 60 Hz — fine for a tumbling crate, marginal for a spinning wheel. Lesson 8.3 meets exactly this choice: its body_world offers both rules, keeps the cheap one as the default, and predicts this lag — θ²/12 relative, 0.23% per step at 10 rad/s — before measuring it.

17. Recap & next

The engine can now write down a rotation in four floats that compose in sixteen multiplies with no trigonometry, have no singularity anywhere, extract from a matrix with no bad case, and repair in one Taylor step. More to the point, nothing in this lesson was asserted.

  • The multiplication table was forced by isotropy. One demand — that space has no preferred direction, so every unit imaginary squares to −1 — gives ji = −ij in three lines, and associativity gives the other eleven entries. ijk = −1 arrives as a consequence.
  • The dot product and the cross product are inside the quaternion product, and the cross product is exactly the term that changes sign under a swap. "Quaternions do not commute" and "rotations of space do not commute" are the same sentence.
  • The sandwich and the half-angle came out of the same calculation — composing two reflections in planes — and so did the double cover, as a sign nobody could determine. Three facts usually presented separately, from one derivation, and Lesson 7.3 had already drawn all of it in the plane with a protractor.
  • Four candidates that sum to 4 leave the largest at least 1, so extraction has no threshold, no crossover and no bad case — where Lesson 7.2's three candidates needed a derived crossover at 120°.
  • 1.57× cheaper to compose, 1.57× dearer to apply, crossing over at 7.6 vectors. That single number is why renderers convert quaternions to matrices before drawing.
  • Two measurements refused the claims they were written to confirm. A quaternion's better conditioning turns out to be a property of holding one, not of extracting one; and renormalised_fast is exact at |q| = 1 and at |q| = 2 and catastrophic between, so a test at the two obvious points certifies a broken function.

Next: Lesson 7.5 — Slerp. The formula is already written: Lesson 7.3's a·(a⁻¹b)^t is not specific to the plane and not specific to complex numbers — it is the definition of a geodesic on any group with those three operations, which is why 7.3's three-line complex_slerp and 7.2's rotation_slerp for mat3 turned out to be the same three steps in different notation. What is genuinely new is the sign: §8 proved there are two quaternions per rotation, so there are two arcs between any two poses, and one of them is the long way round. 7.5 also performs the storage swap §11 declined, with the decomposition of §11.3 in the one place that needs it.

18. Further reading

  • Hamilton's 1865 letter to his son Archibald, widely reprinted and easy to find. Two pages, disarmingly frank about the thirteen years, and best read immediately after §3 — the difference between knowing the derivation and understanding what it cost to find it the first time.
  • Ken Shoemake, "Animating Rotation with Quaternion Curves", SIGGRAPH 1985. The paper that brought quaternions into graphics. §3's table and §5's sandwich are its section 2; everything after that is Lesson 7.5's.
  • Stanley Shepperd, "Quaternion from Rotation Matrix", Journal of Guidance and Control 1(3), 1978. One page, and it is §9.2. Worth reading for how carefully a 1978 aerospace note reasons about a division that might be small.
  • Real-Time Rendering, 4th edition, §4.3. The standard engineering treatment, including the component-order warning of §15.6 and a compact table of the conversions. Keep it open beside Lesson 7.5.
  • Marc ten Bosch, "Let's remove Quaternions from every 3D Engine". An argument that rotors — §5's object, stated in geometric algebra rather than in Hamilton's notation — are the right primitive and that quaternions obscure them. Read it now rather than before: its central construction is §5.2, and having just derived that, you are in a position to judge the claim rather than to be persuaded by it.
  • John Vince, Quaternions for Computer Graphics. A whole short book, and the place to go if §4's expansion went past too quickly — it multiplies everything out by hand, repeatedly, which is exactly what is wanted the first time.
  • Jim Blinn, "Consider the Lowly 2×2 Matrix" and the quaternion columns in Notation, Notation, Notation. Blinn is the best available writer on why the notation matters, which is §4.1's whole argument about storage.
  • 3Blue1Brown and Ben Eater, the interactive quaternion videos. A stereographic projection of the unit 3-sphere that lets you drag a quaternion around and watch the rotation it performs. It is the one visualisation that makes the double cover of §8 something you can feel rather than verify.
