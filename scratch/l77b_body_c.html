9A clip from an animation

With the curves converted, a glTF animation becomes one anim::clip: a track per joint, parallel to the skeleton — so the permutation the sort produced is applied to the tracks by construction, which was 7.7's first prediction — and three channels per track. What the file does not say, the importer has to decide, and there are four decisions.

9.1 — The duration is not in the file

glTF has no duration. The specification says the inputs "are relative to t = 0, defined as the beginning of the parent animations entry" and that outside a sampler's range "output MUST be clamped to the nearest end" — so an animation's natural length is the last input of any of its samplers, and that is what the importer uses. Lesson 7.7 argued that a clip's duration should be authored, because the last key of a walk sampled at 30 Hz can land a frame short of the cycle; here the argument arrives from the other side, and Blender answers it. The exporter writes the closing frame — frame 30 of a 30-frame walk, frame 60 of the wave — whose pose repeats the opening one, so the last input is the cycle's length. 7.7's instrument confirms it: 

  clip walk  1.000 s  keys 520  seam 9.84e-07 deg
  clip wave  2.000 s  keys 409  seam 7.76e-07 deg

9.2 — Looping is the importer's choice, and the specification clamps

glTF "doesn't define any runtime behavior, such as: order of playing, auto-start, loops" (§3.11), so import_settings::loops decides, and defaults to true because the clips this engine plays are cycles. That decision has a consequence at the end of the clip that twice looked like a bug in this lesson. Past the last key the specification clamps; a looping clip wraps. Sampled at exactly t = 1 s, §3.4's one-second turn returned to rest until the harness imported it with loops = false. And the first draft of the end-to-end check (§13) sampled at 1.013 s, compared the wrapped pose with the specification's clamped one, and reported an 11.7 mm "error" that was the walk's motion over thirteen milliseconds. Neither was wrong; they were two definitions of what comes after the end.

9.3 — What stays still stays where the file put it

A channel the animation does not animate is left empty in the track, and 7.7's sampler fills an empty channel with the joint's local_bind. For an imported rig that is the node's own transform — which is exactly what the specification requires: "Non-animated properties MUST keep their values during animation" (§3.11). This is the second reason §5 left local_bind holding the node's transform rather than the bind pose the inverse matrices describe: it is the rest value of every channel no clip touches.

9.4 — Sign flips: none, on this content

Lesson 7.7's prediction was that a real exporter's clips would contain adjacent rotation keys on opposite halves of the sphere, and that finding some would prove the importer read the file. canonicalise_rotations runs on every imported clip and counts them: 

  sign flips 0, frozen vertices 0

Zero. That is not the exporter canonicalising, as far as this file can tell; it is the content. A sign flip needs a rotation that passes the half-turn from rest, where w changes sign — and the mannequin's keys were authored as Euler angles under 180° and never go near one. The prediction was a claim about exporters, and the measurement can only speak for this file: the count is zero because nothing here could have produced one, which is a reason to keep the counter rather than a reason to remove it.

9.5 — 7.7's reducer, on real content

An imported clip is exactly as dense as the file, and the file wrote everything — every property of every bone, sampled or held. Lesson 7.7's reduce exists for this, and it takes the importer's clips unchanged: 

  walk  keys  520 -> 195 (-325), channels 57 -> 14
          9980 B ->  3828 B, worst playback 0.498 deg
  wave  keys  409 -> 103 (-306), channels 57 ->  5
          7876 B ->  2060 B, worst playback 0.494 deg

Forty-three of the walk's 57 channels and 52 of the wave's are Blender's constant STEP channels, and the reducer deletes them outright: each one holds a single value that equals the node's rest transform, and 7.7's third stage removes a channel whose one value is the bind value, because the sampler would reproduce it anyway. What is left is the motion — the walk's thirteen turning joints and the hip's bob — within the reducer's half degree. The importer did not reduce by default, and that is deliberate: reduction is lossy and budgeted, and whether to spend the budget is the caller's decision. The demo plays either with R.

10The first export: a joint nobody animates

The first time the importer ran on the mannequin, its joint table had twenty skin joints, not nineteen. The twentieth was called neutral_bone. It is not in the armature the script builds; it is a child of the Armature node with no animation channel, and twenty-five vertices were weighted to it — all of them at the tip of the nose.

Blender's automatic weights solve a heat-diffusion problem over the surface from each bone, and on the thin protrusion of the nose the solution for every bone fell below the exporter's threshold. The exporter's response is in its source (io_scene_gltf2/blender/exp/primitive_extract.py): a vertex with no influence above 0.0001 is given full weight on "a joint that will be created later", and tree.py creates it — neutral_bone, parented to the armature, appended to the skin. The file is valid. Every weight sums to one. Nothing warns. And the nose tip is rigidly attached to a joint that never moves, so when the head turns, the tip stays behind.

A check for "a joint named neutral_bone" would find this one exporter's convention and nothing else. The importer measures the general condition instead: frozen_vertices counts the vertices no clip in the file can move — every joint they are weighted to is one that no channel changes, directly or through an ancestor. It is not an error in itself; a pack strapped to an unanimated root is frozen correctly. It is a number worth reading, and on the first export it read 25: 

  unfixed: 20 skin joints, last 'neutral_bone'; frozen vertices 25
  vertices fully on neutral_bone: 25
  fixed:   19 skin joints; frozen vertices 0
  wave: the unfixed face plate lags the fixed by up to 16.9 mm

The fix is in the authoring, where a rigger would make it: after automatic weighting, every vertex with no weight above the exporter's threshold is painted onto its nearest bone (weight_the_missed in make_mannequin.py). The first export is kept as scratch/mannequin_unfixed.glb, because without it the check has never seen the thing it checks for. 11Which way it faces

Lesson 6.6 compared glTF's conventions with this engine's and found one difference: "glTF says an asset's front faces +Z; our camera looks down −Z. So a model dropped into the world unrotated presents its back to you." Every asset 6.6 had was symmetric front to back, so nothing could test it. The mannequin has a face plate. 

  head joint (0.000, 1.620, 0.000), nose tip z = 0.121
  camera at z = 3 looking down -z: nose 2.879 m away, head 3.000
  [PASS] J.1 the nose points +z, toward a camera looking down -z

The nose is on the +z side of the head, as the specification promises: "+Y as up, +Z as forward, and -X as right; the front of a glTF asset faces +Z" (§3.4). A camera that looks down −z sits on the +z side of whatever it looks at and sees the surfaces facing +z — so the nose is 2.879 m from the camera and the centre of the head 3.000. An unrotated glTF character looks at you. Figure 11 is the geometry; Figures 3 and 12 are the renders, face plate forward.

What the two conventions really disagree about is forward. glTF's is +z; this engine's is −z. A character dropped in unrotated faces the camera, and gameplay code that then moves it along the engine's forward walks it backwards, face first toward you while its feet stride away. The fix 6.6 prescribed is still right — a yaw on the entity, outside the loader, which the demo's T key is — for this reason rather than the one it gave. 12Implementation

12.1 — The parser describes; it still decides nothing

Lesson 6.6 drew the line this lesson keeps: gfx/gltf.cpp turns bytes into descriptions in the file's own indices, and knows nothing about joints. So the parser grows four description types and four readers, and gfx/ still includes nothing from anim/:

  • gltf_node_desc — every node, with its parent and its local transform. TRS is read field by field rather than through cgltf_node_transform_local, which would hand back a matrix only to be decomposed again; a node that wrote a matrix goes through 7.5's transform_from_affine, and its out_of_square is kept so the importer can check the specification's promise that such a matrix has no shear.
  • gltf_skin_desc — the joint list and the inverse binds, read column-major into mat4 with no transpose (6.6's coincidence of three layouts, again). An absent accessor leaves the vector empty, which the importer reads as the specification's identity.
  • gltf_primitive gains its node, its skin and the flattened joints and weights arrays — every set that pairs up, read by read_influences, with joint indices through cgltf_accessor_read_uint so an index never passes through a float.
  • gltf_animation_desc — samplers (times, raw values, interpolation) and channels (node, path, sampler). A path the parser does not recognise leaves the channel with no node, which the specification says to ignore; the obvious default — translation — would animate a position with somebody else's numbers.

A file with no skins — every file this engine loaded before this lesson — gets a node table and two empty vectors, and nothing else about its load changes. That is §17's first rule for an inserted lesson, and §13 checks it on every harness in Modules 6 to 8.

12.2 — import_rig: skeleton, then meshes, then clips

The order is forced: the meshes need the slot-to-joint table the skeleton builds, and the clips need the table and the rest pose. The skeleton's heart is the stable sort from §4.2:

Iterative rather than recursive, so a file's depth never becomes the loader's stack depth. Then each joint takes its node's transform as local_bind, bake_inverse_binds fills every joint the 7.6 way, and the skin's joints are overwritten with the file's matrices or the identity.

The meshes loop over each vertex's influences: skip zero weights, count and drop negative ones and out-of-range slots, map slot to joint, stable_sort by weight, keep four, record what was dropped, pad with joint 0 at weight 0 (7.6's rule that an unused slot must still name a valid joint), and finally run 7.6's own normalise_weights — the one repair the importer makes, and it is counted.

The curves share one routine, fit_interval, a template over the value type with the three things that differ — how the file evaluates, how we interpolate, how far apart two values are — passed in as lambdas. Its search is the whole idea of §8:

C++: a template whose policy arrives as lambdas

Lesson 7.7 met its first template, keyframe<T>, as a container of a type that varies. fit_interval is the other common use: an algorithm written once whose steps vary by type. Its parameters FileFn, OurFn and DistFn are deduced from the lambdas at each call, so each call site compiles its own copy with the lambdas inlined — no function pointers, no virtual calls, and no common base class that a vec3 and a quat would have to pretend to share. The one type that cannot be deduced, the key's value type, is written explicitly at the call: fit_interval<quat>(…).

LINEAR positions and scales skip the search, because they are ours already; LINEAR rotations and every CUBICSPLINE go through it; STEP writes its held key and moves on. After each clip is built, 7.7's canonicalise_rotations and validate run on it, and at the end a pass over parents marks every joint some channel moves — its own or an ancestor's — which is all frozen_vertices needs.

12.3 — Three corrections to Lesson 7.6's files

None of them changes a number any later lesson measured (§13), and each is corrected in place with the story beside it:

  • skeleton.hpp and skeleton.cpp: what an out-of-order joint does (§4.3), why an importer writes joint_from_model (§5.1), and k_uniform_scale_tolerance in place of exact equality (§5.4).
  • skin.hpp: k_weight_tolerance from 0.02 to 10−5, with the specification's rule in place of the argument it replaces (§6.3).

The course's rule for a later lesson that fixes an earlier one's code is that the fix ships in the later lesson's listings and the story is told there; Lesson 7.6's page stays an archive of what it shipped. 7.7b is written after Module 8 but sits before it, and no later lesson lists these three files, so this lesson's listings are the ones a student ends with.

12.4 — The demo, and the build lists

demos/mannequin is demos/rig's shape with the character replaced by the file: resolve mannequin.glb through the engine's search path, load, import, then sample, blend, palette and skin into two pool meshes each frame. Each import bug from this lesson is built once at start-up as a broken copy of the rig — in_node_order reorders every parallel array with the same permutation and deliberately does not re-sort the parents; swizzled reads every rotation w-first — so a key or a flag switches between them at no cost per frame. It uses the software renderer, as rig does, so a headless --shot is the same picture on every machine.

engine/CMakeLists.txt gains src/anim/import.cpp, and the comment above the anim group records that 7.7's prediction came true: "Alphabetical order and dependency order agree here by luck … and will not keep agreeing" — import.cpp depends on the files on both sides of it. engine.hpp gains the header, which 5.12's lint would otherwise refuse at configure time; the first attempt carried a trailing comment on the include line, and the lint, which matches whole lines, refused that too.

13.1 — End to end, against code that shares nothing

Section G is the one the rest lean on. It skins both of the mannequin's meshes at 24 moments of the two clips, twice: through the engine (import, sample, compose, palette, skin_positions), and through a reference written from the specification's text in double precision, with its own 4×4 matrix type, its own sampler (Appendix C's slerp formula, not quat_slerp), its own tree walk (recursion up parent pointers, not a parent-first array), and the file's influences read directly — every set, unnormalised, never through the importer's top-four. The reference also samples inside each clip, having learned §9.2's lesson: 

  24 poses x 2 meshes: worst vertex 39.51 um from the spec
  control, inverse binds skipped: worst 1.620 m
  [PASS] G.1 every vertex within 0.1 mm of the spec's formula
  [PASS] G.2 control: without inverse binds, metres off

Forty micrometres is the importer's 0.05° budget at the end of a limb plus float noise down a chain of eight joints. The control is 7.6's classic bug, and it misses by 1.6 metres, so the instrument can tell a broken pipeline from a working one by five orders of magnitude.

13.2 — What it costs

  libengine.a built as Release
  load_gltf 0.178 ms, import_rig 0.226 ms (min of 15)
  per frame: sample 0.16 us, palette 0.22 us, skin 16.6 us

Minima over fifteen runs, and they move by about a third between runs of the harness (other runs read 0.125 to 0.136 ms and 0.159 to 0.174 ms), so read them as sizes rather than as measurements to three figures. Loading and importing the character is under half a millisecond, once. Playing it is about 17 µs a frame, a tenth of a percent of a 60 Hz frame, and almost all of it is the per-vertex skinning loop Lesson 7.6 wrote — 6.2 ns a vertex here; Lesson 7.7's harness put the same loop at 9.1 ns on its own fixture, and this lesson did not measure why the two differ. The importer made nothing slower to play: it produces the same types 7.6 and 7.7 always consumed.

13.3 — Additive, measured

An inserted lesson must not move anything a later lesson measured (the authoring guide's §17). The harnesses of every lesson from 6.1 to 8.13 — forty-one programs — were run before the first edit of this lesson and after the last, and every differing line was read. What moved: timings and ratios of timings, log timestamps, one heap pointer printed as proof that a handle survives a reallocation, and 6.18b's GPU races, which its own page documents as varying from run to run. No count, no position, no check. Two harnesses, 8.5's and 8.6's, fail to compile before and after alike — a later lesson changed a struct under them — and that is recorded for repair rather than fixed here. The golden render is E917C06C, byte-identical.

14The ninety-percent picture

What the importer does not do, each with the place it would go:

  • Morph targets. Channels with the weights path are read and counted (morph) and not played; the engine has no blend shapes for them to drive. Faces are where this matters most.
  • Animated nodes that are not in a skeleton — a prop, a camera, a light — are counted as unbound and dropped. Lesson 5.9's hierarchy could play them; it would take a second, non-skinned importer.
  • One skin, one skeleton. Two skins over one armature — a body and its clothes, commonly — import as two skeletons with the same joints: correct, and twice the work to pose. Sharing needs the second skin's joints mapped into the first skeleton.
  • Skinning on the GPU. The palette is exactly what a vertex shader would read from a storage buffer, under 6.18b's rules; the CPU path stays the reference it is held to. Exercise 2.
  • Root motion. The walk is in place, and 7.7's root_travel is there to measure a clip that is not. Extracting it — moving the entity and subtracting the travel from the pose — is Exercise 3.
  • skin.skeleton is ignored, as the specification allows: it "is not needed for computing skinning transforms".
  • A cubic rotation whose keys change sign interpolates through a near-zero quaternion exactly as the specification's formula does. The specification forbids exporters to write one, and the importer follows the formula rather than second-guessing it.
  • The 65,536-vertex ceiling of mesh_data's 16-bit indices (6.6) still applies per primitive. A production character exceeds it; splitting or widening is 6.6's exercise.
