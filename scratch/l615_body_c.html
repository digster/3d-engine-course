image_based_light is the function that replaces base * ambient. Both halves live in it, because both halves are ambient: the irradiance map through Lambert's BRDF, and the split sum. They are added, and the metallic workflow divides the albedo between them exactly as f0_of and diffuse_albedo_of already do for the direct light — which is the check that this is the same shading model and not a second one bolted on.

7Cube maps in SDL_GPU

Three new things on the device side: a texture type the engine has never created, a number format the CPU has never had to write, and a shader resource type that is neither Texture2D nor Texture2DArray.

7.1 The texture

A cube map is SDL_GPU_TEXTURETYPE_CUBE with layer_count_or_depth = 6 — exactly six, which SDL requires. The six layers are the six faces, and SDL_GPUCubeMapFace is documented as "can be passed in as the layer field", so the upload loop is Lesson 6.9's cascade-array code with a loop bound of six:

What differs is the shader side. A TextureCube takes a float3 direction where a Texture2DArray takes a float2 and an index, and the hardware does the face selection, the divide by the major axis, and the part software cannot reproduce: filtering across face edges.

Our CPU cube_map::sample clamps at a face edge instead of stepping onto the neighbour, so it has a seam. We measured it, and measuring it took three attempts — which is a story worth §9.

7.2 The mip chain is uploaded, not generated

This is the opposite of what create_sampled(..., mips = true) does two functions up in the same file, and the difference is the whole point of the lesson. SDL_GenerateMipmapsForGPUTexture builds a chain by box-filtering — the right answer when the levels mean "this texture, smaller". These levels mean "this environment, blurred by a GGX lobe of roughness r", which no box filter produces. They are computed by prefilter_environment and shipped up level by level.

7.3 Half floats, because HDR data has no other way to get there

Here is something the engine has never needed until now. Everything HDR so far has been produced on the GPU — gpu_post_stack renders into a k_hdr_format target and reads it back through a sampler, and no half float ever crosses the CPU boundary. An environment map is the first HDR data computed on the CPU that has to be uploaded, and SDL_UploadToGPUTexture takes bytes in the texture's own format. There is no half type in C++20 and no SDL helper, so float_to_half is ours to write.

The format in one line: 1 sign bit, 5 exponent bits biased by 15, 10 mantissa bits. Largest finite value 65,504, and about three decimal digits of precision — relative precision, which is exactly the property gpu_post.hpp argued makes half floats right for light. The harness confirms it across five decades:

2⁻¹¹ = 4.883 × 10⁻⁴, and we measured 4.85 × 10⁻⁴. That is half an ulp, which is the best a correctly-rounding conversion can do, and it is the number that says the rounding is round-to-nearest-even rather than truncation. Truncation would bias every value downward by half a step on average, which over a whole environment map is a systematic darkening rather than noise.

Three cases in that function are not the common one, and each produces a visible artefact if skipped:

  • Overflow clamps to 65,504 rather than becoming infinity. An inf in an environment map propagates through the prefilter into a whole mip level of NaN. A sun stored at 65,504 instead of 100,000 is wrong by a stop and a half; an inf is wrong by everything.
  • NaN stays NaN. Turning it into the largest finite half would hide the bug that produced it.
  • Subnormals. Below 2⁻¹⁴ the exponent cannot go lower and the mantissa absorbs the shift. Getting this wrong rounds small values to zero, which is invisible until you tonemap at a low exposure and find the shadows have quantised.

7.4 Three bindings, one call

The scene shader gains three resources at fragment slots 3, 4 and 5: two TextureCube and one Texture2D. On the C++ side they join the existing albedo, normal-map and shadow bindings, and the array goes from three entries to six in the same call:

Slots 3 to 5 do not change within a frame, so binding them here rather than once at the top is redundant work — but the rule Lesson 6.8 discovered still applies: a partial SDL_BindGPUFragmentSamplers does not merge with an earlier one, it REPLACES the range it names. Slot 3 would be unbound the moment slot 0 changed. One call for the whole set is the only spelling that is correct, and it costs the same.

When there is no environment, the renderer binds its own 1×1 black cube and a 1×1 black table. That is the fourth instance of this class's stated pattern — hand the shader the identity element of the feature it is missing. White was the identity for the albedo's multiply, lavender for a basis change (6.7), 1.0 for a depth comparison (6.8), and black is the identity for the addition the ambient term performs. The fallback is bound whether or not ibl_intensity is zero, because HLSL does not elide a resource because a branch did not reach it, and an unbound sampler slot is undefined rather than empty.

7.5 The skybox needs no geometry

A skybox is traditionally a cube: thirty-six indices, scaled up around the camera, drawn with its faces inward. That works and it has two problems. It can be clipped — too big and it reaches past the far plane, too small and the near plane enters it. And it has twelve internal edges, which Lesson 6.12's fullscreen.vert already told us costs double-shaded 2×2 quads.

So we reuse the oversized-triangle trick and turn each pixel into a ray. A pixel at NDC (nx, ny) sees through

\[ \mathbf{forward} + n_x \, \mathbf{right} + n_y \, \mathbf{up} \]

provided right and up are pre-scaled by the image plane's half-extents at unit distance — tan(fovy/2) vertically and aspect × tan(fovy/2) horizontally. The CPU does that scaling once per frame, so the shader is three multiplies and two adds. It is deliberately not an inverse view-projection multiply, which would cost a 4×4 inverse on the CPU and a matrix-vector product plus a divide per pixel to compute the same three numbers.

Two lines in that shader are load-bearing:

The depth is exactly 1. Conventions §4 puts this engine's device depth at 0 on the near plane and 1 on the far one, so the sky belongs at 1, and position = float4(nx, ny, 1, 1) puts it there. The pipeline uses LESS_OR_EQUAL with depth writes off, and the OR_EQUAL is not optional: the depth buffer was cleared to 1, so a strict LESS would reject every sky pixel. That failure produces a completely black sky in front of correctly lit objects, which reads as "the cube map failed to load" and sends you looking in the wrong place entirely.

It uses SampleLevel, not Sample, and the reason is not the debug feature it also enables. Sample picks a mip level from the screen-space derivatives of the coordinate, which is exactly right for a surface texture and meaningless here: the derivative of a view ray across the screen says how fast the camera is turning, not how much sky a pixel covers.

And it does not tonemap. The skybox renders into the HDR target Lesson 6.12 built, so what belongs there is a quantity of light; tonemap.frag.hlsl converts the finished image once, over everything. Tonemapping the sky in its own shader has a very recognisable signature: the sky looks right on its own and every reflection of it comes out brighter than the sky it reflects, because reflections read the untouched cube map while the background reads a compressed copy.

7.6 A uniform that finally cost something

scene_light_uniforms gains two floats. ibl_intensity lands in the pad slot Lesson 6.8 added to fill its eleventh register, so it costs zero bytes — the third free ride in a row after 6.7 and 6.14. ibl_max_level does not fit, and takes the block from 176 to 192 bytes.

That is worth naming rather than absorbing, because three lessons of free slots could reasonably have set an expectation. The affordability argument is 6.8's, unchanged: this is a per-frame push, so one extra register is 16 bytes a frame, where the same register in material_uniforms would be 16 bytes per draw.

And it cannot be a constant in the shader. The chain's depth is chosen at run time by bake_environment, and the roughness-to-level mapping is a property of how the chain was built — so a hard-coded 5 in HLSL would be a number that must agree with a number in C++, in another language, with nothing checking it. That is precisely the failure mode the static_asserts in gpu_uniform.hpp exist to make impossible.

10Common pitfalls & debugging

Four of the five below happened during this lesson. Two of them produced plausible output, which is the kind worth the most words.

10.1 A channel-order mistake that looks like "IBL is too dark"

Symptom. The specular half of IBL vanishes at normal incidence and survives at grazing angles. Head-on a metal sphere is black; toward its silhouette a rim of reflection appears. The diffuse half is perfect.

Cause. make_brdf_lut packed scale and bias with a hand-written shift — (bias << 8) | scale — reasoning about an RGBA word. engine::texture stores ARGB, so red is bits 16–23 and scale went into blue.

Why it was hard. Green is bits 8–15 in both layouts, so bias read back correctly. And the shader computes F0 × scale + bias: with scale reading zero, the specular term becomes bias alone — which is ~0 at normal incidence and ~0.078 at grazing. So the picture was not obviously broken. It was dim in a way that invited tuning.

Fix. Use pack_argb from colour.hpp, which has had the right answer since Lesson 1.6. A packing spelled out at the call site is a packing that can disagree with the one the engine uses — and the same mistake appeared a second time, in the harness's ARGB→RGBA unpack for the GPU upload.

How to catch it in five seconds. Print the table. scale at (n·v = 0.9, roughness = 0.1) must be 1.000: a smooth surface seen head-on reflects all of F0 and nothing more. If it is 0, you have this bug.

10.2 A Monte Carlo estimate that has not converged

@@FIG_NOISE@@

Symptom. The split sum appears to be 20% dark at roughness 0.25 — a number of the right order, with no sign of being wrong.

Cause. The reference was brute force at 8,192 importance samples, against an environment containing a 6000:1 sun disc. With a lobe a couple of degrees wide and a source smaller than that, the estimator's variance is enormous: whether a given run's samples hit the disc changes the answer by a factor of two. Measured, 8,192 against 1,000,000 disagree by 1.98×; with the sun removed, the same two counts agree to 0.040%.

The rule. Lesson 6.14 established one: check that a measurement can produce a non-null result before believing a null one. This is its mirror — check that a non-null result has converged before believing it — and both are the same rule underneath: establish what your instrument can see before you read it. The cheap test is to run at two sample counts and compare; if they differ, the smaller one is measuring its own noise.

10.3 A seam test that measures nothing

Symptom. The CPU sampler's cross-face seam measures 0.0000%. Twice.

First cause. The probe directions never crossed a face boundary, so the test compared a face with itself. Fix: assert the crossing before measuring the difference — the harness now reports "31 of 31 probe pairs land on different faces" as its own check.

Second cause, and the better one. They did cross, and the answer was still zero — because sky_radiance depends only on d.y. Along a vertical face edge our sky is literally constant, so the two clamped edge texels hold the same value and there is nothing for a seam to break.

A null result from an environment that cannot exhibit the defect is not evidence that the defect is absent. The harness now measures on a probe environment that varies with azimuth — the axis a vertical face edge actually separates — and finds the seam at 12.09% at 16×16, one texel's worth of the function, exactly as a clamp instead of a face step should cost. It then reports the sky's 1.06 × 10⁻⁷ alongside, because "this environment happens to be seamless" is itself worth knowing.

10.4 A build that cannot find a shader it just compiled

Symptom. Adding skybox.vert and rebuilding gives

Cause. engine_use_shaders made the copy-stamp depend on shader files produced by custom commands in the top-level directory, while the stamp target lives in demos/. The Makefile generator cannot resolve a cross-directory file dependency without a target-level edge as well.

Why nobody hit it for fourteen lessons, which is the nastiest part. Once a shader has been built once, the file exists — and make is perfectly happy to depend on an existing file it has no rule for. So every shader added before this one worked from its second build onward, and the bug sat waiting for the next person to add one. A build from a genuinely clean tree would have fired it on all twenty-three at once.

Fix. One line: add_dependencies(${target}_shaders ${shader_targets}). Depend on the shader targets, not only on their outputs.

10.5 The ambient term applied twice

Symptom. With the environment on, everything is washed out and the shadows have no contrast — and turning env_intensity down to about 0.6 makes it look fine.

Cause. shade() already added albedo × ambient and the IBL term was added on top. Fix: subtract it (§6.6). And notice how close this one comes to escaping — tuning the intensity produces an acceptable picture, so the bug survives as a "magic number" that will be wrong the first time anyone changes lighting::ambient.

10.6 Two more, for the record

  • A cube map bound to a Texture2D. The validation layer catches it; on a driver without one, the result is a black reflection and no error. Check the shader's declared type before anything else.
  • A repeat-addressed BRDF table. An interpolated normal on a silhouette can give a very slightly negative n·v, which under any address mode but CLAMP wraps to the far edge of the table and reads the roughest, most grazing entry there is — a bright rim on exactly the pixels where it is most visible. The shader saturates it and the sampler clamps, which is belt and braces on purpose.

11Exercises

  1. 11.1 — Sixteen bits for the table (guided). make_brdf_lut stores scale and bias as bytes, which the code calls its one real compromise. Measure it properly: for each of a grid of (n·v, roughness), compare the sampled table against integrate_brdf directly, and report the worst absolute error and the worst error as a fraction of the specular term it multiplies. Then say which of the two numbers an artist would care about, and why they are different.

    Hint. §G already measures the first at 0.01118. The second needs the prefiltered radiance it multiplies, which varies by three orders of magnitude across the sky. The answer is that a fixed absolute error on a multiplier is a relative error on the result, so it is invisible in a bright reflection and significant in a dark one.

  2. 11.2 — Bake the sky without its sun (guided). §4.3 found the split sum 73% dark with a sun disc in the environment, and noted that production renderers keep the sun as a separate analytic light. Do that: bake the environment with sun_radiance = {0,0,0}, set the engine's directional light to match the disc's total power, and compare the result against brute force over the full sky.

    Hint. The disc's irradiance is its radiance times its solid angle, 2π(1 − cos θ) — at 1° that is 9.57 × 10⁻⁴ sr. The comparison should improve dramatically for rough materials and barely move for smooth ones; explaining why is the exercise.

  3. 11.3 — Spherical harmonics for the diffuse half. An irradiance map is a cube of 6 × 32² = 6,144 texels representing a function with no detail finer than about 60°. That is wildly redundant. Nine spherical-harmonic coefficients (order 2, three channels) reproduce it to within a couple of per cent — Ramamoorthi & Hanrahan's result, in §12's reading. Implement the projection and the evaluation, and measure the error against irradiance_map texel for texel.

    Hint. Projection is one pass over the source cube accumulating Li(d) · Ylm(d) · dω — the same loop irradiance_map already has, with the cosine replaced by a basis function. Evaluation is a quadratic form in the normal. You should find 27 floats replacing 73 KB, and the bake time going from four seconds to a few milliseconds.

  4. 11.4 — Prefilter on the GPU (engine challenge). Five seconds is acceptable for a lesson and unacceptable for a loading screen. Move the prefilter onto the device: render each face of each level with a fragment shader that importance-samples the previous level, using the COLOR_TARGET usage create_cube does not currently request and SDL_GPUColorTargetInfo's layer_or_depth_plane and mip_level fields.

    Hint. Six faces × six levels is 36 render passes with a full-screen triangle each, which sounds like a lot and is a millisecond. Use the CPU result as the reference: the two should agree to within the sampler's filtering differences, and where they do not, the likely cause is the handedness note in §3.1.

  5. 11.5 — Multi-scattering compensation (engine challenge). §G measures the BRDF table summing to 0.3276 at roughness 1 — 67% of the energy is missing, because the Smith G models single scattering only. Implement Fdez-Agüera's single-term compensation (§12), and check two things: that the table's energy comes back to near 1, and that a rough white metal under a uniform environment now reflects close to the radiance it is standing in.

    Hint. The compensation is built from the same scale + bias you already have, so it costs no new table. Watch out that it must not push the table above 1 — a surface that reflects more than arrives is the failure Lesson 6.2 measured at 1.1386 and 6.4 fixed.

12Recap & next

The engine's ambient term is no longer a constant. It is an environment map — radiance as a function of direction, on the six faces of a cube — and the same texture is the sky behind the scene and the light falling on it, so a reflection cannot disagree with its surroundings.

What the engine gained:

  • Cube maps, CPU and GPU, with the face table written once as data and asserted against SDL's enum, and with the right-handed/left-handed mirror measured rather than assumed.
  • An irradiance map — the diffuse half, which is exact for Lambert and which reproduces Lesson 6.2's albedo × ambient to 1.3 × 10⁻⁵ on the environment 6.2 assumed.
  • A prefiltered environment chain and a BRDF table — the split sum, whose two approximations are named separately and whose error is measured, decomposed into the bake's contribution and the technique's, and shown to have a shape: 0.08% at roughness 0.10, 5.8% at near-normal incidence, 29.0% at grazing, 73.0% with a sun.
  • A derived roughness-to-level mapping, from 6.14's closed-form lobe width — and the finding that the folklore everybody ships is within 0.77 of a level of it.
  • Half floats, because HDR data has no other way onto the device.
  • A skybox with no geometry, on both renderers.

And three habits carried forward. The identity element pattern got its fourth instance (black, for an addition). The derived versus fitted boundary is marked in the source in two places. And 6.14's rule about null measurements acquired its mirror — check a result has converged before you believe it — which caught a 20% error that was entirely Monte Carlo noise.

What is still missing, stated plainly. One probe, infinitely far away: no parallax, no local reflections, no second room. No multi-scattering compensation, so two thirds of the energy is lost at full roughness. And the prefilter runs on the CPU in five seconds, which is a loading screen you would not ship. All three are exercises, and all three are what a production IBL system adds on top of exactly what we built.

Next — the renderer now draws everything in the scene every frame, including the objects behind the camera. Lesson 6.16 extracts the frustum's six planes, tests the AABBs Lesson 6.8 already computes against them, and feeds what survives into the instanced draws that have been sitting built and unused since 4.5.
