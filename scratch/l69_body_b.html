3The theory

Four things to derive, in the order the code needs them: where to cut the frustum, what shape to fit around a cut, how to stop that shape sliding, and what any of it does to the bias.

3.1  Where to cut: two schemes that are both wrong

We need n distances between the near plane and wherever we stop caring. There are two obvious answers and neither survives contact with a picture, which is a good sign that the right answer is between them.

Uniform splits put the cuts at equal world distances:

di = near + (far − near) · i/n

With near = 0.1 m, far = 60 m and n = 4 that is 15.08, 30.05, 45.03 and 60 metres. Every slice is the same depth. This sounds fair and is useless: the near slice — the one filling your screen — now covers fifteen metres, which is most of what you can see, so cascade 0 is barely denser than the single map we are trying to beat.

Logarithmic splits come from an actual argument. A surface at distance d covers screen pixels in proportion to 1/d. To hold texels per screen pixel constant — which is the quantity §1 identified as the one that matters — the slice depths must grow in proportion to d. Something whose growth is proportional to its own size is a geometric series:

\[ d_i \;=\; near \cdot \left(\frac{far}{near}\right)^{i/n} \]
d_i = near · (far/near)^(i/n)
You have met this 1/d before

Lesson 4.7 spent its precision section on the fact that a perspective depth buffer spends its codes in proportion to 1/d, so the near field gets a hundred times the depth resolution of the far field. That was the same geometry arriving as a problem. Here it arrives as a prescription: if screen density falls as 1/d, then equal density means slices whose size rises with d. One fact, met twice, from opposite sides.

Now push the numbers through, which is where the logarithmic scheme falls over. With the same near = 0.1 and far = 60:

d0 = 0.1 · (600)1/4 = 0.1 · 4.95 = 0.55 m

Cascade 0 covers from 0.1 m to 0.55 m. That is your own feet. Three quarters of your shadow budget is spent before you reach the far end of a dining table, and the interesting middle distance — where things you can actually interact with are — is crammed into the last cascade. The scheme is theoretically right and practically miserable, and the reason is that the near plane is an arbitrary number chosen for depth precision, not a statement about where you are looking.

The practical scheme (Zhang et al., 2006) is the blend:

\[ d_i \;=\; \lambda \cdot \underbrace{near\left(\tfrac{far}{near}\right)^{i/n}}_{\text{logarithmic}} \;+\; (1-\lambda)\cdot \underbrace{\left(near + (far-near)\tfrac{i}{n}\right)}_{\text{uniform}} \]
d_i = lambda · logarithmic_i + (1 − lambda) · uniform_i

At λ = 0.5 the cuts land at 7.78, 16.25, 28.57 and 60 metres. Cascade 0 now covers the first eight metres — the room you are standing in — and the far cascade absorbs everything beyond thirty. λ is the one number in this lesson chosen by eye rather than derived, and that is worth stating plainly. What makes it defensible is that it interpolates between two schemes that are each explicable: it is not a magic curve, it is a dial between "equal metres" and "equal density", and 0.5 says you believe both arguments about equally.

3.2  The slice: eight corners from three numbers

To fit a box around a slice we need the slice's eight world-space corners. That is an inverse projection — and we do not have one. mat4 has no general inverse(): Lesson 2.9 gave us rigid_inverse, which handles a view matrix because a view matrix is a rotation and a translation, and nothing in this engine inverts a perspective matrix.

We could write it. A perspective matrix's inverse is closed-form and about six lines. But there is a better route, and it is the one most engines take: the frustum's shape is three numbers, so build the corners directly from them. At distance d, the frustum's half-height is

half_height = tan(fovy / 2) · d,    half_width = half_height · aspect

which is not a re-derivation of anything — it is the definition of a vertical field of view, and it is the same tangent perspective() is built from in mat4.hpp. Going through a matrix to recover numbers we already have would be a round trip that can only lose precision.

Worked example. fovy = 60°, aspect = 16/9, and the near plane of a slice at d = 1 m.

tan(30°) = 0.57735
half_height = 0.57735 × 1 = 0.57735 m
half_width = 0.57735 × 1.7778 = 1.02640 m

So in view space the four near corners are (±1.02640, ±0.57735, −1). The harness prints corner 0 as (−1.0264, −0.5774, −1.0000), which is the prediction to five figures.

And the independent check. Agreeing with my own formula proves nothing, so §B pushes a far corner through the actual perspective() matrix and asks where it lands. It comes out at (1.0000, 1.0000, 1.0000) — exactly the corner of the NDC box, which is what a frustum corner means. That is the test that makes §3.2 a check of the corner builder rather than a restatement of it.

−z is forward

A corner at distance d sits at z = −d (conventions §2). Write +d and you have built the frustum behind the camera — and the symptom is not a crash or a blank screen. It is a shadow map that is correct exactly when you turn around, which is one of those bugs that gets diagnosed as "the light direction is wrong" and fixed by negating something unrelated.

3.3  Fitting: a sphere, and what it costs

Now fit the light's orthographic box around those eight corners. The obvious thing is to transform them into light space and take their axis-aligned bounds. Do that and the picture boils.

Here is why, and it is worth being precise because the mechanism is not obvious. Turn the camera on the spot, changing nothing else. The eight corners sweep around, and an axis-aligned box in light space that contains them has to grow to hold the diagonal and shrink again as they come back into alignment. The harness yaws a camera through 360° and measures the box side varying by 30.2% — from 24.6 m to 35.3 m.

Now recall what that side is:

world_per_texel = box_side ÷ resolution

A box side that changes by 30% is a world_per_texel that changes by 30%, which means every texel in the map changes size, which means every shadow edge in the picture moves, while the camera did nothing but turn. That is the shimmer, and no amount of filtering hides it because it is not noise — it is the whole map breathing in and out.

A sphere has no orientation. Take the slice's eight corners, average them for a centre, take the largest distance from that centre for a radius, and make the box the square that contains the sphere. Rotating the camera rotates the corners within their own sphere and the radius does not care. The harness measures the spread across the same 360° yaw as 9.9 × 10−8 — which is float rounding, not geometry.

@@FIG_SPHERE@@
Be honest about the price

A sphere that contains a box is bigger than the box. The harness measures both on the same slice: the corner-fitted box would give 0.01508 m per texel and the sphere gives 0.01945 m — so stability costs 29% of the resolution.

That is a real trade and it is worth knowing which side you are on. If your camera is on rails and never rotates, fit the box and keep the 29%. Almost nobody is, so almost everybody pays it — but pay it deliberately, and if a reader works the frustum's dimensions out by hand and expects a bigger win than §1's numbers show, this is where the difference went.

3.4  Snapping: the grid that jumps

The sphere fixed the box's size. Its position still slides continuously as you walk, and a texel grid sliding under stationary geometry is exactly as bad: each shadow edge is quantised to whichever texel it happens to land in, so it jitters back and forth by a texel every frame. Edges crawl.

The cure is to make the grid land in the same place every frame: round the box's centre, in light space, down to a whole number of texels.

\[ centre'_{x} = \left\lfloor \frac{centre_x}{world\_per\_texel} \right\rfloor \cdot world\_per\_texel \]
centre'_x = floor(centre_x / world_per_texel) · world_per_texel

The box now advances in texel-sized jumps rather than sliding, so its grid lands on the same world positions it landed on last frame. A shadow edge either stays exactly where it was or moves exactly one texel — and the eye reads a one-texel step as stability where it reads a continuous slide as crawling.

@@FIG_SNAP@@

The harness walks a camera forward in sub-texel steps and watches where a fixed world point falls on the grid. Snapped, it stays within 7.6 × 10−6 texels of the same position; unsnapped it wanders 0.499 texels, which is the worst it can possibly be — half a texel is the furthest anything can get from the nearest grid line.

Two ways to get this exactly wrong, and both compile

Snapping in the wrong space. The grid we are rounding to is the map's, and the map's axes are the light's. Round in world space and you round along axes that have nothing to do with the texel grid, which achieves precisely nothing while looking correct.

Anchoring the basis at the thing you are rounding. This one bit me while writing the lesson, and the test caught it. If you build the light's basis with look_at(centre, centre + fwd, up), then basis · centre is (0,0,0) by construction — the centre is the eye — so there is nothing left to round and snapping silently does nothing. The grid has to be nailed to the world, so the basis must be anchored at the world origin. The symptom was §E reporting an identical 0.499 texels with snapping both on and off, which is a much better error message than a picture that merely looks a bit crawly.

floor, not a cast

A cast to int truncates toward zero, so it rounds negative coordinates the opposite way from positive ones. Light space straddles the origin by construction, so half the world would snap one way and half the other, with a discontinuity running through the middle — which is roughly where the camera usually is.

3.5  The bias: the audit, and a surprise

Now the question this lesson exists to ask. Lesson 6.8 derived

\[ bias \;=\; \frac{reach \cdot world\_per\_texel \cdot \tan\theta}{depth\_range} \]
bias = reach · world_per_texel · tan(theta) / depth_range

and argued that every term was there for a reason. Cascades change world_per_texel by a factor of 7.3 and depth_range along with it. If that derivation was real, nothing needs to change. Here is what the harness reports:

@@FIG_BIAS@@

No line of 6.8's bias code was touched. Each cascade owns a light_camera, light_camera is where world_per_texel and depth_range live, and shadow_map::visibility already read them from there. A cascade is a fit, not a feature. That is the audit passing.

But look at the fourth column, because it says something nobody set out to arrange. In world units the biases differ enormously — 0.0413 m for cascade 0 against 0.3005 m for cascade 3, a factor of 7.3, exactly as world_per_texel does. In device depth — the units the buffer actually stores and the comparison is actually performed in — cascades 1, 2 and 3 all want the same number, 2.031 × 10−3.

That is not a coincidence, and it falls out of the sphere fit in three lines:

\[ bias = \frac{reach \cdot wpt \cdot \tan\theta}{range}, \quad wpt = \frac{2r}{res}, \quad range \approx 2r \;\Longrightarrow\; bias \approx \frac{reach \cdot \tan\theta}{res} \]
bias = reach·wpt·tanθ / range,  wpt = 2r/res,  range ≈ 2r  ⟹  bias ≈ reach·tanθ / res

The radius cancels. Both the texel size and the depth range are set by the same sphere, so a sphere-fitted cascade's device-space bias depends on the resolution and the slope and on nothing else — not on which cascade it is, not on how far away it is. Putting the numbers in: reach = 2.1213 texels for a 3×3 kernel, tan θ = 1 for a 45° surface, resolution = 1024, giving 2.0716 × 10−3 against a measured 2.031 × 10−3 — agreement to 2%, and the 2% is the half-texel padding the fit adds.

The exception that confirms the mechanism

Cascade 0 does not follow the rule: it needs only 64% of cascade 1's bias. And the reason is exactly the one the derivation predicts. Its depth range is 31.9 m where its own sphere is only about 19.9 m across — because the range was stretched to reach the casters, not the slice (§3.6 below). The moment something other than the sphere sets the range, the cancellation is spoiled and the bias goes its own way.

An exception you can predict from the mechanism is worth more than a rule with no exceptions, because it is the difference between having a formula and having a coincidence.

3.6  The depth range belongs to the casters

One subtlety that is easy to miss and produces a memorable bug. The sphere tells us how wide the box is. It must not tell us how deep it is.

An occluder standing between the light and a slice is outside that slice — it is above it, or beside it — but it still casts into it. Fit the near plane to the slice and that occluder is clipped out of the depth pass, so it stops casting the moment it leaves the camera's view. The symptom is shadows blinking out at the edge of the screen, which reads as a culling bug and is not one.

So the near and far planes come from the casters' extent along the light's axis. The harness checks both halves of that: a 50-metre caster above a small slice stretches the depth range from 26.1 m to 62.3 m — and leaves world_per_texel unchanged at 0.024999, because a tall caster is a fact about depth, not about texel density.

3.7  The seam

Two cascades meet at a split distance. On one side the texels are 0.0384 m and the bias is one number; on the other they are 0.0670 m and it is another. Both are correct answers to slightly different questions, and they disagree — along a line, at a fixed distance from the camera, which is precisely the shape the eye is best at finding.

@@FIG_SEAM@@

The usual cure is to fade. Pick a band at the end of each cascade, look up both cascades inside it, and interpolate. Since both answers are defensible, anything between them is too, and a ramp is invisible where a step is not. It costs a second lookup — but only for the fragments inside the band, which is a small fraction of the screen.

cascade_settings::blend_fraction is that band, as a fraction of each cascade's own depth. It defaults to 0, which shows the seam — Lesson 3.5's rule again: the failure has to be reachable, or the fix is folklore.

Selecting on the wrong depth bends the seam

The splits are distances along the view axis. A fragment shader knows a world position, and the tempting conversion is length(eye − world) — which is the radial distance, not the axial one. They differ by 1/cos(angle off axis), and at the corner of a 60° frame that is about 22%.

Select on radial distance and the corners of the screen land in the wrong cascade, so the seam — which should be a straight line at a fixed distance — bows into a curve that follows the edge of the frame. The fix is to carry the camera's forward axis and use dot(world − eye, forward): sixteen bytes to keep selection and fitting talking about the same quantity.
