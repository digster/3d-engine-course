4Implementation

Six steps, in dependency order: the geometry gains normals, the uniform blocks gain two members, the two shaders arrive, a renderer turns a list into draws, the demo stops being a probe, and the harness counts what it all cost.

4.1Normals, at import

§2.3 established that the fallback has to leave the renderer. It goes into src/gfx/mesh.hpp, next to flip_uv_v — which is the other function in this engine whose entire job is to reconcile somebody else's data with ours at the import boundary, and which is exactly the right neighbour.

The flat branch writes three vertices per triangle and an index buffer of 0, 1, 2, 3, … — every vertex used once. Note that this is exactly Lesson 4.5's expanded form, arriving for a reason other than a comparison: there, it existed so the cost of not having an index buffer could be measured; here it is forced by the geometry, because there is no sharing left to express.

The smooth branch is four lines of accumulation and the area weighting from §3.3, and the whole of it is this:

Then one normalised_or pass at the end. A degenerate triangle contributes a zero cross product and therefore contributes nothing — the right answer arriving for free rather than as a special case. A vertex no triangle references accumulates nothing and takes the fallback, which is Lesson 1.7's rule about never producing a NaN, and the reason an unused vertex cannot poison a frame.

C++ — why this returns mesh_data and takes mesh

mesh is a bundle of std::span: a view that owns nothing, so this function can be handed the inline constexpr arrays behind cube_mesh(), a loaded model, or the demo's generated floor, without any of them being copied to get here. mesh_data owns four vectors, because a function that creates geometry has to hand the ownership to somebody.

That asymmetry — take a view, return an owner — is the rule this engine follows everywhere and it is worth stating once. Functions that only read take mesh and therefore work on both kinds. Only the ones that produce geometry deal in mesh_data. It is also why view() is never called on a temporary anywhere in this codebase: the spans would outlive the vectors and it would compile.

4.2Two more blocks, and their offsets

gpu_uniform.hpp gains three structs: object_uniforms (vertex slot 1), scene_light_uniforms (fragment slot 0) and material_uniforms (fragment slot 1). Each states its byte offsets and asserts them with packed_offset, which is Lesson 4.6's executable version of HLSL's packing rule — a rule you can run cannot drift away from the code it describes.

Two details worth defending. First, scene_light_uniforms replaces 4.7's light_uniforms for the scene pipelines rather than extending it. The two describe different shading models, and a block that served both would carry fields one of them ignores. The probe's block stays exactly as 4.7 left it, and nothing that worked stops working — which is a rule this codebase has kept since Lesson 2.12.

Second, look at what gets multiplied on the CPU before it is pushed:

directional_light keeps colour and intensity apart because an artist wants to adjust a lamp's hue and its brightness independently — light.hpp makes that argument. Nothing downstream of authoring needs them apart, so they arrive as one product. The same reasoning decodes the object's Uint32 tint to linear once per draw rather than per pixel. Work that is constant over a draw belongs on the side that runs once, and a uniform block is the natural place to notice it.

scene.vert.hlsl is twenty lines and every one has a counterpart at the top of collect_triangles. Read them side by side once; it is the clearest possible statement of what a vertex shader is.

scene.frag.hlsl is the same exercise against src/gfx/light.hpp, and it is the stronger of the two because those two files were written three modules apart without either being adjusted to suit the other. normalised_or becomes normalize; std::max(0, dot(n,l)) becomes saturate; mirror_direction, halfway and the clamp before pow transcribe unchanged. §3.6 measured the result at one float ULP.

The clamp before pow is not defensive tidying

Both files write pow(min(1, alignment), shininess). Two unit vectors' dot product is a cosine and cannot exceed 1 — but it can round above it, and pow amplifies the excess instead of absorbing it. Lesson 3.7 measured an un-clamped peak of 1.00001. The GPU spells the same clamp saturate or min and pays nothing for it, which is a small illustration of a general point: the things a CPU implementation had to be careful about are usually still worth being careful about, and are usually cheaper.

One line in the fragment shader deserves a look because it is the only place the port genuinely simplified something:

lerp, not if. Both sides cost one instruction, and a branch on a value that is uniform across the whole draw is not free enough to be worth the words. Note also that the sample happens unconditionally — which is fine, and is why the renderer binds a 1×1 white texture for objects that have no image. A draw with nothing bound at a sampler slot the shader declares produces no pixels and no error message; that failure has now cost this project two debugging sessions (Lessons 4.6 and 4.7 each hit it once), and the fix both times was the same: hand the shader the identity element of the feature it is missing.

4.4A renderer that takes a list

src/gfx/gpu_scene.hpp introduces three things and deliberately not a fourth.

surface_style is the part of a material that cannot be a number in a buffer: solid (cull back), two_sided (cull none), wireframe (fill LINE). Three values, three pipeline objects, created at startup. Three and not more, because every additional axis of pipeline state multiplies the count — two cull modes × two fill modes × two depth settings is eight — and that combinatorial growth is the honest reason "just make it a parameter" is not on the table. Real engines either enumerate a small fixed set like this or build them lazily and cache.

gpu_draw_item is one object ready to draw: a borrowed mesh pointer, two matrices, a material, a texture, a style. Note what is a pointer and what is a value — the mesh is borrowed because it lives on the device and somebody else owns it; the matrices are copied because they are small and change every frame. The day that pointer becomes a handle is the day the renderer stops being possible to hand a dangling pointer, and that day is Module 5.

gpu_scene_renderer owns the pipelines, the depth target and the white texel, and its render() is thirty lines: push the per-frame blocks once, then for each item bind what changed, push the per-draw blocks, draw. The only cleverness is that it skips a redundant bind and counts the ones it issues, next to the number a perfectly sorted list would have needed.

And the fourth thing it is not: render() does not sort. §3.2's callout gives the reason.

The wireframe pipeline, and what Module 2 cost

SDL_GPU_FILLMODE_LINE. One enum value, and it delivers what Lessons 2.1 and 2.12 spent two lessons building — Bresenham, DDA, the whole wireframe milestone. It is worth sitting with that for a second, because the reaction it invites ("so that was pointless") is exactly backwards. You now know what the hardware is doing when you set that enum, which is why you will not be mystified when its line-rasterization rules differ subtly from your own, or when it turns out to be slower than filled triangles on tiled hardware. The enum is cheap because somebody wrote the lesson.

Which brings us to the HUD, and to an honest failure

The software demo draws its HUD with SDL_RenderDebugText, which needs an SDL_Renderer, and Lesson 4.2 established that a window is claimed by an SDL_GPU device or driven by an SDL_Renderer and never both. Text on the GPU needs a font atlas and a shader — stb_truetype, Module 6 — and inventing three lessons' worth of that here to print eleven numbers would be the tail wagging the dog.

So the ported demo has no on-screen text. The numbers go to the log, once a second and on every keypress. That is a real loss and it is named rather than glossed: for eight lessons the HUD has been how this course showed its working, and this lesson gives it up because the alternative was worse. Exercise 4.8.5 is the way back.

The mesh cache, which is deliberately terrible

build_scene hands back an engine::mesh — spans into somebody's arrays — and the GPU needs those uploaded once and reused. So run_gpu_scene has a cache, and it keys a mesh by the address of its first vertex, holds eight, and never evicts.

Every one of those is wrong for an engine and right for a lesson. The pressure for a real asset system has now been named in Lessons 3.2 ("the awkwardness is the point"), 3.5, 3.9, 4.5 and here — five times, and this is the first where it actually hurts, because a std::vector rebuilt in place keeps its address and changes its contents, and the cache has to carry two extra fields to notice. That is precisely the bug a handle exists to make impossible. Module 5 builds one; letting the pain accumulate first is what makes it land as an answer rather than as a framework.

4.6Measuring a port

scratch/verify_48.cpp is where the numbers in §3 come from, and two of its design decisions are the difference between a measurement and an anecdote.

One import, two consumers. The cache holds the mesh_data after normal generation, and both renderers draw from it — the software rasterizer takes cpu.view(), the GPU takes the interleaving made from the same arrays. If the two paths imported separately, every disagreeing pixel would be ambiguous: is that the rasterizer, or did they start from different triangles? Sharing the import is what makes §3.6's comparison mean anything. The same argument applies to the checkerboard, built once by make_checker and given to both.

Controls, everywhere. Every surprising number in §3.6 has a control sitting next to it. The textured floor differs wildly; the untextured floor — same quad, same camera, sampler off — differs by at most one code, which is what makes "the disagreement is the sampler" a finding rather than a guess. The boxes show zero pixels changing under the naive normal matrix; the squashed icosahedron in the same scene shows 2,160.

Three bugs in the harness itself, all instructive

The software reference had no near clipping, so it skipped any triangle crossing the near plane — and the floor quad's near edge sits behind the camera. Result: the GPU covered 35,532 pixels the CPU did not, and the "port" looked catastrophically broken. The fix was to call engine::clip_polygon_near, which is Lesson 3.3's code, doing Lesson 3.3's job. A reference implementation that has been simplified is not a reference.

The sRGB sweep ran without a depth attachment while its pipelines declared one — undefined behaviour that happened to produce an entirely plausible table. Caught only because the table disagreed with a second measurement.

The first sweep probed the wrong values. Fourteen points across the whole range, none of them near the boundary where the disagreement lived, so the hypothesis was declared refuted when it was merely unmeasured. Resolution matters: a sweep is only evidence where it has samples.

And one thing the harness cannot do, which is worth stating because it is the fourth lesson in a row to hit it. build_scene, scene_object, collect_triangles and draw_triangles all live in main.cpp's anonymous namespace, so the harness cannot link to them and has to transcribe the scene by hand. That transcription is a real risk — it can drift from the demo and nothing would notice. Module 5's engine/demo split is exactly the fix, and this is the strongest argument for it the course has produced so far.

7Common Pitfalls & Debugging

Everything is black, or one object is

Symptom: the geometry is clearly there — you can see silhouettes against the sky, or the wireframe view shows it — but every fragment is the ambient colour.

Cause: zero normals. The mesh carried none and you uploaded it without generating any, so the vertex buffer holds (0,0,0) in the normal field, normalize produces a NaN or a zero, and dot(n, l) is not a cosine.

Fix: run the geometry through with_normals at import. The general form of this bug is worth recognising: a vertex attribute that is silently zero produces a plausible-looking picture, because zero is a legal float. Check the upload log's vertex counts — a flat-shaded cube that still reports 8 vertices never went through the generator.

The ground plane disappears from some angles

Symptom: the floor is there when you orbit one way and gone when you orbit the other; the horizon line is still visible.

Cause: it is being back-face culled. A ground plane is a sheet, not a solid, so both of its sides are visible and SDL_GPU_CULLMODE_BACK removes whichever one you happen to be looking at. This is Lesson 3.4's finding — culling is only valid on a closed surface — now enforced by hardware.

Fix: give it surface_style::two_sided. In the demo, scene_object::closed chooses; press U to force back everywhere and watch the floor go, which is the fastest way to confirm the diagnosis.

One object is unlit, or lit as if it were facing the wrong way

Symptom: a single surface is uniformly dark while everything around it shades correctly, and orbiting the light does nothing to it.

Cause: its generated normal points away from the light, because with_normals took the triangle winding at face value and the winding is backwards. This is exactly what happens to Lesson 3.2's ground plane: its generated normal is (0, -1, 0), pointing down, so the cosine term is zero everywhere and all it receives is ambient. Nothing in Module 3 noticed, because the floor scene there was never lit — it drew a procedural checker, unshaded.

Fix: reverse the triangle winding, or negate the generated normal — and pick the first, because winding is what culling reads too and a mesh whose normals and winding disagree will be wrong twice. The general point: a convention that nothing consumed was never actually being checked.

The whole scene is lit from a plausible but wrong direction

Symptom: nothing looks broken. Shading is smooth, highlights are round, and the lighting simply does not match where you put the lamp — most visibly on non-uniformly scaled objects.

Cause: the normal matrix arrived transposed. §3.4 is why this engine ships three explicit columns instead of a float3x3.

Fix: push the columns explicitly and rebuild the product in the shader. To confirm the diagnosis first, use matrix_probe.frag.hlsl: push a matrix whose transpose is unmistakably different and read both answers back. Never debug a suspected transpose with a symmetric matrix — a great many hours have been lost to test data that could not distinguish the two answers.

Nothing draws, and the validation layer says nothing

Symptom: the pass runs, the draw is issued, the pixels do not change.

Cause, in order of likelihood: (1) no texture bound at a sampler slot the fragment shader declares; (2) a pipeline created with has_depth_stencil_target = true used in a pass begun with a null depth info, or the reverse; (3) the winding is backwards and every triangle is culled.

Fix: for (1), bind the 1×1 white texture — the renderer does this for you and it is the third time this course has needed the trick. For (2), the pipeline and the pass must agree about attachments, not just formats. For (3), switch the pipeline to CULLMODE_NONE for one frame; if the geometry appears, it is winding, and Lesson 3.4 §2 is the fix.

The split view's two halves are subtly different sizes

Symptom: the left picture and the right picture do not line up; one is slightly stretched.

Cause: the projection's aspect ratio and the viewport's aspect ratio disagree. Half a window is not 16:9.

Fix: compute the aspect from the rectangle you are actually drawing into — fit.w / fit.h, after fit_centred — and put that in the matrix. This is precisely what Lesson 4.6's uniform block bought: a compile-time 16:9 could not have survived a split view at all.

Comparing two renderers and finding 100% of pixels differ

Symptom: a per-pixel comparison reports total disagreement, and the two pictures look identical.

Cause: almost certainly a one-code difference on a uniformly coloured surface. §3.6 has the full story.

Fix: report a histogram, never a percentage. "100% of pixels differ, all of them by one code, in one channel" and "2% of pixels differ, by up to 134 codes" are opposite findings, and only the second is a bug.

8Exercises

  1. 4.8.1 — Make the sort worth something. Three objects and two styles saves one pipeline bind, which is nothing. Build a scene of sixty objects alternating between solid and two_sided, and measure the bind count with O off and on. Then measure the frame time and report honestly whether you can detect a difference.

    Hint: you probably cannot, at sixty objects, and that is the useful result. A pipeline bind on modern hardware is cheap; the reason to sort is that the count scales with the scene while the sort does not, and that having a place to put a policy is worth more than the policy's current value. Report the crossover point if you can find one.

  2. 4.8.2 — Move normal generation offline. with_normals runs at startup, every run, for every mesh. Write the generated geometry back out with save_obj (Lesson 3.5) and load that instead. Measure the startup cost you removed.

    Hint: it will be a fraction of a millisecond, and the exercise is not really about the milliseconds — it is about noticing that you have just built the first two steps of an asset pipeline: a source format that is convenient to author and a cooked format that is convenient to load. Module 9 formalises the distinction.

  3. 4.8.3 — Gouraud, on the GPU. scene.frag.hlsl shades per pixel. Write a second pipeline that evaluates the lighting in the vertex stage and passes a colour down — which is Gouraud shading, and is what Lesson 3.6 did before 3.8 moved the equation into the fragment. Compare the two per pixel and count.

    Hint: the interesting comparison is on the icosahedron with smooth normals and a tight exponent (E to 128). Lesson 3.8's argument was that Gouraud cannot represent a highlight smaller than a triangle; here you can put a number on how much of the highlight it loses. Two pipelines sharing one vertex buffer is also the cleanest possible illustration of what a pipeline object is.

  4. 4.8.4 — Find the sub-pixel rule. §3.6 attributes the 42 and 60 coverage pixels to the GPU snapping triangle corners to a sub-pixel grid while our fill rounds to whole pixels. Verify it: change the software fill to keep fractional corner coordinates and re-run the comparison. Does the coverage disagreement shrink?

    Hint: engine::vertex stores int x, y, so this is not a one-line change and you should think about where the rounding should live before you start. If you get the count down substantially, you have measured your hardware's sub-pixel precision from the outside, which is a genuinely satisfying thing to have done.

  5. 4.8.5 — Give the HUD back. §4.5 gave up on-screen text. The cheapest route back is not a font: draw the HUD into the 320×180 software framebuffer and blit it over the GPU's picture as an overlay.

    Hint: a straight blit would erase what is underneath, so you need alpha blending — which means a pipeline with enable_blend and a full-screen quad sampling the framebuffer texture, rather than SDL_BlitGPUTexture. That is also the first post-processing pass this engine will have, and Module 6's stack is built on exactly this shape. pong.cpp already has a 3×5 digit font if you want numbers before Module 6 gives you letters.

  6. 4.8.6 — Break the port on purpose. Pick one convention from §1's list — the winding, the depth comparison, the NDC y flip, the matrix column order — and reverse it on one side only. Predict the picture before you build, then look.

    Hint: some of these produce nothing at all (winding), some produce an upside-down scene (the y flip), and one produces a picture that is subtly, plausibly wrong in a way you would ship (the matrix order, on a symmetric scene). Rank them by how long you think each would survive code review. That ranking is the real content of this exercise.

9Recap & Next

  • The port was an API change, not a maths change, and that was earned rather than lucky. Of fifteen pipeline stages, two are the same C++ function called from both sides, eight became the hardware's, one was translated line for line, two were folded into one matrix — and two could not cross.
  • A material and a cull mode cannot ride on a triangle. Fragments enter a GPU thousands at a time with no moment between them, so anything constant across a draw is fixed before the launch — as pipeline state or as a uniform push. Four objects is four draws, and that is the shape of the machine rather than a failure of ours.
  • Group data by rate of change, not by subject. Per frame, per draw, per instance, per vertex. Our three-object frame moves 560 bytes of uniform traffic and would move the same 560 if each object had a million triangles.
  • A vertex shader sees one vertex, so the face-normal fallback moved out of the renderer and into the importer. Flat shading forces unshared vertices — a cube's 8 positions become 36 — which turned Lesson 3.8's runtime toggle into a build-time decision. Area weighting comes free, because the cross product's length is twice the triangle's area.
  • The naive normal matrix is invisible on boxes by construction, because a box's normals are its own axes and a diagonal scale changes only their length. Zero pixels on the boxes; 2,160 on a squashed icosahedron. Choose test geometry that can fail.
  • The two renderers agree. 87% of shared pixels byte-identical; the shading equation itself within one float ULP across 12,288 comparisons. The disagreements have causes: sub-pixel coverage at silhouettes, texture undersampling under minification, and a one-code floor in the darks where two implementations of the sRGB curve part company. They agree in the configuration this harness built — which, as Lesson 6.1 discovers, is not the one the demo shipped. See the note in §3.
  • Never report a percentage without a magnitude. 100% of a flat floor's pixels differ, by one code, in one channel — and that finding is worth nothing at all.

Next: Lesson 4.9 — Debugging a Frame with RenderDoc. This lesson found its bugs by writing a harness, rendering offscreen and counting pixels, which works and is slow. There is a tool that shows you every draw call in a captured frame, every resource bound to it, every uniform's actual bytes, and the contents of every attachment at every stage — and the reason it comes after this lesson rather than before is that a frame debugger is only useful to somebody who already knows what they expect to see. You now do. Every object in the next lesson's capture is one you created and named.

10Further Reading

  • SDL_gpu.h, the documentation comments on SDL_PushGPUVertexUniformData and SDL_PushGPUFragmentUniformData. One sentence — "Subsequent draw calls in this command buffer will use this uniform data" — is the entire licence for the per-draw rate this lesson adds.
  • Real-Time Rendering, 4th ed., §18.4 "Pipeline Optimization" and §18.4.5 on batching. The canonical treatment of why draw-call count is a first-class performance concern and what the strategies are.
  • Eric Lengyel, "Transforming Normal Vectors" (Mathematics for 3D Game Programming, §4.5). The inverse transpose, derived rather than asserted — and worth reading now that §3.5 has shown you the exact class of geometry on which it does not matter.
  • Íñigo Quílez, "Normals for a mesh". A short, sharp treatment of area weighting versus angle weighting for smooth normals, including the cases where each one misbehaves.
  • IEC 61966-2-1 (the sRGB standard) and the Vulkan specification's section on sRGB conversions, which is explicit that implementations are held to a tolerance rather than to a bit-exact result. §3.6's one-code floor is that tolerance, measured.
  • Fabian Giesen, "A trip through the Graphics Pipeline 2011", parts 6 and 7, on triangle setup and rasterization. The best available account of sub-pixel precision and fill rules on real hardware, and the direct explanation of §3.6's coverage sliver.
  • Our own src/gfx/light.hpp and shaders/scene.frag.hlsl, read side by side. This is not padding: the exercise of confirming for yourself that the two are the same equation is worth more than any external reference in this list.
