8. Implementation: recovering one

Given a rotation matrix, find the turn. This is the harder half, it is where the two ambiguities of §3 arrive as real code, and it is the part most implementations get subtly wrong — usually by clamping something and hoping.

8.1 The angle, which 7.1 already computed

Split any matrix into a symmetric part and an antisymmetric part; it is the matrix version of splitting a function into even and odd halves, and for a rotation both halves turn out to carry exactly one thing we want. Take the closed form from §5.3 and read it:

\[ \operatorname{tr}(R) = 1 + 2\cos\theta, \qquad R - R^{\mathsf T} = 2\sin\theta\,[\mathbf{n}]_\times \] trace(R) = 1 + 2 cos(theta), and R - R^T = 2 sin(theta) [n]_x

The trace comes from the diagonal: I cos θ contributes 3cos θ, [n]ₓ has a zero diagonal and contributes nothing, and (1 − cos θ)n nᵀ contributes (1 − cos θ)(nx² + ny² + nz²) = 1 − cos θ because n is unit. Total 3cos θ + 1 − cos θ = 1 + 2cos θ.

The antisymmetric part is even easier: I and n nᵀ are both symmetric, so they cancel completely in R − Rᵀ, and only the sin θ [n]ₓ term survives — doubled, because subtracting an antisymmetric matrix's transpose is adding it.

So we have the cosine and the sine of the angle, and Lesson 7.1 §8 established at length why atan2 of both beats acos of one: the trace alone cannot resolve a small turn, because near θ = 0 the trace is 3 − θ² and a float at 3.0 cannot hold a change of 1.6e-05. The measured relative error of the textbook acos((tr R − 1)/2) at a 0.004° turn is 1.00 — it returns zero.

\[ \theta \;=\; \operatorname{atan2}\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\big\|R - R^{\mathsf T}\big\|,\; \tfrac{1}{2}\big(\operatorname{tr}(R) - 1\big)\right) \] theta = atan2( |R - R^T| / 2 , (trace(R) - 1) / 2 )

The first argument is a magnitude, so atan2 returns a value in [0, π] — which is §3's canonical range, arriving for free rather than being clamped into place. This is the same four lines Lesson 7.1 wrote as angle_between_rotations, and the only thing 7.2 changes is that it stops throwing away the vector it takes the length of.

8.2 The axis, for free, almost everywhere

That vector is the axis. R − Rᵀ = 2 sin θ [n]ₓ, and an antisymmetric matrix has three independent entries which are exactly the components of the vector it crosses with. In written notation they are (r21 − r12, r02 − r20, r10 − r01), and that vector is 2 sin θ · n: direction the axis, length twice the sine.

So the general case is one division. Normalise the skew vector and you have the axis, with the correct sign — because sin θ > 0 for every θ strictly between 0 and π, so normalising cannot flip it. 

const vec3 skew{m.at(2, 1) - m.at(1, 2),
                m.at(0, 2) - m.at(2, 0),
                m.at(1, 0) - m.at(0, 1)};
// ... and after computing the angle from atan2 of its length and the trace:
out.value.axis = skew / two_sin;

8.3 What an axis error actually costs — the formula that explains both ends

Before handling either degenerate case, it is worth knowing what is at stake in each, and there is one formula that answers for both. Suppose the axis you recover is off by a small angle φ. How wrong is the resulting orientation?

Two turns of the same angle θ about axes φ apart differ by a rotation of Θ, where

\[ \cos(\Theta/2) = \cos^2(\theta/2) + \sin^2(\theta/2)\cos\varphi \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; \Theta \approx 2\sin(\theta/2)\,\varphi \quad\text{for small }\varphi \] cos(Theta/2) = cos^2(theta/2) + sin^2(theta/2) cos(phi), so for small phi, Theta is about 2 sin(theta/2) phi

Read the consequence rather than the derivation. An error in the axis is multiplied by 2 sin(θ/2) on its way into the orientation. At θ = 0 that factor is zero. At θ = 180° it is two — the error is doubled.

That single curve settles the design of the whole extraction, and it says something that is not obvious: the two hard ends are not equally dangerous. At the identity the axis becomes unrecoverable, and it does not matter, because whatever nonsense you return is multiplied by essentially zero. At a half-turn the axis becomes hard to find, and it matters more than anywhere else, because that is exactly where the multiplier peaks. A library that treats the two ends symmetrically — the same epsilon, the same fallback — has misunderstood its own problem.

8.4 The half-turn, where the easy route runs out

As θ approaches 180°, sin θ approaches zero, the skew vector's length 2 sin θ approaches zero with it, and normalising a vanishing vector amplifies whatever error it carries. A float's ε of absolute error in the matrix entries arrives in the axis direction as roughly ε / (2 sin θ), which grows without bound. At exactly θ = 180° the matrix is symmetric, the skew vector is identically zero, and there is nothing at all to normalise.

The information has not been destroyed, though — it has moved. At a half-turn, everything about the rotation is in the symmetric part, which is where we have not looked. Take the closed form again and read the diagonal and the off-diagonal separately:

\[ r_{ii} = \cos\theta + (1-\cos\theta)\,n_i^2, \qquad r_{ij} + r_{ji} = 2(1-\cos\theta)\,n_i n_j \quad (i \neq j) \] r_ii = cos(theta) + (1 - cos(theta)) n_i^2, and r_ij + r_ji = 2(1 - cos(theta)) n_i n_j for i not equal to j

The [n]ₓ term is antisymmetric, so it cancels exactly in the second line — which is precisely why this route is immune to whatever is happening to the part the first route depends on. Rearranged, with cos θ already known from the trace:

\[ n_i^2 = \frac{r_{ii} - \cos\theta}{1 - \cos\theta}, \qquad n_j = \frac{r_{ij} + r_{ji}}{2(1-\cos\theta)\,n_i} \] n_i^2 = (r_ii - cos(theta)) / (1 - cos(theta)), and n_j = (r_ij + r_ji) / (2 (1 - cos(theta)) n_i)

Two details make this work rather than merely exist. Pivot on the largest diagonal. Every other component is obtained by dividing by ni, and the three squares sum to 1, so the largest is at least 1/3 and the divisor at least 1/√3. Pivot on the smallest instead and an axis lying close to a coordinate plane divides by something near zero — trading one catastrophe for another.

And squares have no sign. The formula fixes the axis only up to an overall ±. Whatever is left of the antisymmetric part still knows the answer: skew is 2 sin θ · n with sin θ > 0 below 180°, so a positive dot product with the candidate means we guessed right. At exactly 180° the dot product is zero and we keep what we have — correctly, because both answers name the same rotation.

Where to switch, derived and then measured

Two routes, each bad at one end. The switch belongs where their errors are equal. The first route divides by 2 sin θ; the second divides by 1 − cos θ, with its pivot at least 1/√3, so its error is about √3 ε / (2(1 − cos θ)). Setting the two equal:

\[ \frac{\varepsilon}{2\sin\theta} = \frac{\sqrt{3}\,\varepsilon}{2(1-\cos\theta)} \;\Longrightarrow\; \sqrt{3}\sin\theta = 1-\cos\theta \;\Longrightarrow\; \tan(\theta/2) = \sqrt{3} \;\Longrightarrow\; \theta = 120° \] eps/(2 sin theta) = sqrt(3) eps / (2 (1 - cos theta)) gives sqrt(3) sin theta = 1 - cos theta, i.e. tan(theta/2) = sqrt(3), i.e. theta = 120 degrees

The last step uses the half-angle identities sin θ = 2sin(θ/2)cos(θ/2) and 1 − cos θ = 2sin²(θ/2); the 2sin(θ/2) cancels off both sides and leaves √3 cos(θ/2) = sin(θ/2). So the threshold is 2π/3.

It is not a compromise between two bad options; it is a point at which both are good.

Why the harness has its own copy of both routes

The engine runs exactly one route per call, chosen by the threshold — so the engine cannot make this measurement about itself. You cannot find where two curves cross by plotting one of them. verify_72 therefore has local axis_by_skew and axis_by_symmetric functions and runs both at every probe. Duplicating engine code in a harness is normally a smell; here it is the only way the question can be asked.

And the cheapest possible check that the threshold is not arbitrary: at exactly 120° the two routes must agree. Measured on clean matrices over twenty thousand random axes, they differ by at most 1.354e-05°. The switch is invisible.

The perturbation is ABSOLUTE, and that is the whole experiment

A freshly built rotation matrix is accurate to a relative 1e-7, which hides the conditioning question completely — the entries the skew route subtracts are themselves proportional to sin θ, so their relative error does not blow up. Lesson 7.1 §6.2 found the same thing about the Euler extraction and had to correct its own first explanation. A matrix that came down a ten-deep hierarchy, or out of a file, carries absolute error, and that is the matrix whose axis is hard to find. Perturbing by a fixed 1e-7 per entry is how this harness builds one.

8.5 The identity end, where nothing much happens

At the other end the axis also becomes unrecoverable — and by §8.3, it costs nothing. The only real requirement is not to divide by zero and not to return a non-unit axis, because normalised() of the zero vector is the zero vector, and an axis_angle with a zero axis builds a matrix that is not a rotation. That is a silent corruption three systems downstream, and it is the reason the branch exists at all.

8.6 The sign at a half-turn is genuinely free

§3 claimed (n, 180°) and (−n, 180°) are the same rotation. It is worth seeing that as a number rather than accepting it, because code that compares two axes for equality has to know, and code that rebuilds a matrix from them does not.

9. The geodesic: 7.1's indictment, answered

Everything so far has been machinery. This is what it was for.

9.1 Three lines

To blend from orientation A to orientation B: find the single turn that separates them, perform t of it, and apply that after A.

\[ \operatorname{slerp}(A, B, t) \;=\; A \cdot R(\mathbf{n},\, t\theta), \qquad (\mathbf{n}, \theta) = \text{axis-angle of } A^{\mathsf T}B \] slerp(A, B, t) = A * R(n, t theta), where (n, theta) is the axis-angle of A^T B

AᵀB is "undo A, then do B" — the relative rotation, in A's own frame. Both endpoints are exact by construction: at t = 0 it is A·I = A, and at t = 1 it is A·AᵀB = B. In between it is the geodesic, because turning steadily about one fixed axis is what "straight line" means in the space of rotations. 

[[nodiscard]] inline mat3 rotation_slerp(const mat3& a, const mat3& b, float t)
{
    const axis_angle_extraction e = axis_angle_from_rotation(transpose(a) * b);
    return a * rotation_from_axis_angle({e.value.axis, e.value.angle * t});
}

And it takes the short way round for free. axis_angle_from_rotation returns an angle in [0, π], so there is no long-way-round branch to forget — where Lesson 7.1 needed shortest_angle_delta applied per angle, three times, because a raw lerp from yaw 170° to −170° performs 340° backwards.

A claim of "zero excess" is worthless unless the instrument that produces it can also produce a non-zero. So the harness runs the same path-length instrument on the same pose pairs Lesson 7.1 published, and the Euler numbers are the control: if they do not reproduce, the instrument has changed and slerp's zero means nothing.

10. What it costs, and why this is not the end

Axis-angle has answered all three of Lesson 7.1's indictments. It has one convention rather than twenty-four; its angle is the distance between orientations; and it interpolates exactly. It would be a strange lesson that then went on to build two more representations, so it is worth being precise about what is still wrong.

10.1 The bill

verify_72 §F — measured as cumulative stages, each one the previous plus the next step of rotation_slerp:

rotation_slerp               31.46 ns/call, and where it goes:
  transpose(a) * b            3.76 ns
  + the extraction           24.95 ns   (+21.18)
  + rebuilding the turn      29.55 ns   (+4.60)
  + composing it onto a      31.46 ns   (+1.91)
the two trig-bearing stages are 82% of the call.

Stages rather than isolated timings, and for a specific reason: the extraction needs a relative matrix, and building one is the first stage, so an "extraction only" loop either measures the product as well or measures it on a fixed input the compiler can hoist out of the repeat loop entirely. The first draft of this section timed a 3×3 product at 0.41 ns — about one cycle for 27 multiplies and 18 adds, which is not a fast matrix multiply, it is five of the forty-five operations it should have done, because reading one element of the result let the compiler discard the other eight.

Thirty-one nanoseconds is not much. A hundred and twenty bones blended between two animation clips is 3.8 µs a frame, which nobody would notice. But a real animation graph blends three or four clips rather than two, and it does it for several characters, and the number that matters is that 82% of the call is trigonometry spent recovering a quantity the representation did not keep. A quaternion carries the half-angle's sine and cosine in its four components; slerping two of them is a dot product, two trig calls and a four-component interpolation, with no matrix and no extraction anywhere.

10.2 The thing it cannot do at all

The cost is an argument about constants. This one is an argument about the representation.

Given (n₁, θ₁) and (n₂, θ₂), what is the axis and angle of doing one and then the other? There is no pleasant formula. There is a formula — the Rodrigues composition formula — and it is a page of half-angle tangents that is numerically poor near θ = π and that nobody uses. In practice you convert both to matrices, multiply, and extract: trigonometry out, trigonometry back in, every time two rotations meet.

That is fatal for the thing engines do most. A transform hierarchy composes rotations at every level, every frame. A physics solver composes an orientation with an angular velocity step thousands of times a second. A skeleton composes a bone's local rotation with its parent's for every bone in the chain. Axis-angle is a fine interface and a fine interpolator, and it is a bad thing to hold a rotation in while you work.

What we are looking for, stated before we go and find it

A representation that composes without trigonometry, interpolates along the geodesic the way this one does, stores in four floats or fewer, and can be renormalised cheaply when it drifts. Lesson 7.3 finds the answer in two dimensions first, where it is a piece of school algebra and not a mystery: a complex number cos θ + i sin θ composes rotations by multiplication, with no trigonometry at the point of use. Lesson 7.4 asks what the three-dimensional version of that object is, and the answer is a quaternion — which will turn out to store exactly the half-angle quantities §8.3's 2 sin(θ/2) has been hinting at for this entire section.
