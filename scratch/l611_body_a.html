Module 6 — Advanced Rendering · Lesson 6.11

Transparency: Alpha Modes, Blending, and Draw Order

This is not a lesson about adding a feature. It is a lesson about a correctness gap in code that already shipped, and three facts make that precise. gpu_pipeline.hpp has said "no blending" since Lesson 4.4. Lesson 6.6's glTF importer reads baseColorFactor[3] and ignores alphaMode entirely. And that was the one gap in that importer which fires no status at all — 6.6 §10 says so in as many words — because a status code says "this file wants what the engine cannot do", and you cannot report a conflict with a concept that does not exist. So every cutout leaf and every pane of glass in every asset anyone has downloaded has been importing as opaque cardboard, silently, for five lessons. Closing that gap turns out to need four separate ideas, one of which takes the z-buffer's most valuable property away from us.

1The problem: three facts that were already true

Most lessons in this course open by wanting something the engine cannot do. This one opens by pointing at something the engine has been doing wrongly, and the difference is worth sitting with, because the wrongness has been invisible.

Three facts, none of them new today:

  1. pipeline_desc has said "no blending" since Lesson 4.4. Not as a to-do — as a description. Its constructor comment reads "triangle list, fill mode, CCW-front and cull-back, no depth test yet, no blending". The depth test arrived in 4.7. Blending never did, on either renderer.
  2. Lesson 6.6's glTF importer reads baseColorFactor[3] and nothing else about transparency. gltf_material_desc::alpha has existed since that lesson, carrying a number that literally nothing consumed. alphaMode and alphaCutoff were not read at all.
  3. That gap is the only one in that importer which fires no status. Every other limit reports: too_many_vertices, unsupported_primitive, a counted and logged factor-versus-texture conflict. 6.6 §10 states its own rule — reported, never truncated — and then names this one exception.

Put them together and you get a specific, checkable claim about the engine as it stood at the end of Lesson 6.10: download any glTF asset with foliage or glass, load it, and it renders as opaque cardboard, with nothing anywhere telling you why. A tree becomes a collection of green rectangles. A window becomes a solid tile. The importer is silent, the renderer is silent, and the picture is confidently wrong.

Why the silence was structural, not sloppy

It is tempting to file this as an oversight and move on. It is more useful to notice why the importer could not report it, because the reason generalises.

A status code says: the file asked for something this engine cannot do. That sentence needs two halves — what the file wants, and what the engine offers. The importer could see the first half perfectly well. It had nothing to compare it against: there was no blend state anywhere in the engine, no alpha_mode type, nothing that could be the other operand of the comparison. You cannot report a conflict with a concept that does not exist.

Which means the gap could not be reported; it could only be removed. That is what this lesson does, and it is the better of the two honest outcomes.

What "transparency" turns out to be four of

The word covers four separate problems, and conflating them is the single most common way to get this subject wrong. They are:

  • A test. Is there a fragment here at all? (Cutout foliage, fences, decals.)
  • A blend. How much of what is behind still shows through? (Glass, smoke, water.)
  • A filter. What happens when a half-covered texel is averaged with its neighbours? (Every bilinear fetch, every mip level.)
  • An order. Which of two overlapping transparent surfaces is composited first? (A per-frame sort, and a hard ceiling on how well it can work.)

Only the second of those is what people usually mean by "alpha blending", and it is the only one of the four that costs the engine its most valuable structural property. We will take them in that order, because each one is a prerequisite for the next.

2Building intuition: a fraction of a pixel

Start with the question the alpha channel is actually answering, because the usual answer — "how see-through it is" — is a description of the effect rather than of the quantity, and it will lead you into arithmetic errors within about two paragraphs.

Alpha is coverage: the fraction of this pixel's area that the surface occupies.

Imagine the edge of a leaf crossing a pixel diagonally. Sixty percent of that little square is leaf; forty percent is whatever is behind. There is no partial transparency anywhere in that picture — the leaf is perfectly opaque and the gap is perfectly empty — and yet the honest colour for that pixel is a mixture, because a pixel is an area and the area contains two things.

That single reframing does most of the work in this lesson:

  • It explains why compositing is a weighted average and not something exotic: you are averaging over the pixel's area, weighted by how much of the area each thing occupies.
  • It explains why alpha is not light, and must never go through a transfer function. A fraction of an area is already linear in the only thing it measures. colour.hpp has said exactly this since Lesson 1.6 — "alpha is a coverage fraction rather than a quantity of light… carry it separately if you need it" — and this lesson is what "carry it separately" turned out to mean.
  • It explains why filtering a texture with an alpha channel is subtle: you are averaging coverages, and the colours you average alongside them belong to regions that may not be there at all.

The three machines

glTF gives a material one enum with three values — OPAQUE, MASK, BLEND — and it reads like a dial from solid to see-through. It is not a dial. The three values select three genuinely different sequences of operations, and they land on opposite sides of the line Lesson 6.5 drew through material: which half of a surface description can be a number in a buffer?

Read the middle column against the right one, because that contrast is the load-bearing structure of the whole lesson:

  • MASK is a number in a buffer. The fragment compares its own coverage against a cutoff and either exists or does not. Nothing about the pipeline changes; alpha_cutoff is a uniform, exactly like roughness. A discarded fragment writes neither colour nor depth, so it occludes nothing, so no draw order can be wrong. Masked geometry is drawn with the opaque geometry and needs no sorting at all.
  • BLEND is pipeline state. Blend factors, a blend op, and — the part that matters most — depth writes turned off. Two objects that differ here cannot be drawn back to back without a pipeline change between them, which is Lesson 4.8's definition of the other side of that line. And because blending reads the destination, the answer depends on what was drawn before it.

The clearest evidence that this split is real rather than pedagogical is the pipeline count. Adding MASK to this engine cost zero new pipelines. Adding BLEND cost six, and §4.6 explains why it is six and not one.
