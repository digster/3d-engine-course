5Implementation

Four filters and a chain that orders them. We build the CPU version first, as this module has every lesson since 6.1 — not because the CPU version ships, but because it is the one you can step through in a debugger and the one the harness can compare the shaders against.

5.1 The knee, on its own

It gets its own function for the same reason apply_tonemap did in 6.12 and is_top_left did in 2.4: a shape you can tabulate, plot and test in isolation is a shape whose bugs have nowhere to hide.

!(luma > 0.0f) rather than luma <= 0.0f, because that spelling also rejects a NaN. A NaN arriving from a shading term would otherwise propagate through every level of the pyramid and out into the composite, turning a single bad fragment into a screen-sized hole.

And the hard cut is reachable on purpose — knee = 0 selects it. The same argument tonemap::clamp made last lesson: a failure you can select is worth more than one you have to describe, and §3.6's flicker is much more convincing when you can turn it on.

5.2 The bright pass: select on luminance, scale the colour

The weight function returns a scalar, and what to do with it is a real decision.

The obvious thing is to apply the threshold per channel — subtract T from red, green and blue independently. Do that and a bright saturated orange has the same constant taken off each channel, which moves it toward white: the filter would be desaturating the very thing it is selecting. A fire would bloom pale.

So: compute the luminance, pass that through the knee, and scale the colour by the ratio. Scaling by a scalar is a move along the ray from black, so the hue and saturation come through untouched.

The order of the four operations inside that loop is load-bearing and it is worth naming, since the shader has to match it exactly:

average the 2×2 block → × exposure → clamp → threshold on luminance → scale

Averaging first is half the firefly story (§5.5), the exposure is §3.7, and the clamp has to come before the threshold or it would be clamping a value the threshold has already reduced.

5.3 The half-texel shift, which is the easiest thing here to get wrong

Every filter in this file is defined by where its taps land relative to texel centres, so sample_bilinear works in texel coordinates where (0.5, 0.5) is the centre of texel (0, 0) — Lesson 3.9's convention, and it matters more here than anywhere it has appeared yet.

Omit those two subtractions and every filter is offset by half a texel. One level of that is invisible; six levels of it compound into a bloom that drifts diagonally away from the thing that produced it, which looks like a coordinate-system bug three files away.

The addressing itself is two mappings that are inverses of each other, and the pyramid only stays aligned because they are:

down: source position = 2·dst + 1 up: source position = (dst + 0.5) × 0.5

5.4 The chain

Nine lines. Note that the upsample loop mutates levels 0 to n−2, so after this call the pyramid is no longer a mip chain of the bright pass — level i holds the sum of levels i through n−1. That is exactly what makes level 0 the answer, and it is why the chain cannot be run a second time without a fresh bright pass.

It also means the gain is not 1. Every level is added at full weight, so for a uniform field level 0 comes out at exactly levels × bright — which is analytically checkable and therefore checked:

Worked example. A uniform field of 3.0, threshold 1.0, hard cut. Every downsample of a constant is that constant; the tent sums to 1, so every upsample adds it again. Level 0 must be levels × (3 − 1).

2 levels → 4.0000 (predicted 4.0) 4 levels → 8.0000 (predicted 8.0) 6 levels → 12.0000 (predicted 12.0)

Which is why bloom_settings::intensity defaults to 0.04 and is documented as a tuned scalar rather than a percentage. Saying so is cheaper than leaving a reader to discover that 0.04 is not four percent.

5.5 Fireflies, and the trade the one-tap downsample forces

A specular highlight can be smaller than a pixel. When it is, it lands in one texel at an enormous value, and that texel is then averaged, blurred and spread across a large area — so a single sub-pixel sample, which appears and vanishes as the camera moves by a pixel, produces a large, blinking glow. These are called fireflies, and the measurement is worse than the description.

Worked example. Drop one pixel of 6.12's polished metal (55,917) into an otherwise ordinary 256×256 field sitting just over the threshold, and measure the finished bloom's total energy with and without it.

without the firefly: 49,152 with the firefly: 132,074 one pixel in 65,536 — 0.00153% of the frame — contributes 62.8% of the bloom

Nearly two thirds of the glow in the frame is produced by a single sample, and it blinks.

The fix is a ceiling on the bright pass, and it is unusually cheap precisely because the firefly is so far out of family — a clamp sixty times above every legitimate value in the frame still catches it. At clamp_max = 100: 99.3% of the firefly's contribution removed, and the honest bloom left within 1.19% of where it was.

It is also a lie, and worth saying so: energy is being discarded. But there is a second, sharper thing hiding here, and the harness found it.

The clamp runs after the 2×2 average, and not by choice. §3.5's one-tap downsample means the averaging happens inside the texture fetch — so by the time the shader sees a number, the four source texels no longer exist separately. Clamping the texels would leave the honest bloom within 0.30% instead of 1.19%, a factor of four better.

That gap is the price of the free box filter, and it is the real reason shipping engines use Karis's 13-tap downsample. Not because it is a wider kernel — because it is the only shape that can see what it is averaging. Exercise 3 builds it.

5.6 The GPU side, and what did not have to be built

Three fragment shaders, and no new vertex shader. That is the clearest evidence that 6.12 drew its abstraction in the right place: every post pass wants the same three corners from fullscreen.vert.hlsl, and only the question asked of each pixel differs.

The downsample is the whole argument of §3.5 as one line of HLSL:

The upsample needs one thing the engine did not have: additive blending. Lesson 6.11 built pipeline_desc::blend() for the over operator; this is the other classic one, and it is six fields to say "add".

Three things are worth noticing about how much simpler src + dst is than over:

  • Alpha is not in the expression. ONE, ONE on both colour and alpha, so 6.11's entire premultiplied-versus-straight distinction evaporates — a source that adds light does not also subtract any.
  • It is order-independent, up to rounding. Addition commutes, so additive geometry needs no back-to-front sort. That is why order_draws exists for over and not for this, and it is why particle systems reach for additive so often.
  • On a float target it does not clip. The same add into an 8-bit UNORM target saturates at 1 — an error of any size, not of the last bit. The upsample chain depends on that, which makes it a dependency worth stating rather than assuming.
Corrected after Lesson 6.18b

This list, and the header comment on blend_add, said the add was order-independent because addition is "commutative and associative", and that a UNORM target "stops being associative in practice" — implying a float one is. Floating-point addition is commutative and not associative: the blender rounds after every add, so the same contributions added in another order can differ in the last bits. Lesson 6.18b measured it, drawing one set of 45,000 additive sparks in two orders into this lesson's half-float target: thousands of channels differed, by up to 17 units in the last place, with the total light equal to a part in ten thousand. The no-sort argument needs only commutativity and stands; a golden-image test of additive geometry needs a tolerance. The comment is corrected in this page's listing too.

Using the hardware's blend rather than reading the destination in the shader is not a micro-optimisation — it is the only legal option. A fragment shader that sampled the texture it was writing would be performing a read-after-write with no defined ordering, which is why every graphics API forbids binding a texture as both input and output of a pass.

And one load op is load-bearing. The upsample blends src + dst, so the destination's existing contents are an operand. Its pass therefore begins with SDL_GPU_LOADOP_LOAD, not DONT_CARE — discard them and the additive blend has nothing to add to, which silently turns the pyramid from a sum over every level into just the widest one. The symptom is a bloom that is far too soft and far too dim, and it looks like a tuning problem rather than a load op.

6The stack: who owns what

Lesson 6.12 shipped a header that said, in its own words:

Deliberately not a "post-processing stack". It is one pass with one input, and Lesson 6.13 is where the question "how do several of these compose, and who owns the intermediate targets?" gets asked properly.

That is a promise on record in a public header, and this is where it comes due.

6.1 Eleven render passes, and why it cannot be fewer

A render pass writes one set of colour targets. The pyramid has six levels at six different sizes, so the bloom is one bright pass, five downsamples and five upsamples: 2n − 1 passes for n levels. There is no way to write two differently-sized targets from one pass, and no way to read a texture in the same pass that writes it.

At eleven, the bloom issues more render passes than the entire rest of this engine's frame — each one a begin/end pair, a pipeline bind, a sampler bind and a uniform push, for a draw of three vertices. That number is the thing to carry into Lesson 6.17, and it is why frame graphs exist.

6.2 The ownership rule, in one sentence each

Two types, and the split between them is the whole answer:

  • gpu_bloom owns the pyramid, because a pass that knows its own intermediates is the only thing that can know their lifetimes. Nothing outside it needs level 3; nothing outside it knows how many levels there are; every intermediate's life begins and ends inside one render call.
  • gpu_post_stack owns what crosses between stages — the float scene target that the scene pass writes and both later stages read, and the exposure that the bright pass and the resolve must agree about.

The pyramid is allocated once and reused every frame, which is not an optimisation but the reason the type is a class: six textures created and destroyed per frame at 60 Hz is 360 driver calls a second, each a potential stall, and that cost does not appear in a profile as a single hot line.

6.3 "No bloom" has to be spelled as a 1×1 black texture

A small thing that teaches a real API constraint. SDL_GPU offers no way to unbind a sampler, and a pipeline whose fragment shader declares t1 must have something bound there on every draw. So "bloom is off" cannot be expressed by omission. The options are:

  • a second tonemap pipeline compiled without the bloom fetch — doubling this pass's pipeline count to save one texel of bandwidth;
  • a branch in the shader — a divergent fetch across a whole wavefront (Lesson 4.1) to save a multiply on some fragments;
  • a 1×1 black texture and an intensity of zero.

One texel wins, and gpu_post_stack keeps one.

6.4 What this type cannot do, stated plainly

You cannot insert a stage without editing it. There is no list, no registry, no add_stage, and no way for a stage to declare what it reads and writes so that something else can compute the lifetimes.

With two stages, that is the right shape. A registry with two entries is an architecture pretending to be a feature — the mistake Lesson 6.5 spent a section arguing against when it declined to give material fields nothing read, and the one 6.12 declined to make with a single stage.

It stops being the right shape somewhere around four or five stages, when intermediates start outliving the stage that produced them and two stages want same-sized targets at different times. At that point you need something that takes a declared read/write set per stage and computes the allocation. That is a frame graph, and it is Lesson 6.17 — which is now carrying a concrete number to justify itself rather than a feeling.

The pipeline count, again. 6.11 made it nine (three surface styles × three blend styles). 6.12 added a resolve against a second colour format: ten. This lesson adds three more — bright, downsample, and the additive upsample — for thirteen, and two of those three differ from each other in nothing but their fragment shader.

That is the point at which enumerating by hand stops being obviously right. A pipeline cache — build the state, hash it, create on a miss — costs a hash function and a map, and stops the count being anybody's problem. We still enumerate, because thirteen is countable and because create_ms() can then tell you what each one cost. Note where the pressure is coming from, though: it is not the number of effects, it is that every effect multiplies against every existing axis.

9Common pitfalls & debugging

The bloom is far too soft and far too dim, and no intensity fixes it

Symptom. A vague wash instead of a glow. Raising intensity brightens the whole frame without sharpening the glow.

Cause. The upsample pass began with DONT_CARE instead of LOAD. Additive blending makes the destination an operand, so discarding it leaves each level holding only the level above — the sum over levels collapses to the widest one alone.

Fix. ci.load_op = SDL_GPU_LOADOP_LOAD on the upsample passes only. Confirm it by checking the gain: a uniform field should give levels × bright at level 0, and a broken chain gives 2 × bright regardless of level count.

The glow drifts diagonally away from what produced it

Symptom. The bloom is offset down and to the right, and the offset grows with the level count.

Cause. A missing half-texel shift, or a downsample and upsample whose mappings are not inverses. Each level contributes half a texel of error and they accumulate in the same direction.

Fix. The two mappings must be 2·dst + 1 going down and (dst + 0.5) × 0.5 coming back up. Test with a single bright pixel in a black buffer: the glow's centre of mass must land on the pixel that produced it.

Every glow has a flat white core

Symptom. The bloom looks like a sticker rather than light, and the core grows with the source's brightness.

Cause. The composite happened after the tonemap curve (§3.8). The curve's output is already in [0, 1], so the addition lands above 1 and the encode clips it.

Fix. Composite in linear light, before the curve. Measured: 324 clipped pixels the wrong way around against 32 the right way.

The bloom turns itself off indoors

Symptom. A dim scene viewed at a high exposure looks bright on screen and blooms nothing; the same geometry under a bright sun blooms everywhere.

Cause. The threshold is being applied to scene-referred values instead of exposure-corrected ones (§3.7).

Fix. Multiply by the exposure in the bright pass, and pass the same number to the resolve. gpu_post_stack::resolve_into hands both the same struct field precisely so they cannot drift.

The bloom is subtly blocky at every scale

Symptom. Soft square edges in the glow, especially while the camera moves.

Cause. The pyramid sampler is filter::nearest. §3.5's identity requires bilinear filtering — the hardware is computing the box average, and a nearest tap turns every downsample into a point decimation.

Fix. Linear, clamp-to-edge. This is a good example of a bug that does not announce itself: five more levels of blurring follow, so the result still looks blurry. It just aliases.

You edit a shader, rebuild, and the program behaves exactly as before

Symptom. The build succeeds, the new HLSL is genuinely compiled and sitting in build/shaders/, and the running program uses the old one. Nothing reports anything.

Cause. This one cost real time while writing this lesson, and it is a general CMake trap rather than a graphics one. The copy that puts compiled shaders beside each executable was an add_custom_command(TARGET x POST_BUILD ...), and a POST_BUILD command runs only when its target is rebuilt. Change only a shader and no executable has any reason to relink — so the copy never fires, and every program keeps the shader it was last linked beside.

Fix. Make the copy a command whose OUTPUT is a stamp file and whose DEPENDS are the compiled shader files, wrapped in a target the executable depends on. Now a changed shader makes the stamp stale, which makes the copy run, which happens before the executable is considered built rather than after. The diagnosis that found it: the deployed .msl was a different size from the canonical one — so when a GPU result disagrees with everything else, check that the binary on disk is the one you think you compiled.

10Exercises

10.1 — Find the knee you actually want guided

Run the demo at --bloom-threshold values of 0.5, 1.0 and 2.0 with knee at 0 and at 0.5 (add a --bloom-knee flag; it is four lines beside the others). Predict, before running, which combination will flicker most as the model turns, and why.

Hint

The flicker comes from pixels crossing the threshold, so it is worst where the image has the most area at luminances near T. A low threshold puts T in the middle of a large smooth gradient; a high one puts it out among the isolated highlights.

10.2 — Measure the tail yourself guided

Re-run §E's measurement with 3, 4 and 8 levels and record the log-log slope at r = 16 each time. At what level count does the inverse-square tail appear, and where does the cliff move?

Solution sketch

The tail needs at least three levels to be a power law at all — two Gaussians do not make a geometric series. The cliff sits at roughly 2n half-resolution texels, so each extra level moves it out by an octave for a cost of a quarter of the previous level.

10.3 — Karis's 13-tap downsample engine challenge

Replace bloom_down.frag.hlsl with the 13-tap kernel from Next Generation Post Processing in Call of Duty: Advanced Warfare: four 2×2 groups plus a centre group, each group averaged and then the five group averages combined with weights (0.125, 0.125, 0.125, 0.125, 0.5). Then add the partial Karis average — weight each group by 1 / (1 + luma) before combining — and remove clamp_max entirely.

Measure the firefly test from §G with each version. You should be able to get below the 0.30% that §G says a per-texel clamp would achieve, because a weighted average discards less than a truncation does. Mirror it in engine::downsample and confirm §J still agrees to the code.

10.4 — Make the kernel resolution-independent guided

The bloom's radius is currently fixed in pixels, so the same scene blooms half as far across the frame at 4K as at 1080p. Scale levels (or radius) with the frame height so the glow covers a constant fraction of the image, and say which of the two you chose and why.

Hint

levels moves the reach in octaves, so it is the coarse control and it changes cost; radius is continuous but widening it too far makes the pyramid's own block structure visible as soft square edges. Doubling the resolution wants exactly one more level, which is a hint about which knob the geometry prefers.

10.5 — Lens dirt engine challenge

Real lens scatter is not isotropic: it picks up the texture of whatever is on and in the glass. Multiply the bloom by a screen-space "dirt" texture during the composite in tonemap.frag.hlsl, so that a bright light reveals smears and specks. One texture binding, one multiply.

Then answer the design question the result raises: the dirt is fixed in screen space while the bloom moves with the scene, so the smears do not move with the camera. Is that correct (the dirt really is on the lens) or wrong (it reads as a post-process rather than as optics)? Both answers are defensible; say which you chose and what would change your mind.

11Recap & next

The engine can now do something it could not do an hour ago, and it is worth stating precisely.

Lesson 6.12 gave it a buffer that can hold a value fifteen stops above white. This lesson gives it a way to show one — not by finding a cleverer curve, which we proved cannot work, but by changing what carries the information. A value the display cannot put in a pixel's intensity goes into the area of the glow around it, and over three decades that conversion is linear to within 8.6%.

Along the way, four things worth keeping:

  • The pyramid is not a cheap approximation. It is a sum of Gaussians whose widths double, whose envelope is an inverse-square tail, and over one octave it decays sixty-seven times more gently than the single Gaussian it supposedly approximates. Cheaper and closer to the physics.
  • Both of the bloom's filters were derived rather than chosen. One bilinear tap is a 2×2 box average; a box convolved with a box is the 1-2-1 tent. Neither was picked for looking right.
  • Every threshold needs a knee, and the knee has exactly one form. Three continuity conditions, three coefficients, one quadratic — and matching the slope is what stops the flicker, not matching the value.
  • Ownership follows lifetime. A pass that knows its own intermediates owns them; the stack owns only what crosses between stages. That answer is correct at two stages and will stop being correct at five.

And one number to carry forward: eleven render passes, for one effect, in an engine whose entire scene is a handful. Lesson 6.17's frame graph now has a concrete problem to solve rather than a feeling.

Next is Lesson 6.14, antialiasing — which is the same question this lesson asked, rotated ninety degrees. Here we had more range than the display could carry and spent area to fix it. There we have more detail than the sample grid can carry, and the pixel grid is once again the thing that cannot be enlarged. The bloom's edges, incidentally, are about to become the smoothest thing in the frame.
