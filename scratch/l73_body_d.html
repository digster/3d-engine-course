Composing is 1.88× cheaper, which is close to what the operation counts predict — four multiplies and two adds against eight and four — and for once the prediction survives, because there is no transcendental anywhere to swamp it. The third row is the one people reach for first and it is the worst. "Just store the angle" is a tempting answer to §1: composing is then a single float addition, which is free. The trouble is that you cannot use an angle — every application needs a cos and a sin — so the fair unit of work is "compose and be ready to apply", and measured that way it is 6.45× the complex product. An angle is a good thing to type and a bad thing to compute with.

The second panel is the honest one. Applying a rotation to a point costs 1.05× — a wash, and the 5% is register traffic rather than arithmetic, since both routes are the same four multiplies and the same two adds. A representation can be strictly better at keeping and combining rotations and not one instruction better at using them. Say this out loud whenever someone claims quaternions are faster: they are faster to store, compose and interpolate, and Lesson 7.4 will measure that applying one to a vector is slower than a matrix, which is exactly why a renderer converts to matrices before it draws.

9.1 Drift, and asking both the same question

A million composed rotations is a plausible amount for a long-lived object in a simulation, and both representations arrive somewhere that is no longer quite a rotation. Comparing them needs care, because "how big is the modulus error" and "how non-orthonormal is the matrix" are not comparable numbers. So the harness converts the complex result to a mat2 and asks both the same question: max |MᵀM − I|.

2.42e−05 against 3.93e−06: the matrix has drifted 6.15× further. Which is what you would expect from a redundant representation — four numbers holding one degree of freedom have three directions to wander in, and the complex number has one.

Neither number is alarming on its own. What differs is what it costs to fix. A mat2 that has drifted needs Gram–Schmidt: normalise the first column, subtract its projection from the second, normalise that. A complex needs §8.4's four arithmetic operations. Here is that function characterised across five decades of drift, with both columns printed for the reason §8.4 gives: 

   renormalised_fast, by modulus and by rotation:
     |z|-1 in      out

     1.0e-04 -> 5.960e-08   rotation moved    0.000 deg  (ok)

Read the residual column down the first five rows and the ε² behaviour is plain: 1e−3 of drift leaves 1.5e−06, and 1e−2 leaves 1.5e−04 — a hundred times the input, squared, each time. That is a Taylor series behaving exactly as advertised.

And then the last row, where the modulus column reads 0.000e+00 — a perfect score — while the rotation is 180° wrong. The function is doing precisely what a truncated series about |z|² = 1 should do when handed |z|² = 4, and the only thing that went wrong was the test. This row is printed on the page rather than described in a comment because it is the clearest example this course has of a measurement that passes while the thing it measures is broken: choose the quantity your check looks at before you choose the threshold.

A timing discipline note: the warm-up

Lesson 7.2's rules — consume the whole result, vary the input with the repetition counter, best of three, and divide by the operation count to see if the answer is physically possible — are all obeyed here and all earned their place there. This lesson added one more.

Without a warm-up pass, the first measurement a freshly-built binary takes reads 15–30% slow: cold instruction cache, cold branch predictors, first-touch page faults on the input arrays. Across five runs, complex * complex reported 0.820 ns once and 0.610–0.628 ns four times, and the odd one out was always the first. Since time_ns takes the minimum of three attempts, one cold attempt out of three is harmless — but the first row of the first section is one cold attempt out of one. One untimed call before the timed ones, and every run now lands in the same band.

10. Interpolation: the path and the schedule

This is 7.2 §10's fourth requirement, and it is the one a rotation matrix cannot meet at all. Average two rotation matrices entrywise and you get something that is not a rotation. Average two unit complex numbers and you get something inside the disc, which is one divide away from being a rotation on the arc between them. That difference is the whole section.

Lesson 7.1 taught us to separate two questions that are easy to run together. Does a blend take the right path — the geodesic, the shortest arc? And does it travel that path on the right schedule — at constant speed? Three answers are possible, and the plane exhibits all three cleanly:

The middle row is the one people get wrong in both directions. Some treat nlerp as a cheap approximation that drifts off course; others as a free lunch. It is neither, and by the end of this section there is a closed form saying exactly what it costs.

10.1 Slerp, in three lines

[[nodiscard]] inline complex complex_slerp(complex a, complex b, float t)
{
    return a * complex_pow_unit(conjugate(a) * b, t);
}

Read it aloud: undo a, take t of the difference, redo a. conjugate(a) * b is the single rotation carrying a onto b — apply the inverse of a and then b. complex_pow_unit(·, t) is a fraction of it. Multiplying back onto a starts the journey where it should start.

\[ \mathrm{slerp}(a, b, t) \;=\; a\,(a^{-1}b)^{t} \] slerp(a, b, t) = a (a^-1 b)^t

That formula is not specific to the plane and not specific to complex numbers. It is the definition of a geodesic on any group where those three operations exist. Lesson 7.5's quaternion slerp is this function with quat substituted for complex; Lesson 7.2's rotation_slerp, written for mat3 in what looks like an entirely different notation, turns out on inspection to be the same three steps — undo, scale the difference, redo.

The angle covered by time t is t · angle_between(a, b) by construction, which is linear in t, which is what constant angular speed means. Measured over two thousand steps of an arc of 145°: 

F. SLERP, NLERP, AND THE DIFFERENCE BETWEEN A PATH AND A SCHEDULE
   arc 145.000 deg, 2000 steps:
     slerp  path 145.0001 deg   speed 1.000501   excess  +0.00%
  [PASS] F.1  slerp travels exactly the arc
  [PASS] F.2  slerp's speed is constant to within 1e-3

10.2 Nlerp travels the right path — and here is why it cannot do otherwise

[[nodiscard]] inline complex complex_nlerp(complex a, complex b, float t)
{
    return normalised(complex{a.re + (b.re - a.re) * t,
                              a.im + (b.im - a.im) * t});
}

Two lerps and a normalise. No atan2, no sin, no cos. And the first surprise is that it does not wander off course at all: 

     nlerp  path 145.0001 deg   speed 11.050107   excess +0.00%
  [PASS] F.3  nlerp wastes no turning (excess below 0.01%)
  [PASS] F.4  nlerp's speed is NOT constant

0.00% excess turning, the same number 7.2's geodesic reported. The reason is structural rather than lucky. The straight segment between two points of the circle stays inside the disc, and every point of it — except the origin, if the endpoints are antipodal — has an argument strictly between the two endpoints' arguments. Normalising changes the modulus and never the argument. So the normalised chord is the arc, point for point: nlerp's path is exactly the geodesic, and it could not be anything else.

A blend that cannot leave the shortest path sounds like it has nothing left to get wrong. It has one thing.

10.3 The schedule, derived

Put the endpoints symmetrically about the real axis, which costs nothing and makes the algebra transparent: a = e^{−iΩ/2}, b = e^{+iΩ/2}, arc Ω. Let u = 2t − 1, which runs from −1 to +1 as t runs from 0 to 1. The lerp is:

\[ p(t) = (1-t)a + tb = \cos\tfrac{\Omega}{2} \;+\; i\,u\,\sin\tfrac{\Omega}{2} \] p(t) = (1-t)a + t b = cos(Omega/2) + i u sin(Omega/2)

The real part does not depend on t at all — the chord is a vertical line, which is obvious once you see it and is the reason this choice of endpoints was worth making. The angle is therefore:

\[ \varphi(t) = \arctan\!\left(u \tan\tfrac{\Omega}{2}\right) \] phi(t) = arctan(u tan(Omega/2)), where u = 2t - 1

Differentiate, remembering du/dt = 2:

\[ \frac{d\varphi}{dt} = \frac{2\tan\frac{\Omega}{2}}{1 + u^2\tan^2\frac{\Omega}{2}} \] dphi/dt = 2 tan(Omega/2) / (1 + u^2 tan^2(Omega/2))

That is largest when u = 0 — the midpoint — and smallest at u = ±1 — the ends. Evaluating both:

  • midpoint: dφ/dt = 2 tan(Ω/2)
  • ends: dφ/dt = 2 tan(Ω/2)·cos²(Ω/2) = 2 sin(Ω/2)cos(Ω/2) = sin Ω

And the ratio between them collapses beautifully:

\[ \frac{\text{fastest}}{\text{slowest}} = \frac{2\tan\frac{\Omega}{2}}{\sin\Omega} = \frac{2\sin\frac{\Omega}{2}/\cos\frac{\Omega}{2}}{2\sin\frac{\Omega}{2}\cos\frac{\Omega}{2}} = \sec^2\tfrac{\Omega}{2} \] fastest / slowest = 2 tan(Omega/2) / sin(Omega) = sec^2(Omega/2)

Nlerp's speed varies by exactly sec²(Ω/2) over the blend. At an arc of 90° that is sec²(45°) = 2 — the middle of the turn happens at twice the rate of the ends. At 179° it is sec²(89.5°) ≈ 13,131.

And since dφ/dt is positive everywhere, φ is monotonic, so the total turning is exactly Ω — which is §10.2's zero excess, falling out of the same derivative that produced the speed ratio.

A derivation deserves a measurement, and at seven arcs it gets one — note the last row, where the two agree to two parts in ten thousand at a ratio of thirteen thousand:

11. A circle without trigonometry

There is a classic optimisation that this representation makes available, and measuring it turns out to be more interesting than applying it.

To generate n points evenly spaced round a circle, the obvious way is a cos and a sin per point. The engine does exactly that in debug_lines::sphere (Lesson 5.11), which draws three great circles of segments points each. The alternative is to compute the step once and then multiply: 

const complex step = complex_from_angle(2.0f * k_pi / static_cast(n));
complex z = complex::identity();
for (int k = 0; k < n; ++k)
{
    use(z.re, z.im);
    z = z * step;                 // four multiplies, two adds — and no trigonometry
}

Two transcendentals per point become one complex multiply per point, with the trig paid once for the whole ring. The operation count says that should be five or six times faster. It is not.

@@FIG_RECURRENCE@@ 
G. GENERATING A CIRCLE: TWO TRANSCENDENTALS, OR ONE MULTIPLY

     cos + sin per point       1.913 ns   independent
     one complex multiply      1.502 ns   (1.27x)   serial chain
     four chains interleaved   0.502 ns   (3.81x)   chain broken
     (D.1's INDEPENDENT complex product: 4-6x a cos+sin pair)
  [PASS] G.1  the recurrence is faster than two transcendentals
  [PASS] G.2  most of its advantage is eaten by the chain

1.21×. A famous optimisation, measured, buys twenty per cent — and the third row says why. z = z * step is a serial dependency chain: each multiply must wait for the previous one to produce its result, so the loop measures instruction latency and the machine's ability to run several multiplies at once is useless. The trig loop has no such chain — every point is computed independently from its own index — so it measures throughput, and a modern core will have four or five sines in flight simultaneously.

Run four circles at once, interleaving four independent chains, and the recurrence gets the same freedom: 0.526 ns, 3.52×. The optimisation is real. It was hiding behind a dependency chain, and no amount of counting multiplies would have found that.

Folklore has a shelf life

"Replace the trigonometry with a recurrence" is advice from an era when a sin cost a hundred cycles and was implemented in a loop. On the machine these numbers came from, a float sin and cos pair costs about 1.85 ns of throughput, which at this clock is a handful of cycles — because it is a short polynomial after a range reduction, and polynomials pipeline.

The advice is not wrong. It is worth 3.5× if you break the chain and 1.2× if you do not, and in 1995 it was worth thirty. Inherited performance advice is a hypothesis, and the harness is how you test it.

11.1 What it costs in accuracy, which is the real question

The recurrence multiplies its own rounding error by itself, n times over. The trig route does not: every point is computed from its own index, so errors do not accumulate. So the interesting measurement is not speed but how long the recurrence stays usable — walked against a double-precision reference at four lengths, with and without §8.4's cheap renormalise:

The renormalise does exactly what it promises and nothing else. The |z|−1 columns show it working perfectly: the plain walk drifts to 3.7e−03 over 16.7 million steps while the tidied one never leaves 6e−08. That is the whole of its job, and it does it for four arithmetic operations.

And it does nothing whatever for the angle — past a quarter of a million steps it makes the angle worse. At n = 16,777,216 the tidied walk is off by 1.28° against the plain walk's 0.72°. The reason is that renormalised_fast scales both components by one factor, which cannot change an argument in exact arithmetic but perturbs it slightly in float — so it is an extra rounding operation per step, contributing error to the one quantity it cannot correct. Renormalising controls the modulus. The phase is on its own.

At the size the engine actually draws circles — 48 segments — the recurrence is wrong by 1.4e−05 degrees, which at any plausible screen scale is four orders of magnitude below one pixel. The plane demo therefore draws its circles this way, and says so in a comment. Whether debug_lines::sphere should is Exercise 7.3.5, and the honest answer involves a number this section has already given you.

12. Where the plane runs out, and what that predicts

Everything above works because the plane's points can be multiplied. Rotations, reflections, interpolation, the lot — all of it is the one multiplication rule, read differently. So the obvious plan for three dimensions is to find the three-dimensional version of complex numbers and repeat.

There isn't one. Not "nobody has found one" — there cannot be one, and the proof is four lines you can check.

12.1 The argument that ended Hamilton's thirteen years

Suppose a three-dimensional number system spanned by 1, i and j, with i² = j² = −1 by analogy with the plane, and with an associative product. Since the space is spanned by those three, the product ij must be one of its own elements:

\[ ij = a + bi + cj, \qquad a, b, c \in \mathbb{R} \] ij = a + b i + c j, with a, b, c real

Multiply both sides on the left by i. On the left-hand side, associativity gives i(ij) = (ii)j = −j. On the right-hand side, expand and use i² = −1 and ij = a + bi + cj again:

\[ i(a + bi + cj) = ai + bi^2 + c(ij) = (-b + ca) + (a + cb)i + c^2 j \] i(a + b i + c j) = a i + b i^2 + c(i j) = (-b + c a) + (a + c b) i + c^2 j

The two sides must agree component by component. Look only at the coefficient of j: the left-hand side has −1 and the right-hand side has c². So:

\[ c^2 = -1, \qquad c \in \mathbb{R} \] c^2 = -1, with c real

And there is no such c. The assumption fails: ij cannot lie in the span of 1, i and j. A fourth basis element is forced — not chosen for elegance, not adopted because it turned out convenient. Forced, by three lines of algebra, in a space that has no room for the answer.

@@FIG_FORCED@@

That is the wall Hamilton spent from 1830 to 1843 walking into, trying every way he could think of to multiply triples. What he eventually realised — famously, on a bridge in Dublin, where he cut the relations into the stonework — was that you need four components and you have to give up commutativity, because the next thing the algebra forces is ij = −ji.

A symbolic argument cannot be "measured", so the harness does the honest alternative: it searches. If some real c did satisfy c² = −1, a fine enough sweep would show a residual heading towards zero. It does not: 

   searching c in [-20, 20] for c^2 = -1:
     best residual 1.0000 at c = 0.000
  [PASS] H.3  no real c has c^2 = -1: the 4th dimension is forced

The residual bottoms out at exactly 1, which is |c² + 1| at c = 0, and rises in both directions. Not evidence of a proof — a proof does not need evidence — but a demonstration that the obstruction is hard rather than a matter of looking harder.

12.2 The thing the plane could never have warned us about

Every quaternion text writes a rotation as a sandwich: v ↦ q v q⁻¹. It is natural to try that here, where we have everything the formula needs, and see what it does.

It does nothing. For any complex z and any v:

\[ z\,v\,\bar{z} = z\,\bar{z}\,v = |z|^2 v \] z v conj(z) = z conj(z) v = |z|^2 v

— because multiplication commutes, so the z and the z̄ slide together and annihilate into a real number. A unit z sandwiching anything returns it unchanged. 

H. WHERE THE PLANE RUNS OUT
   z v conj(z) vs v, 4,000 cases:  worst deviation 9.611e-07
  [PASS] H.1  the two-sided form is the identity in the plane
   CONTROL: z v z turns by 2 theta, to 4.098e-05 deg
  [PASS] H.2  CONTROL: the same factor twice rotates by 2 theta

That is not a curiosity — it is the reason. In the plane the working two-sided form is z v z, the un-conjugated one, which is §7's rotor applied twice and turns by 2θ. The conjugated sandwich is useless here precisely because the algebra commutes.

In three dimensions it is the other way round, and for the same reason read backwards. Multiplication will not commute, so q v q̄ no longer collapses; and the conjugate is needed — rather than q v q — because it is what keeps the result inside the three-dimensional subspace the vectors live in. The plane cannot show you that, because the plane has nothing outside the vectors to fall into.

Four predictions for Lesson 7.4, stated now so they can be checked then
  1. Four components. §12.1 forces the fourth basis element; a rotation of space has three degrees of freedom, so one constraint — unit length — will tie them together.
  2. The product will not commute, because rotations of space do not (§2), and because the algebra that describes them has to reflect that.
  3. A half-angle: cos(θ/2) and sin(θ/2)n, because a rotation is built from two reflections and the object built from two mirrors carries the angle between them, which is half the turn (§7.2).
  4. A double cover, q and −q being the same rotation, because (−q) generates the same rotation that q does for the same reason (−R)² = R² here (§7.5).

None of those is a quaternion fact. All four are visible in the plane, and Lesson 7.4's job is to carry them up a dimension and pay the one price — commutativity — that the trip costs.
