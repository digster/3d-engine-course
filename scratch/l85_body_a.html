Module 8 — Physics · Lesson 8.5

GJK: Convex Distance from a Support Function

8.4's Separating Axis Theorem answered are these two touching? and was honest, in its own doc comment, about the question it could not answer. Two unit cubes whose centres are (2, 2, 2) apart are √3 ≈ 1.732 m apart, corner to corner — and every one of the SAT's fifteen candidate axes reports 1.0, because every one of them is a coordinate axis and the gap along an axis is the offset's component rather than its length. The SAT is not wrong. It is 42.3% low, and on random box pairs it is more than 10% low 5.7% of the time. A lower bound answers a yes/no question and nothing else; a character controller wants to know how far it can step, and a continuous test wants the distance so it can bound the time of impact. Worse, the SAT's fifteen candidates were a fact about boxes. Hand it a cylinder and there is no list to write down. This lesson replaces it with an algorithm that has no list at all. The move is 8.4 §3.2's, finally cashed: two shapes become one set, the Minkowski difference A ⊖ B, and a question about a pair becomes a question about a point — does that set contain the origin? You cannot build the set, and you never need to, because its support function is two calls to theirs. Then GJK searches it with a simplex of at most four points, and the search is honest in a way few algorithms are: it maintains an upper bound (a real vector between two real surface points) and a lower bound (a supporting plane with the origin outside it), and when they meet the distance is not believed but proven — which is what lets this lesson's harness verify GJK without a second GJK. Then the things nobody warns you about, all of them measured. The textbook simplex solver, the one every implementation copies, returns a point that is not on the triangle when the triangle is a sliver — 1,048 failures in 200,000, worst case 14.5% wrong — and a sliver is not an edge case in GJK, it is the last two iterations of every query, so we compute four candidates instead of deciding between seven regions. |v| is monotone by construction, which makes a two-line check both a terminator and a corruption detector: it caught a search converging cleanly to 0.0233827 m and then jumping to 0.0674522. GJK terminates exactly on polytopes and only converges on curved shapes — box, hull and sphere are flat against tolerance while a capsule costs one more pass per decade. Two boxes standing on the same floor produce a difference set in which every vertex has y exactly zero, so the search is confined to a plane and can never build a tetrahedron: 0 of 100,000, against 77,326 once one box is tilted a single degree — which is a problem for 8.6, and named here. There is a resolution floor, and two separate things give out at two different gaps. And 8.4 §11's precision wall is fixed rather than merely repeated: taking every support point relative to its own shape's centre makes the error 96,270× smaller a thousand kilometres from the origin.

Milestone
The engine can measure the distance between any two convex shapes — box, sphere, capsule, arbitrary point set, in any combination — and hand back the two surface points that realise it. convex.hpp reduces a shape to one function pointer, so gjk.cpp never learns what a box is; shape.hpp gains a capsule and a hull to prove it. Nine measured sections, thirty-three checks, every separated pair certified by re-deriving its own two bounds in double from the shape data alone. The gjk demo draws the Minkowski difference beside the world and steps the search one iteration at a time, and its third preset shows the simplex trapped in a plane by nothing more exotic than two crates on a floor.

1. The problem: a lower bound is not a distance

Make two unit cubes. Put the first at the origin and the second at (2, 2, 2), axis aligned, no rotation at all. Ask 8.4's collide how far apart they are. 

const shape cube = box_shape(vec3{0.5f, 0.5f, 0.5f});
const obb A = world_obb(cube, vec3{0.0f, 0.0f, 0.0f}, quat::identity());
const obb B = world_obb(cube, vec3{2.0f, 2.0f, 2.0f}, quat::identity());

const separation s = collide(A, B);
// s.depth == -1.0f       "they are 1 metre apart"

They are not 1 metre apart. The nearest point of the first cube is its (+, +, +) corner at (0.5, 0.5, 0.5); the nearest point of the second is its (−, −, −) corner at (1.5, 1.5, 1.5); and the distance between those two points is

|(1, 1, 1)| = √(1² + 1² + 1²) = √3 = 1.7320508…

So the answer is out by 42.3%, and the interesting part is that nothing is broken. Run the SAT's whole candidate list rather than stopping at the first axis that separates, and the best of all fifteen is still exactly 1.0. That is not a bug in the implementation; it is what the algorithm measures.

@@FIG_LOWERBOUND@@

Here is why. A separating axis test projects both boxes onto a direction and looks for a gap between the two intervals. The gap it finds is the component of the offset along that direction, minus the two projected radii. For these cubes the offset is (2, 2, 2), its component along x is 2, the two radii are 0.5 each, and 2 − 1 = 1. Perfectly correct — and it is a component, while the distance is a length, and a component is never longer than the length it came from.

The gap on an axis is a lower bound on the distance, always, and it is tight only when that axis happens to point along the true separation. For two convex polytopes the direction between the closest points is a face normal when the contact is vertex-to-face, and an edge cross product when it is edge-to-edge — both of which are in the SAT's list. But when the closest features are two vertices, the direction is whatever line joins those two corners, and no finite list built from faces and edges contains it. That is the case above, and it is not rare.

The measurement is §A.2 of this lesson's harness. Over 200,000 random oriented box pairs, 124,216 of them apart, comparing the best of all fifteen SAT axes against the true distance: 

  A.2  200000 random OBB pairs, 124216 of them apart
       SAT best-of-15 / true distance:
         mean  0.9817    worst  0.6901
         more than  1% low: 35671  (28.7%)
         more than 10% low: 7031  (5.7%)

The mean is reassuring and the tail is not. Nearly three in ten separated pairs are reported more than 1% too close together, one in eighteen more than 10%, and the worst case in the run was 31% low. If all you want is a boolean, none of this matters — a proof of separation is a proof of separation however loose the number beside it. The moment you want the number, it does.

1.1 And the axis list was a fact about boxes

There is a second, larger problem, and 8.4's own doc comments keep bumping into it. The fifteen candidates were derived from a specific fact: a box has three distinct face normals and three distinct edge directions. Every step of that derivation consulted the shape.

Hand the same theorem a cylinder and there is no list to write down, because the rim has infinitely many face normals. Hand it an artist's twenty-vertex convex collider and the list is twenty face normals from each shape plus several hundred edge pairs — no longer a test but a survey, and one whose cost grows as the product of the two vertex counts. The Separating Axis Theorem is still true for those shapes. It has simply stopped being an algorithm.

So this lesson does not improve the SAT. It replaces it with something that never asks what shape it is holding, and — this is the part worth sitting with — that turns out to be more accurate rather than less, because knowing less about the shape means having nothing to enumerate and therefore nothing to be wrong about.

2. Building intuition: two sets become one set

Put the boxes away for a moment and think about two people walking around a field. Person A wanders inside one fenced area, person B inside another. You want to know whether the two areas overlap.

Here is a strange way to find out. Instead of watching both people, watch the arrow from B to A: its tail on B, its head on A. As they wander, that arrow changes length and direction; collect every arrow that is possible, and translate all of them so their tails sit at one point. What you get is a cloud of arrowheads, and that cloud is a new region — one region, not two.

Now ask a question about that cloud. When does it contain the point where all the tails are? That happens exactly when some arrow has zero length — when A and B can be standing on the same spot, which is exactly when their two areas overlap. And when the cloud does not contain that point, the shortest arrow from the tail-point into the cloud is the shortest distance between the two areas, because it is one of the arrows, and every arrow runs from a point of B to a point of A.

Two regions have become one region, and a question about a pair has become a question about a point. That is the whole trick, and the rest of this lesson is arithmetic.

Hold on to why this is progress rather than bookkeeping. "Do these two sets intersect?" is a question about a relationship, and relationships are awkward to search: there is no obvious thing to move, no obvious direction to move it in. "Does this one set contain this one point?" is a question about a location, and a location is something you can walk toward. The 8.4 approach searched a space of directions and had to enumerate it; this one searches a space of points and can descend through it.
