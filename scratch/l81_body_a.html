Module 8 — Physics · Lesson 8.1

Integrators: Why One Explodes

Eighty-three lessons in, this engine can rasterize a triangle by hand, light it with a microfacet BRDF, cast a cascaded shadow into it, skin a character across it and put a sound beside it — and nothing in it moves on its own. Every position in every demo so far was authored: a sine of time, a key press, a keyframe, a slider. Module 8 is the subsystem that computes where things are from how they are moving, and before mass, before shapes, before contacts, there is one question in the way. You have a position, a velocity, and an acceleration. Advance all three by h seconds. Go. The answer that occurs to everybody first is unconditionally unstable — not inaccurate, not needs-a-smaller-step, but wrong at every step size there is, adding energy on every step forever. Swap two lines so the position moves at the velocity it has just been given and the same arithmetic preserves phase-space area exactly. Same loads, same multiplies, measured at 0.994 ns against 0.963 ns per body per step, which is a difference smaller than the run-to-run noise. This lesson is about why, and the why is one number: the determinant of a 2×2 matrix, which is 1 + h²ω² for one rule and exactly 1 for the other. On a 1 Hz spring at 60 Hz that surplus is 1.0966% per step, which is ×1.92 energy per second, ×1.13 × 10¹⁷ in one minute — a one-centimetre overlap becomes 3,357 km. Along the way: why the standard yardstick (order of accuracy) is blind to all of this and reads both Euler variants as respectable; what semi-implicit Euler actually conserves, which is not energy but a sheared cousin of it, held to 4.6 × 10⁻⁶ over 3,600 steps while the true energy wobbles by exactly hω; why that shadow quantity being an ellipse rather than a hyperbola is the stability limit hω < 2, verified by bisection at 0.3182939 against 0.3183099; why gravity alone forgives everything and your first falling-cube demo will look fine; and why v *= 0.99f makes four different games on four different monitors.

The engine can advance a state through time, and can tell you whether the step size you picked is a step size that works. engine::phys is one header and one source file: three integration rules over a motion of two vectors, exact linear drag, a frame-rate-independent damping factor, and four diagnostics — spring_energy, shadow_energy, area_factor and a max_stable_step that returns zero for explicit Euler, because that zero is the lesson. Nine measured sections, twelve of them controls, including one that runs the order measurement on a system with no restoring force (where every rule is exact, so the result must be meaningless rather than good) and one that evaluates the determinant at h = 0 (where the answer must be 1 for reasons that have nothing to do with integration). The integrate demo draws all three rules in phase space, where one spirals out of the panel and two trace a single closed curve, and it runs Lesson 1.4's accumulator nested inside the application's own — because the simulation rate is the variable under study and the frame rate is not.

1. The problem: an engine where nothing moves on its own

Go back through the demos in this repository and ask of each one: what decided where that thing is? In hello_cube a sine of the clock. In gimbal three sliders. In rig a keyframe sampled and blended. In ecs_swarm a formula per entity. In audio a circle parameterised by time. In every single case the answer is the same: a person wrote down where it would be, and the program evaluated that function of time.

That is a perfectly good way to make things move, and it is what animation is. It is also the exact opposite of what physics is. A physics engine does not know where anything will be. It knows where everything is now, how fast each thing is going, and what forces are acting — and from those three it works out where everything is a moment later, and then does it again. Nobody authors the arc of a thrown box. The arc is what falls out.

So the first question of Module 8 is not about mass, or shapes, or contacts, or friction. It is the question that has to be answered before any of those mean anything:

You know x and v, and a force tells you how fast v is changing. Advance both by h seconds.

Half of that question has been answered since Module 1. Lesson 1.4 built engine::fixed_step, derived the accumulator, and established why the frame rate must not decide how far the simulation advances — a 144 Hz machine and a 30 Hz machine must agree about where the ball lands, so the simulation gets its own clock and a constant h. That class has been sitting in core/ for eight modules doing exactly one job, and this lesson does not touch it. It has always handed out the h; this lesson is about what runs inside it.

And here is the shape of the trouble, stated up front because it is not what you would guess. The two lines that first occur to you — 

position += velocity * h;      // using the velocity you HAD
velocity += acceleration * h;

— are explicit Euler, and on a spring they are not approximately right. They add energy on every step. Not sometimes, not when h is too big, not when the spring is too stiff: always, at every step size, by a factor you can write down in closed form. A simulation built on those two lines does not drift; it diverges, and it diverges exponentially. The fix is to swap them so the position moves at the velocity it has just been given, and the swap is free.

You might reasonably ask why, if this is so catastrophic, anybody ever ships it — and the honest answer is §7. Under gravity alone, all three rules in this lesson behave, and their error grows linearly at about eight centimetres per second of falling. Your first cube-falling demo will look completely correct. The disaster is reserved for forces that depend on where the body is, and by the end of this module essentially every force does: a contact that pushes a box out of the floor is a stiff spring, a joint that holds an elbow together is a stiff spring, a suspension arm and a soft-body edge and a spring-damper camera are all springs. a = −ω²x is not a toy problem chosen because it is easy to analyse. It is the linearisation of every restoring force in Module 8, and an integrator that cannot survive it cannot carry anything we build on top of it.

2. Building intuition: a step is a bet that the slope holds

Forget springs for a moment and think about a car. The speedometer says 60 km/h. Where are you in one minute?

One kilometre further on — obviously. And that answer is exactly right if and only if the needle does not move for the whole minute. If you are braking, you travel less than a kilometre; if you are accelerating, more. The error is not mysterious and it is not random: it is entirely accounted for by how much the speed changed during the minute.

That is an Euler step, complete. You take the rate you have, assume it holds for the duration of the step, and multiply. The whole of the method is the word assume, and the whole of the error is that the assumption is false: the rate you used is the rate at the start of the step, and by the end it is something else.

@@FIG_TANGENT@@

Figure 1 is that picture drawn properly. The grey curve is where the quantity actually goes; the red line is the tangent at the moment you sampled it, extended by h; the amber gap is what you got wrong. Notice what controls the gap. It is not the value, and it is not the slope — a straight line has a slope and no gap at all. It is the curvature: how fast the slope itself is changing. A step across a straight stretch is free; a step across a bend costs you the bend.

Now the part that is easy to miss. In a physics step there are two of these bets, not one, and they are usually described as though there were only one:

  • Position from velocity. You assume the velocity holds across the step. Its curvature is the acceleration.
  • Velocity from acceleration. You assume the acceleration holds across the step. Its curvature is how fast the force is changing, which for a spring is −ω²·v — proportional to how fast you are already going.

Two bets, and here is the question the rest of this lesson turns on: do you have to make them independently? The obvious code makes them independent — it computes both updates from the same starting state, as though the two lines were happening simultaneously. But they are not simultaneous; they are two lines in a program, and the second one runs after the first. Whichever you do second, you could do it using the answer from the first. That is free information, and explicit Euler throws it away.

3. The theory: Taylor, and what "first order" buys you

Let us make the picture into arithmetic. Write the true position a step later as a function of everything you know now. If the motion is smooth — and it is, between collisions — the value at t + h is the value at t plus the slope times h, plus a correction for the fact that the slope was changing, plus a correction for the fact that that was changing, and so on forever:

\[ x(t+h) \;=\; x(t) \;+\; h\,v(t) \;+\; \frac{h^{2}}{2}\,a(t) \;+\; \frac{h^{3}}{6}\,\dot a(t) \;+\; \cdots \]

In plain text: x(t+h) = x + h·v + (h²/2)·a + (h³/6)·ȧ + … — the position, plus the velocity's contribution over the step, plus the acceleration's, plus the jerk's, forever.

That is the Taylor series, and you do not have to take it on faith. Each term is the previous one's rate of change, given the same treatment: velocity is how fast position changes, so over h seconds it contributes h·v. Acceleration is how fast velocity changes, so the velocity was not really v for the whole step — it averaged v + (h/2)·a, which contributes the extra (h²/2)·a. The pattern continues, each term picking up another factor of h and another division.

Explicit Euler is that series truncated after two terms. That is the entire definition. It keeps x + h·v and throws away everything from (h²/2)·a onward, which — since h is small and the terms shrink — is dominated by the one you dropped first. So the error in a single step is about (h²/2)·a. This is called the local error, and it is O(h²).

But you do not take one step. To simulate T seconds you take T/h of them, and if each costs you O(h²) and there are T/h of them, the bill is T·O(h). One factor of h is paid back by having to take more steps. That is the global error, it is O(h), and "first order" means exactly this and nothing more: halve the step and the error after a fixed amount of simulated time roughly halves.

3.1 One step, by hand

Numbers, because a formula you have not pushed a number through is a formula you do not own. Take the spring this whole lesson uses: a 1 Hz oscillator, so ω = 2π = 6.283185 rad/s, and a = −ω²x = −39.478418·x. Start it pulled out to x = 1 m and released, so v = 0. Step size h = 1/60 s.

The exact answer is available in closed form, which is precisely why this problem is the test case: x(t) = cos(ωt) and v(t) = −ω·sin(ωt). At t = h = 0.0166667, ωt = 0.1047198, and so

\[ x_{\text{exact}} = \cos(0.1047198) = 0.9945219, \qquad v_{\text{exact}} = -6.283185\sin(0.1047198) = -0.6567717 \]

Now the three rules, each pushed through by hand. The acceleration at the start is a₀ = −39.478418 × 1 = −39.478418 m/s², and h·a₀ = −0.6579736.

Three things in that table are worth more than the table. First, the two Euler rules end up with exactly the same velocity, −0.6579736, because they run the identical velocity line — the only thing they disagree about is which velocity the position line was told to move at. Second, their position errors are equal and opposite, both of size 0.005488, and that number is ½h²ω²x = 0.5 × (1/3600) × 39.478418 = 0.0054831 — the Taylor term both of them dropped, appearing once with each sign. Explicit Euler did not move at all when it should have moved inward; semi-implicit Euler moved inward too far by the same amount.

Third, velocity Verlet's position error is 5.0 × 10⁻⁶, a thousand times smaller — because the term it adds, ½·a·h², is the term the other two dropped. What is left over is the next one down, the h⁴ term, and cos's fourth-order coefficient gives ω⁴h⁴/24 = 5.01 × 10⁻⁶. The arithmetic closes.

So far this looks like an ordinary accuracy story: three methods, three error sizes, pick the one you can afford. That story is wrong, and the next section is the measurement that shows it is wrong.
