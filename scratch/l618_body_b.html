5. The atlas, and measuring a packer honestly

Ninety-five glyphs is ninety-five little bitmaps, and ninety-five textures would be ninety-five binds and ninety-five draw calls for one line of text. So they go in one texture, and the question is where.

We use shelf packing: sort the rectangles by descending height, then lay them left to right in rows whose height is set by the first rectangle placed on them. It is a simple algorithm with a known weakness — the waste is the vertical slack under short rectangles sitting on a tall shelf — and it is worth saying why that weakness barely applies here. Shelf packing is bad when heights vary wildly and good when they cluster, and glyph heights at one size cluster hard: every printable ASCII glyph in Karla at 16 px is between 3 and 14 texels tall, and sorting puts the 14s together and the 3s together.

@@FIG_ATLAS@@

Figure 4 is the result: six shelves, descending in height from 14 px to 7 px, holding 17, 14, 17, 15, 16 and 15 glyphs respectively. Sixty-seven of the atlas's 128 rows are used and the rest are untouched.

5.1 The measurement that measures the wrong thing

The obvious way to judge a packer is to ask what fraction of the atlas is glyph. Ours reports:

7,145 texels of glyph ÷ (128 × 128) = 43.6%

That number sounds like a verdict and is not one. Look at the denominator: the atlas is a power of two, so its size was decided before the packer ran — by the observation that 7,145 texels will not fit in 64 × 64 = 4,096. The numerator is the total area of the glyphs plus their padding, which is a property of the font at this size. Neither half of that fraction depends on anything the packer did. A perfect packer scores 43.6% and a hopeless one that put every glyph on its own shelf would score 43.6% too.

The honest question is: of the space the packer reached for, how much did it use? That is the glyph area over the rows it actually touched:

7,145 ÷ (128 × 67) = 83.3%

Now both halves move when the algorithm changes. Put every glyph on its own shelf and the denominator explodes; sort ascending instead of descending and the slack grows. 83.3% says shelf packing wastes about a sixth of the rows it uses, which for an algorithm that is fifteen lines is a good trade, and it says so in a way that could have come out badly.

A check that passed on infinity

This section shipped its first draft against a stale library, where used_height was still zero. The denominator was therefore zero, efficiency was inf, and the assertion efficiency > 80.0 is true for infinity. The harness printed shelf efficiency = inf% and reported a PASS.

That is Lesson 6.16's golden_615 finding — a comparison that printed identical=YES when both of its file reads failed — arriving in a division instead of a comparison. A check whose degenerate case is a pass is not a check. The version in the listings asserts that the footprint is plausible before it divides by it, and asserts std::isfinite on the result.

5.2 Padding, and 6.10's argument in a new place

Glyphs get one texel of empty space around them. The reason is the same one Lesson 6.10 gave for atlas padding under minification, arriving at the other end of the scale: a bilinear tap at the edge of a rectangle reads half of the texel outside it. Pack the next glyph flush against this one and that texel belongs to a neighbour, so a fraction of the wrong letter bleeds into this one's edge.

The harness bakes a second atlas with padding = 0 and counts, for every glyph, how many of the texels immediately right of its rectangle carry somebody else's ink:

paddingedge-adjacent texels carrying a neighbour's ink
10 of 842
0398 of 842 — 47.3%

Nearly half the edge texels. And here is the honest qualifier, which matters more than the number: at a 1:1 blit with nearest filtering, none of this happens. A nearest fetch at a pixel centre reads exactly one texel, so an unpadded atlas draws perfectly today. The padding is insurance against the first person who scales the overlay, rotates it, or turns on mip generation — and it costs 1,559 texels in an atlas that had 9,239 spare rows' worth.

Which sets the rule for a mipped atlas, because it is the same argument scaled: a level-N texel spans 2N of level 0's, so a chain L levels deep needs 2L texels of padding. One texel buys you level 0 and nothing else, and font_atlas::padding is reported precisely so a consumer can work out how deep a chain it is allowed to build.

5.3 One texel that is entirely covered

A HUD is not only text. There is a panel behind the readout, a divider under the heading, a bar that fills up. Those are untextured rectangles, and drawing them looks like it needs a second pipeline with no texture bound — a state change between the panel and the words on top of it.

It does not, because a solid quad is a textured quad whose coverage happens to be 1 everywhere. The bake reserves a 2 × 2 block of fully-covered texels at the top-left of the first shelf and reports its centre's texture coordinates, and overlay_batch::rect emits a quad with all four corners at that coordinate. The panel and the string are then in one vertex buffer, in one draw call, with nothing between them.

Two by two rather than one by one, deliberately: a bilinear tap at the exact centre of a 1 × 1 block is correct, and one bit of drift in the rasterizer's interpolation reads the transparent padding beside it, which gives the panel a faint dark edge. Two texels leave half a texel of slack in every direction. Placing it at the start of the first shelf — rather than in its own reserved strip — means that on any atlas whose tallest glyph is at least two texels high, it costs no area at all.

6. Decoding, and three rules that are security bugs

A string arrives as UTF-8 bytes and the atlas is indexed by codepoint, so something has to decode. This is twenty lines and it is worth every one of them, because the failure mode of a sloppy decoder is not a rendering bug.

The arithmetic is the easy part. A leading byte's high bits say how many continuation bytes follow — 0xxxxxxx is one byte, 110xxxxx is two, 1110xxxx three, 11110xxx four — and each continuation contributes six bits. Three rules that most hand-rolled decoders omit are the part that matters:

  1. Over-long forms. The two-byte sequence 0xC0 0xAF decodes, by the arithmetic above, to U+002F — the character /. A naive decoder returns a slash. A path check performed on the raw bytes before decoding therefore sees nothing to reject, and the slash appears after the check. This is the classic directory-traversal vector, it has shipped in web servers and in Java's own decoder, and the rule that stops it is one line: a codepoint must use the shortest sequence that can represent it.
  2. Surrogates. U+D800 through U+DFFF are UTF-16 machinery — the mechanism by which UTF-16 represents codepoints above the basic plane — and are not characters. Encoded in UTF-8 they are ill-formed; the practice of doing it anyway has a name, CESU-8, and it is a compatibility hack rather than a format.
  3. Beyond U+10FFFF. The four-byte form can express up to U+1FFFFF. Unicode stops at U+10FFFF, and the extra range is not reserved for later, it is invalid.

And a fourth that is not about validity at all: a malformed byte must still advance the pointer. A decoder that returns U+FFFD without consuming anything is an infinite loop, which is the other way this function can become a security bug. The harness feeds it five consecutive malformed bytes and asserts that five calls consume all five.

7. Stem weight: the colour space of a glyph

Now the part this course has been building toward since Lesson 1.6.

Compositing a glyph over a background is Porter and Duff's over operator:

result = source × a + destination × (1 − a)

where a is the glyph's coverage at that pixel. Lesson 6.11 established the key fact about that expression: it is a lerp, and a lerp between two light values must happen in linear light. Lerping sRGB codes does not lerp the light they encode. Lesson 6.11 measured the single-pixel case — half-coverage white over black gives 0.5 of the light correctly and 0.2159 incorrectly, 43% — and this lesson is where that number becomes an artefact with a name.

7.1 Why text is the worst case

A 16 px glyph is mostly edge. Its stems are one to two pixels wide, so a large fraction of its pixels are partially covered — and partially covered pixels are exactly where the two blend spaces disagree. Worse, the disagreement is not uniform: it is largest where the coverage is lowest.

A tenth-covered pixel emits a tenth of the light it should. The faintest parts of a glyph — the tapers, the joins, the antialiased flanks of every stem — very nearly disappear. That is why this bug presents as weight rather than as a colour shift: the interior of a stem is fully covered and comes out right, and everything around it is wrong.

7.2 The measurement: ink mass

To put a number on a whole string we need a measure of how much ink is on the page. The harness uses ink mass: the total light the text adds, summed over every pixel, in linear light.

mass = Σpixels ( luminance(pixel) − luminance(background) )

Linear light rather than code values, deliberately — the question is "does this text emit the right amount of light", and summing sRGB codes would beg exactly the question under test. Rendering "Handgloves 0123" at 16 px, white on black:

blend spaceink massshare of correct
linear (correct)253.04100.0%
sRGB codes157.2862.2%

Thirty-eight percent of the light, gone. And now the same string, black on white — where ink mass is negative, because the text removes light from the page, so we take the magnitude:

blend spaceink massshare of correct
linear (correct)252.73100.0%
sRGB codes348.37137.8%

The same error, in opposite directions. Light text on dark gets thinner; dark text on light gets fatter. That asymmetry is the reason this bug is so durable: half the people looking at it see text that is too spindly and half see text that is too heavy, both conclude the font is wrong, and neither suspects the compositor.

7.3 Two bugs that are the same function

There is a second, entirely independent way to get this wrong, and it lives in a different file. Upload the coverage atlas as an _SRGB texture and the sampler will "decode" every texel on the way out — applying an inverse gamma to an area fraction, which is a category error, since coverage was never encoded through anything. The measured result:

bugink mass, white on blackshare
lerping sRGB codes157.276362.2%
_SRGB coverage atlas157.233862.1%

That is not a coincidence, and noticing it is the most useful thing in this section. Work both out for white over black:

lerping codes: code = 255a, so light = srgb_to_linear(a)
_SRGB atlas: a′ = srgb_to_linear(a), and the linear blend emits a′

They are the same function, applied at different points in the pipeline. One is a colour-space error in the blend equation; the other is a format error on a texture; they are in different files, written by different people, on different days — and on a dark background they are numerically indistinguishable. The remaining 0.04 of difference is the 8-bit quantisation of the modified atlas.

So measuring light-on-dark text cannot tell you which one you have. Figure 5's right-hand table is the test that can:

bugwhite on blackblack on white
lerping sRGB codes62.2%137.8%
_SRGB coverage atlas62.1%62.1%

Render your text both ways round. A blend-space bug fattens dark-on-light, because it corrupts the lerp and the lerp is directional. An atlas-format bug thins both, because it corrupts the coverage before the lerp ever sees it and coverage does not know which way the contrast runs. That is a diagnostic you can run in five minutes on any text renderer, including one you did not write.

7.4 The part that is usually left out: text is tuned for the wrong arithmetic

Everything above says linear compositing is correct, and it is. Here is the complication, and leaving it out is how you end up in an argument with a type designer.

Outlines are drawn, and hinted, and their coverage tuned, against what the renderer of the day actually did — and for thirty years the renderer of the day blended in sRGB space. So switching to correct blending makes light-on-dark text measurably lighter than its designer intended it to look. The correct answer is not to go back to the wrong arithmetic. It is stem darkening: a deliberate adjustment to coverage, applied before compositing, that every serious text stack has. FreeType calls it stem darkening, Skia folds it into gamma and contrast, and macOS's historical "font smoothing" was a version of it.

Ours is the simplest honest form — a power curve, a′ = a^(1−k), which pushes partially-covered pixels up and leaves 0 and 1 exactly where they are. At k = 0.2 a half-covered pixel becomes 0.5743, a 15% gain in coverage where it matters and none at the ends. Measured over the whole string:

casek = 0k = 0.2change
white on black253.04281.59+11.3%
black on white252.73281.34+11.3%

And there is the reason it is a parameter and not the default. The correction is identical in both directions, because it is a function of coverage and coverage does not know which way the contrast runs. It helps light-on-dark, where correct blending thinned the glyph — and it hurts dark-on-light by exactly the same 11.3%, where correct blending already gives the right weight. One global constant cannot be right for both. A real implementation varies k with the contrast direction and the point size; ours exposes the knob, defaults it to zero, and says what it would take to set it properly.

7.5 Where the blend actually happens, and why the format decides

On the GPU, none of this arithmetic is in our shader. Hardware blending happens after the fragment shader, in the raster operations stage, on whatever is in the target — so what decides the blend space is the colour target's format:

  • an _SRGB target: the ROP decodes the destination, lerps in linear light, and re-encodes. Correct, and free.
  • a plain UNORM target with a shader that encodes its own output: the ROP lerps sRGB codes. That is blend_over_encoded, in silicon.

Lesson 6.1 predicted this in a comment — "hardware blending happens after the shader, and if we encode there, the blender adds codes" — and Lesson 6.11 put the 43% on it. This lesson can finally measure it on real hardware, because this engine still supports both paths: gpu_device asks for an _SRGB swapchain and falls back to UNORM plus a shader-side encode when SDR_LINEAR composition is unavailable.

So §11 draws the same batch twice — once at an _SRGB target, once at a UNORM one with encode = 1 — and compares each against the two CPU implementations:

the UNORM target's own blender, compared against…pixels differing
blend_over_encoded (the wrong one)0
blend_over (the right one)933

Zero. Not "close to" — the hardware fallback path reproduces our deliberately-wrong CPU function exactly, pixel for pixel, on a Metal backend. A prediction written in a comment seventeen lessons ago, discharged with an equality.

8. Before the curve or after it

The engine's frame now ends: scene → HDR float target → bloom → tonemap → swapchain. An overlay could composite at two points in that chain, and this is not a matter of taste.

@@FIG_WHERE@@

Composite into the HDR target, before the tonemap, and the text goes through the curve with everything else. Pure white at linear 1.0 comes out of the ACES fit at:

ACES(1.0) = 0.803797 → sRGB code 232

Your white UI is grey. Not dramatically — 232 rather than 255 — which is worse than dramatically, because it looks like a design choice. And it is grey by an amount that changes with the exposure:

exposure0.25×0.5×1×2×4×
white becomes code165206232245252

A HUD that dims when the player walks into a dark room is not a stylistic decision, it is that table. It would also bloom, because the bloom pass runs between the two — which some games want deliberately (a holographic visor, a diegetic UI drawn on glass) and which no debug overlay wants ever.

Composite into the swapchain, after the tonemap, and white is 255 and stays 255. That is the right answer, and the reason is the line Lesson 6.12 drew through the whole module: a UI colour is display-referred. It is a code somebody picked in a colour picker, not a quantity of light arriving from a surface. Scene-referred values belong before the tonemap; display-referred values belong after it. Text is the first thing in this course that is display-referred from birth.

The exception, stated so you know it is a choice

Diegetic UI — an interface that exists inside the world, on a screen the character is looking at — should composite before the tonemap, because it is light in the scene. It should bloom, it should be exposed, and it should dim when the room is bright, because a real monitor does. The rule is not "always after"; it is "decide whether this is light or a code", and then put it on the matching side of the curve. Our overlay is a code.
