Module 8 — Physics · Lesson 8.11

Constraints and Joints: Hinge and Ball-Socket

Lesson 8.10's solver holds a stack of crates up and cannot hold a lamp up. A contact may push and may not pull, and a lamp on a chain is nothing but pulling. This lesson takes 8.9's three lines — a relative velocity along a direction, divided by an effective mass, clamped — and writes them once, for any question of that shape, as a Jacobian row: twelve numbers saying which combination of two bodies' velocities a constraint cares about, and two bounds saying what it is allowed to do about it. A contact normal turns out to be exactly such a row, checked to 1.9 × 10⁻⁷ on two hundred real contacts — and the general form turns out to be 27% faster than the hand-written one, which refused the prediction this lesson was handed. Then the new constraints, each a choice of rows and bounds: a rod, a rope (a contact turned inside out, which lets go at exactly the height a rod starts to push), a ball-socket solved as one 3×3 block that is exact where three rows leave up to 92% of the error, and a hinge with a limit and a motor. Along the way the measurements refused three more claims, and each refusal was the better lesson. A joint solved at the velocity level drifts outward along a tangent, by a Pythagorean recurrence that tracks the simulation to 1.7 × 10⁻⁷, and loses ρ·(ω·h)² of its energy every step. Baumgarte, which 8.10 showed throwing crates out of floors, turns out to conserve a turning joint's energy — and to leave 0.35 J of spin in a limb pulled back into its socket. And one number kept arriving, three times, in three different costumes: the parallel-axis ratio ρ = m·d² / (Icm + m·d²). It sets a swinging body's period, the energy a velocity joint loses, and the rate at which a hinge limit converges against its pin — 0.7481 per sweep for a door, measured to four figures, which is why a door with no restitution still bounces off its stop.

1. The problem: a solver that cannot pull

Here is where Lesson 8.10 left the engine. It can drop a crate on a floor and have it land, stop, settle at the slop and go to sleep. It can stack five crates and hold them, partition a yard of towers into islands, and stop paying for any of them once they are still. Every part of that is built on one operation, which 8.9 wrote in three lines and 8.10 wrapped in a loop: take the relative velocity of two bodies at a point, along a direction; divide the error by an effective mass to get an impulse; and clamp the accumulated impulse at zero, because a contact may push and may not pull.

Now hang a lamp from the ceiling. There is no contact between the lamp and the ceiling — they are not touching — and if there were one it would be no use, because what holds a lamp up is a pull. The same is true of a door on its hinges, a trailer on its hitch, a pendulum, a chain, and every limb of the ragdoll 8.12 is going to build. None of it can be said to this solver.

There are three obvious things to try, and each fails in a way that is worth knowing:

  1. A spring. Pull the lamp toward the ceiling with a force proportional to how far it has sagged. That is a penalty method, and 8.9 §1 already priced it: the stiffness you need to hold a small error is a stiffness whose oscillation demands a frame rate you do not have, and a spring tuned to hold a resting lamp still lets a swinging one stretch by centimetres. Nothing about joints makes that argument weaker.
  2. A teleport. After every step, move the lamp back to where the chain says it must be. The position is then right and the velocity is not: gravity keeps adding to it, the teleport never removes any, and a lamp corrected that way is falling faster every frame while appearing to stand still. (Correct the velocity from the displacement and you have invented position-based dynamics, which is a real and good method — and a different engine from this one.)
  3. A contact with the clamp taken off. This one works, and it is the whole of the lesson. 8.9's normal solve with max(0, …) deleted is an impulse that may push or pull along a direction, and pointed along a rod it holds a rod. What it does not do on its own is hold a door: a door needs five things held at once — its hinge pin in three directions, and its axis in two — and the harness in §9 measures what happens to a door given only the first three. It falls over, 176°, in five seconds.

So the job is to take 8.9's normal solve, find out which parts of it were about contacts and which were about constraints in general, and write the general part once. That is a Jacobian row, and the rest of this lesson is choosing rows.

2. Building intuition: a constraint is a question about velocity

Start from what a joint is, stated as geometry. A ball-and-socket says: the point on the lamp that is screwed to the chain, and the point on the chain it is screwed to, are the same point. Write that as a function of where the two bodies are:

\[ C(x_a, q_a, x_b, q_b) \;=\; p_b - p_a \;=\; (x_b + R_b\,\ell_b) - (x_a + R_a\,\ell_a) \;=\; 0 \] C = p_b - p_a = (x_b + R_b l_b) - (x_a + R_a l_a) = 0, with l the anchor in each body's own frame

Here x is a body's centre, R its orientation as a matrix, and ℓ the anchor point in the body's own frame — the place the screw goes, which does not move relative to the body. A rod says the distance between two such points is L. A contact says the separation along the normal is at least zero. Every constraint in this lesson is a function C of the bodies' positions and orientations that the solver must keep at zero (an equality) or keep non-negative (an inequality).

But the solver does not move positions. It changes velocities, and positions change because velocities are not zero. So the useful question is not "what is C" but "how fast is C changing, given the velocities the bodies have right now". Differentiate: ẋ is the velocity v, and the derivative of R ℓ is ω × (R ℓ), which is the lever arm r swept round by the angular velocity — the same v + ω × r that 8.2 called point_velocity. So

\[ \dot C \;=\; (v_b + \omega_b \times r_b) - (v_a + \omega_a \times r_a) \] dC/dt = (v_b + w_b x r_b) - (v_a + w_a x r_a)

and the thing to notice is that this is linear in the twelve numbers V = (v_a, ω_a, v_b, ω_b). Double every velocity and Ċ doubles. A linear function of twelve numbers is a row of twelve coefficients, and that row has a name:

\[ \dot C \;=\; J\,V, \qquad J = \bigl[\;\text{linear}_a \;\big|\; \text{angular}_a \;\big|\; \text{linear}_b \;\big|\; \text{angular}_b\;\bigr] \] dC/dt = J V, with J a row of twelve numbers in four blocks of three

The Jacobian. For a scalar constraint — one number that must be zero — it is one row of twelve; for the ball-socket's three-component C it is three rows. To read off a row, take one component of Ċ along a direction d and turn each cross product round with the scalar triple product, d · (ω × r) = (r × d) · ω:

\[ d\cdot\dot C \;=\; (-d)\cdot v_a \;+\; (-\,r_a \times d)\cdot\omega_a \;+\; d\cdot v_b \;+\; (r_b \times d)\cdot\omega_b \] d . dC/dt = (-d).v_a + (-(r_a x d)).w_a + d.v_b + (r_b x d).w_b

so the row that constrains two material points along a direction d is J = [−d, −(r_a × d), d, r_b × d]. Hold on to that shape; it is going to be a contact normal, a rod, a rope and each axis of a ball-socket, and they will differ only in which d they use and what they are allowed to do.

The impulse is Jᵀλ, and that is not a choice

Now the solver has to push. It could apply any combination of impulses to the two bodies — a twelve-number vector P — so why should the answer be one number? Because of a property every real joint has: an ideal constraint does no work on any motion it allows. A hinge pin does not slow a door that is turning about the pin; a rod does not slow a pendulum that is swinging on its arc. The motions a constraint allows are exactly the V with J·V = 0, and the work an impulse does on a motion is P·V. So P must satisfy P·V = 0 for every V with J·V = 0. The vectors orthogonal to everything orthogonal to J are the multiples of J — that is the whole of the linear algebra — and so

\[ P \;=\; J^{\mathsf T}\lambda \] P = J^T * lambda: the impulse points along the row, and lambda is one scalar

An entire constraint's response is one scalar, λ, in a direction the row already names. For a contact that was obvious — the impulse is along the normal, at the contact point — and now it is a theorem.

The effective mass is 1/(J M⁻¹ Jᵀ)

Apply P = Jᵀλ and each body's velocity changes by M⁻¹P, where M⁻¹ is the block-diagonal inverse mass: 1/m_a on v_a, the world inverse inertia tensor I_a⁻¹ on ω_a, and the same for b. So the rate J·V changes by

\[ \Delta(J V) \;=\; J\,M^{-1} J^{\mathsf T}\,\lambda \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \lambda \;=\; \frac{\text{target} - J V}{J\,M^{-1} J^{\mathsf T}} \] change in JV = (J M^-1 J^T) lambda, so lambda = (target - JV) / (J M^-1 J^T)

J M⁻¹ Jᵀ is a 1×1 matrix — a number — and for the point row it expands to 1/m_a + 1/m_b + (r_a×d)·I_a⁻¹(r_a×d) + (r_b×d)·I_b⁻¹(r_b×d). That is 8.9's effective_mass exactly. 8.9's header called the scalar effective mass "the reason it is a scalar is the whole trick"; the trick, seen from here, is that a one-row Jacobian makes J M⁻¹ Jᵀ one by one. §7 will meet a three-row Jacobian, where it is a 3×3 matrix and the trick stops being free.

Worked, with real numbers. A 10 kg crate of half-extent 0.25 m rests on a fixed floor, and we take the contact at one bottom corner. The floor is body a, so its 1/m and I⁻¹ are zero and only b's halves matter. The normal is n = (0, 1, 0) and the lever arm is r_b = (0.25, −0.25, 0.25), so r_b × n = (−0.25, 0, 0.25), of squared length 0.125. A cube's inertia about any axis through its centre is m·(0.5² + 0.5²)/12 = 0.4167 kg·m², so I⁻¹ is 2.4 times the identity and J M⁻¹ Jᵀ = 1/10 + 2.4 × 0.125 = 0.1 + 0.3 = 0.4. The contact's effective mass is 1/0.4 = 2.5 kg — a quarter of the crate. Three quarters of a push at a corner goes into turning the crate rather than lifting it, and that is the same 0.3 against 0.1 that 8.10 §2 found coupling the four points of one manifold.

What a constraint is allowed to do is two numbers

Solving a row is then 8.9's three lines with the contact taken out: measure J·V, compute the λ that would move it onto its target, add it to the accumulated impulse, clamp the accumulated impulse into the row's bounds, and apply whatever change that leaves. Everything that distinguishes one kind of constraint from another is in the row's direction and in those bounds:

  • A contact may push and not pull: [0, ∞).
  • A rod may do either: (−∞, ∞) — a contact with the clamp taken off.
  • A rope may pull and not push: the contact's row with its direction reversed, so [0, ∞) again, measured the other way.
  • A motor may push or pull up to a limit it has the torque for: [−τh, τh], with a target that is its speed rather than zero.
  • And friction, which 8.9 wrote as a clip to μ times the normal impulse, is a motor whose target speed is zero.
