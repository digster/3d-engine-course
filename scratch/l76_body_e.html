14. Exercises

  1. Break the bind, and watch the residual find it. After bake_inverse_binds, rotate one joint's local_bind by a degree and re-run validate. Plot the residual against the size of the nudge, for nudges from 1e−5 to 1 radian.

    What to notice: it is linear, and it has no threshold. That is what makes it a usable assertion rather than a heuristic — any residual above a few times 1e−6 on a rig of this depth means something is genuinely wrong, and §4.2's depth table tells you how the floor moves with the joint count.

  2. Attach a sword. Add a second mesh to rig — a cube will do — parented to joint 4, and make it follow the limb. Then deliberately multiply it by palette[4] instead and describe what happens.

    Hint: the right matrix is posed_[4], and §5.2 says why. Sketch: at the bind pose the wrong version puts the sword exactly where it would be if it were part of the character's skin — which is to say, it does not move at all — and the difference only appears once the joint moves. A bug that is invisible in the pose you test in is the expensive kind.

  3. Skin the tangents. skin_into deliberately does not touch mesh_data::tangents. Add it — one more skin_directions call — and then re-orthogonalise each tangent against its skinned normal.

    Hint: Lesson 6.7's Gram-Schmidt step, t − (t·n)n, normalised. A vec4 tangent carries handedness in w, and w must be copied rather than blended: it is a sign, not a quantity. What to notice: nothing in Module 7 draws a normal-mapped skinned mesh, so this is work you cannot yet see — which is exactly why the engine left it out rather than doing it quietly and wrongly.

  4. Build the normal palette. §9 accepts up to 36.9° of normal error for a non-uniform joint scale. Build the correct palette — the inverse transpose of each skinning matrix's linear part — blend normals against it, and measure both the error it removes and the cost it adds.

    Hint: transpose(inverse(linear_of(palette[j]))), one per joint. What to notice: §10 says per-joint work is 2.34% of the total, so the arithmetic is nearly free and the real cost is a second array to store and, on the GPU, to upload. Then ask the question the engine asked: is a rig that needs this a rig you should accept?

  5. Weight the tube properly. The demo's weighting is linear in the distance between two joints, which is the simplest thing that works. Replace it with a smooth falloff — smoothstep over the same interval — and compare the pinch.

    What to notice: the worst pinch does not move at all, because the ring at the midpoint is still weighted 0.5/0.5 and §8.1 depends on nothing else. What changes is how quickly the surface recovers on either side. Weighting redistributes the artifact; it cannot remove it.

  6. An engine challenge: a topological sort at import. §4.3 puts the ordering cost on the importer. Write it: take a skeleton whose joints are in arbitrary order, produce the permutation that puts parents first, and apply it to the joints, the inverse binds and every skin_influence that names them.

    Hint: Kahn's algorithm, or repeated passes until nothing moves. What to notice: the third of those three is the one that gets forgotten, and forgetting it produces a character whose vertices are attached to the wrong joints — which looks like a weighting problem and is an indexing one. Write the check first: validate's out_of_order must be zero afterwards and the bind residual must not have moved.

15. Recap & next

The engine can deform a surface. Not place an object — deform one continuous mesh by several joints at once, which is the thing a transform hierarchy structurally cannot do and the thing every character in every game is built on.

What made it tractable was refusing the vocabulary. A skinning matrix is model_from_joint times joint_from_model; the inner labels agree because they name one joint at two times, and the outer labels agree because both are model space. From those two readings came everything else: the blend is well-typed because the matrices share a domain and a codomain, the blend is not a rotation because that set is not closed under addition, and at the bind pose the two factors are inverses, so the whole rig can be tested with one number and no picture.

The inverse binds cost no inversions, because inverting a product reverses it and the inverse chain composes with the same flat loop on the other side — which finally wrote the local_from_parent that Lesson 2.8 declined to write until a caller existed. And the artifact turned out to be a third sighting of the module's half-angle: cos(θ/2) between two joints, cos(θ/2n) across n of them, and the same cosine on the minor axis of an ellipse when the joint bends instead of twisting.

What is missing is the pose. Everything above takes std::span<const transform> and does not care where those transforms came from, and in this lesson they came from two sliders. Lesson 7.7 replaces the sliders with a clip: keyframes on a timeline, sampled at a time, interpolated between — which is where 7.5's quat_slerp and quat_nlerp finally get the caller they were built for, with the threshold already derived (under 73.50° of arc, nlerp is within half a degree and 3.42× cheaper, and a 30 Hz clip's adjacent keyframes are far below that). Then blending two clips, which is the same weighted sum as §1 one level up — and which inherits §8's warning, because blending two poses that are far apart collapses a character for exactly the reason a twisted forearm does.

16. Further reading

  • Jacob Gundersen / Alan Watt & Mark Watt, Advanced Animation and Rendering Techniques (1992), ch. 16. One of the earliest careful write-ups of vertex blending, from before it had settled on a name. Worth it for seeing the technique described without the vocabulary that later grew around it.
  • Ladislav Kavan et al., "Geometric Skinning with Approximate Dual Quaternion Blending", ACM TOG 2008. The fix §10.2 names and does not build. It is the right next step if the candy wrapper is unacceptable for your content, and it is honest about its own artifact — the bulge on the outside of a bent joint — which is the trade rather than a detail.
  • Ilya Baran and Jovan Popović, "Automatic Rigging and Animation of 3D Characters", SIGGRAPH 2007. Where the weights come from when nobody paints them: a heat equation over the surface, which is what "automatic bind" means in every DCC tool. Read it for why smooth falloff is the natural answer rather than a stylistic one — and then against Exercise 5, which finds that smoothness does not touch the worst case.
  • The glTF 2.0 specification, §3.7.4 "Skins". The format this engine will import from, and the place to check two things this lesson asserted: that inverseBindMatrices is an accessor of exactly the matrices §3 derives, and that skins.joints is an arbitrary permutation of node indices — which is what puts §4.3's topological sort on the importer.
  • Real-Time Rendering (4th ed.), §4.4 "Vertex Blending". The standard reference treatment, four pages, with the matrix palette and the GPU formulation. Its equation 4.30 is §1's sum; reading it after this lesson is a good test of whether the vocabulary has stopped being in the way.
  • Marco Tarini et al., "Accurate and Efficient Lighting for Skinned Models", Eurographics 2014. The paper for §9's compromise, taken seriously: what it costs to transform normals correctly under skinning, and when the cheap rule is genuinely enough.
  • The CMake documentation on CMAKE_BUILD_TYPE. Unrelated to skinning, and the reason §10.1's first set of numbers was wrong by a factor of eighteen. The sentence to find is the one that says the default is empty.
