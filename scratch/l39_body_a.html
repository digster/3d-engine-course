Texture Mapping and Bilinear Filtering

Every colour this engine has ever produced came from a rule. Today one comes from an array somebody painted — and the four lines that change in the fill loop are the least interesting part of the lesson. The interesting part is the three questions you have to answer before image[y][x] is a defensible thing to write.

1The Problem

Here is every source of colour in this engine as it stands, in the order we built them. Lesson 2.2 filled a triangle with a constant. Lesson 2.4 blended three corner colours. Lesson 3.2 added checker_at(u, v), which computes a checkerboard from a formula. Lessons 3.6 through 3.8 evaluated a lighting equation. Five sources, and every one of them is a rule: a short piece of arithmetic that turns a position into a colour.

Rules are wonderful. They cost no memory, they are exact at any magnification, and checker_at is the reason we could check uv layouts for seven lessons without an artist. But there is a category of thing a rule cannot express, and it is most of what you see on a screen: wood grain, a face, rust on a bolt, a logo, lichen on stone. You cannot write the formula for a face. The formula is the face — several million numbers of it — and at that point you have stopped writing a rule and started storing an image.

The gap, precisely

Lesson 3.8 shaded every surface in the scene per fragment, with one exception it named at the time: the floor. It stayed unlit, and the reason was not an oversight. shading::uv_checker computes a finished colour from a rule, and there is no albedo in it — nothing for a quantity of light to multiply. The floor has been the only surface in this scene that light does not touch since Lesson 3.6.

So there are two things to fix, and they turn out to be the same fix. We need a colour that comes from data rather than arithmetic, and we need that colour to be the kind of quantity a light can multiply. Both arrive the moment the fragment reads a texture.

And then the actual work starts. Because a texture is an array — 64 by 64 numbers, say — and the question the fill loop asks is "what colour is at u = 0.31, v = 0.77?" That question has no direct answer. There is no entry in the array at 0.31. The array holds 4,096 numbers and the question has infinitely many possible inputs, so every texture lookup that has ever run has been an act of reconstruction: inventing a continuous function from a discrete set of samples, and doing it fast enough to run once per pixel.

That reconstruction is what this lesson is about. The array index is four lines of code. The three questions you have to answer before those four lines mean anything are the rest of it.

2Building Intuition

2.1 An image is a set of measurements

Think about how a photograph is made. Light falls on a grid of sensor wells for a fraction of a second, and each well reports one number: how much light landed there. Not "how much light landed in this little square region" — although that is closer to the physical truth for a real sensor — but, in the model every graphics system uses, how much light there was at that point.

So a 64×64 image is 4,096 measurements of a continuous scene, taken on a regular grid. And the natural mental picture — a grid of little coloured squares, like a mosaic — is wrong in a way that produces real bugs. A mosaic tile has an extent, and a value that is constant over that extent. A measurement has a position and no extent at all.

Figure 1 draws both pictures over the same four numbers. Look at where the samples sit.

@@FIG_SAMPLE@@

This is the sentence to carry out of this lesson, because half its content follows from it mechanically:

A texel is a sample, not a square. Its value belongs at its centre.

Four samples spread evenly across the unit interval do not sit at 0, ¼, ½, ¾ — they sit at ⅛, ⅜, ⅝, ⅞. The interval is divided into four equal shares, and the sample is in the middle of its share. That is the half-texel offset, and notice that we have not written a formula yet; it is a consequence of what the numbers are.

Notice the second thing Figure 1 shows, too, which is where the reconstruction stops. Joining the samples with straight lines gives a value everywhere between the first and last sample — but outside them, there is nothing to interpolate with. That gap is not a rounding detail. It is a whole design decision, and it has a name.

2.2 Three questions before it is a lookup

Before image[y][x] is a defensible thing to write, three questions have to be settled. Each has a visible, nameable failure if you get it wrong, and each is settled independently of the other two.

The three questions a texture lookup must answer
QuestionAnswerThe failure if you get it wrong
Where is (0, 0)? Top-left, v increasing downwards. Not a taste question — it is what SDL_GPU does, and §3.2 quotes the header. Everything is upside down. Obvious on a face, invisible on a checkerboard, and therefore usually discovered late.
What is outside [0, 1]? Three answers, all useful: repeat, mirror, clamp. §3.3. A smeared streak running to the horizon, or a hairline seam along every tile boundary.
What is between texels? Either the nearest one, or a weighted blend of four. §3.4 and §3.5. A blocky mosaic — or, if you forget the half texel, an image that is soft and shifted, in a way nearest-neighbour sampling cannot show you.

Those three questions are exactly the fields of a GPU sampler object. That is not a coincidence and it is worth taking seriously: SDL_GPUSamplerCreateInfo has min_filter, mag_filter, mipmap_mode, address_mode_u, address_mode_v, address_mode_w, and nine more fields for things we will not need until Module 6. Our sampler will mirror it — same names, same enumerator order — so that Module 4's port is a rename rather than a re-education.

Where the image comes from

We are not writing an image decoder this lesson, and the boundary is deliberate rather than a shortcut. Decoding PNG or JPEG is a compression problem, not a graphics one: it is Huffman codes and DEFLATE and colour-space chunks, and none of it teaches you anything about rendering. stb_image is the approved answer (course conventions §4) and it arrives with the asset pipeline.

Everything in this lesson takes an array of texels and does not care where the array came from — which is precisely why generating it in memory costs us nothing. Our test images are a checkerboard and an orientation chart, both computed at startup.

3The Theory

3.1 Texel space

A texture coordinate u runs 0 to 1 across the image, whatever the image's resolution — that independence is the whole point, and it is what lets you swap a 64×64 texture for a 4096×4096 one without touching a mesh. To index the array we need to convert that into a coordinate measured in texels, and there is exactly one sensible way:

\[ x_{\text{texel}} = u \cdot N \] x_texel = u * N

In this coordinate, the image occupies [0, N], integers are the boundaries between texels, and texel i occupies the interval [i, i+1) — with its value living at the midpoint i + 0.5. Both facts matter and they answer different questions:

  • "Which texel does this point land in?" — the nearest-neighbour question. The answer is floor(x_texel), and the half texel plays no part in it.
  • "Which two texel centres does this point lie between?" — the bilinear question. Here the half texel is unavoidable, and §3.4 does it properly.
Worked example — an 8-texel row, sampled at u = 0.31

x_texel = 0.31 × 8 = 2.48. The point is 2.48 texels in from the left edge.

  • Nearest: floor(2.48) = 2. The point lies inside texel 2's interval [2, 3), so the answer is texel 2's value.
  • Bilinear: texel centres are at 2.5 and 1.5, so 2.48 lies between the centres of texels 1 and 2 — not 2 and 3. It is 0.98 of the way from centre 1 to centre 2, so the answer is 0.02 × texel[1] + 0.98 × texel[2].

Note that the two disagree about which texels are even involved, and that is correct. They are answering different questions.

One thing worth flagging before we go further. std::floor, never a cast to int — a cast truncates toward zero, so -0.4 and +0.4 would both land in texel 0 and the image would grow a doubled column straddling the origin. This is the same argument checker_at made in Lesson 3.2, and it matters for the same reason: texture coordinates go negative routinely, the moment a mesh is tiled or a uv is offset or animated.

3.2 Where (0, 0) is, and who disagrees

Lesson 3.5 loaded texture coordinates from an OBJ file, stored them exactly as written, and left a question open in the STATE block — flagged, deliberately, because nothing yet could make the answer observable:

"⚠ THE uv ORIGIN IS STILL OPEN: exporters write v bottom-up, SDL_GPU samples top-down; we store the file's numbers and settle the flip in 3.9, where a sampler first makes it observable."

A sampler is the first thing in this engine that can tell the difference, so this is where it gets settled. And it gets settled by reading the header, not by trying both and keeping the one that looks right. From the "Coordinate System" section of SDL3/SDL_gpu.h, verbatim:

Texture Coordinates: The top-left corner has an x,y coordinate of (0, 0) and extends to the bottom-right corner at (1.0, 1.0). +Y is down.

So v = 0 is the top row of the image and v increases downwards. That is consistent with everything else on the screen side of the projection: the framebuffer counts rows downward from the top (Lesson 1.5), and the viewport transform flips y on the way there (Lesson 2.11). One boundary, crossed once, in a place with a name.

Wavefront OBJ does not agree. It writes vt with the origin at the bottom left and v increasing upwards — the older, mathematician's convention, and what every exporter emits. Figure 2 shows the same coordinate landing on two different colours.

@@FIG_ORIGIN@@
Worked example — the same number, two answers

Take the orientation chart: a 64×64 image whose top-left quadrant is red, top-right green, bottom-left blue, bottom-right amber. Ask both conventions for the colour at (u, v) = (0.25, 0.25).

  • Texture space: v = 0.25 is a quarter of the way down from the top, so the row is 0.25 × 64 = 16, in the top half. Column 16, also in the left half. → red.
  • OBJ space: v = 0.25 is a quarter of the way up from the bottom, i.e. row (1 − 0.25) × 64 = 48, in the bottom half. → blue.

Confirmed against the real sampler in verify_39 §G, which probes all four quadrants and the white corner mark: (0.02, 0.02) returns 0.89 0.86 0.81, the near-white mark, so v = 0 really is the top.

The transform between them is a single subtraction, v → 1 − v. The interesting question is not what but where, and there are three candidate homes with only one right answer.

Three places the uv flip could live
CandidateVerdictWhy
In the OBJ parser No Lesson 3.5 settled that a loader stores what the file says, so two loads of a file are reproducible and diffable. A parser is not a place where data may quietly differ from its source.
In the sampler No The sampler is SDL_GPU's, and SDL_GPU will not flip anything for us. A sampler that "helpfully" flipped would be correct in this renderer and wrong in Module 4's — the worst possible place to hide a convention.
In the import step Yes The boundary where somebody else's data becomes ours. This is where every real engine puts it: Assimp calls it aiProcess_FlipUVs; Unity and Unreal do it on import and never mention it again.

One property makes that safe, and it is not obvious. 1 − v is a reflection of the entire v axis about 0.5, so what does it do to a tiled coordinate like 2.5 — two and a half tiles up? It gives −1.5, whose fractional part under repeat is 0.5: half way down a tile, which is the same point of the same tile. The flip commutes with wrapping. That is what lets it be applied once, at load, and then forgotten. Measured over 100,000 random coordinates in verify_39 §G: worst disagreement 6.5e-06, which is float rounding and nothing else.

3.3 Addressing: three answers, all correct

What is texel 5 of a 4-texel image? The question sounds like a mistake, but every tiled surface asks it constantly — our floor's uvs run from roughly −3 to +3, because they are computed from world position divided by a cell size. There are three useful answers, and a real sampler offers all three per axis, because a strip of road wants to repeat along its length and clamp across its width.

@@FIG_ADDRESS@@

Read the periods off Figure 3. repeat cycles with period 4. mirrored_repeat cycles with period 8 — 0,1,2,3,3,2,1,0 — doubling the edge texel at every fold, which is exactly what makes it continuous there. clamp_to_edge has no period at all: it holds 0 to the left and 3 to the right, forever.

The wrong modulus

i % n is not the repeat rule. C++'s % truncates toward zero, so -1 % 4 is -1, not 3. Index −1 then falls out of range, gets clamped by whatever safety net you have, and paints a one-texel stripe along precisely the boundary a tiling texture was supposed to cross invisibly.

The fix is one line — m = i % n; if (m < 0) m += n; — and adding n once is enough, because i % n is already in (−n, n).

Mirroring has a one-line definition worth knowing, because it makes the implementation obvious: index −1−k maps to the same texel as index k. That is what "reflect about the boundary at 0" means, and it forces the doubled edge texel rather than making it a choice. verify_39 §B checks that identity over every index from −32 to 32.

Worked example — mirrored_repeat, i = 5, n = 4

Period 2n = 8. Fold: m = 5 % 8 = 5. Since 5 ≥ 4 we are in the reflected half, so the answer is 2n − 1 − m = 8 − 1 − 5 = 2.

Check it against the reflection identity: index 5 should mirror index −1 − 5 = −6… and going the other way, −1 − 2 = −3, which the table gives as 2 as well. The sequence reads … 2 3 3 2 … across the fold at index 4, and the repeated 3 is the edge texel appearing on both sides of its own boundary.

There is one more thing to say about addressing, and it is the detail that separates a sampler that seams from one that does not. Under bilinear filtering, the address mode must be applied to each of the four texel indices independently, after the neighbours are chosen. Wrapping the coordinate first and then taking neighbours puts both neighbours inside the image, so a repeating texture blends the last texel with the last texel instead of with the first — a hairline seam along every tile boundary, present only in the filtered mode, and very easy to blame on the geometry.

Measured

With the indices addressed independently, sampling either side of u = 1 gives the identical colour to the last bit: verify_39 §B reports a difference of 0.0000000 across both u = 0 and u = 1. And under clamp_to_edge, sampling far outside at u = 2.5 returns the edge texel exactly — difference 0.0000000 — which is the smear, working as designed.

3.4 The half texel, derived

Now the part that produces more texture bugs than everything else in this lesson combined. We know from §2.1 that texel i's value lives at texel-space coordinate i + 0.5. Bilinear filtering blends the two texels whose centres bracket the sample point. So: given x_texel, which two centres bracket it, and by how much?

The centres are at 0.5, 1.5, 2.5, … — which is an annoying set to take a floor of. So shift the coordinate so that the centres land on the integers:

\[ x_c = x_{\text{texel}} - \tfrac{1}{2} = u \cdot N - \tfrac{1}{2} \] x_c = u * N - 0.5

and now the whole thing falls out with no special cases at all: i₀ = floor(x_c) is the left texel, i₀ + 1 is the right one, and t = x_c − i₀ is how far along we are between them. One subtraction buys the entire indexing scheme.

Leave that subtraction out — and this is the plausible mistake, the one you make if you picture texels as little squares whose corners are at their indices — and every sample lands half a texel from where it belongs. Figure 4 measures exactly that on a step image.

@@FIG_HALF@@
Worked example — sampling exactly at a texel centre

An 8-texel row, sampled at texel 3's centre: u = (3 + 0.5) / 8 = 0.4375. What should come back is texel 3's value, unmodified — a filter that alters a value at the point that value was measured is not a filter, it is a bug.

  • With the offset: x_c = 0.4375 × 8 − 0.5 = 3.5 − 0.5 = 3.0. So i₀ = 3 and t = 0. The weights are (1, 0) and the answer is texel 3, exactly.
  • Without it: x_c = 3.5, so i₀ = 3 and t = 0.5. The answer is ½ × texel[3] + ½ × texel[4] — a blend of two texels at the one place where no blending should happen at all.

Swept over every texel centre of a 16×16 image, verify_39 §C measures the worst error as 0.000000000 with the offset and 0.625 without it. Not "close" — zero.

So the error is a blur. It is also a shift, and Figure 4 pins the size of it: on a step image whose edge sits at u = 0.5, the correct ramp crosses half brightness at exactly u = 0.5000, and the offsetless one crosses at 0.4375. The gap is 0.0625 of the image, which on eight texels is half of one texel — measured by bisection to four decimal places, and it comes out −0.5000.

Why this survives in real codebases

Nearest-neighbour sampling cannot see this error at all. Nearest asks "which texel contains this point", and shifting your idea of where the value lives inside a texel does not change which texel a point is in. verify_39 §C sweeps 160,801 sample positions: under nearest, 0 of them change. Under bilinear, 160,632 of them do — 99.9% of the image.

So a texture pipeline can carry this bug for years while everything looks sharp and correct, and reveal it the day somebody enables filtering — at which point the symptom is "filtering makes everything look soft and slightly misaligned", and the filter gets blamed.

One more consequence, and it is the reason this lesson's headline test is what it is. Get the half texel right and a texture drawn at 1:1 — one screen pixel per texel — comes back bit-identical to the source image, because every sample lands exactly on a centre and every set of weights is (1, 0, 0, 0). Get it wrong and a 1:1 blit is soft. That is the bug behind every UI toolkit whose icons look faintly blurry, and it is checked in verify_39 §A: 0 of 4,096 texels differ, under both filters, against 1,779 for the control.

The half texel exists at both ends

Constructing that 1:1 test taught something the first draft got backwards. This rasterizer evaluates a fragment's attributes at the pixel's integer coordinate — the edge functions in fill_triangle are stepped over integers — so pixel i's sample point is i. A texel's value lives at (i + 0.5) / N. Lining those up requires offsetting the quad's uvs by half a texel, so u runs from 0.5/N to 1 + 0.5/N, not from 0 to 1.

The half-unit question is not a texture quirk. It is what happens whenever a continuous coordinate is mapped onto a discrete grid, and this engine has the same question at the framebuffer end that it has at the texture end. Lesson 2.11 answered one; this one answers the other; a 1:1 blit is exact only when both are answered consistently.

3.5 Bilinear as a double lerp

In two dimensions the sample point is surrounded by four texel centres rather than bracketed by two, and the construction is the obvious one done twice. Interpolate along u on the top pair. Interpolate along u on the bottom pair. Then interpolate between those two results along v. Three lerps, and the name says so: bi-linear.

@@FIG_LERP@@

Write it out. With c₀₀, c₁₀, c₀₁, c₁₁ the four texels and tu, tv the two fractions:

\[ \text{top} = (1-t_u)\,c_{00} + t_u\,c_{10}, \qquad \text{bot} = (1-t_u)\,c_{01} + t_u\,c_{11} \] top = (1 - tu) * c00 + tu * c10; bot = (1 - tu) * c01 + tu * c11
\[ \text{result} = (1-t_v)\,\text{top} + t_v\,\text{bot} \] result = (1 - tv) * top + tv * bot

Multiply that out and the four texels each pick up one weight:

\[ w_{00} = (1-t_u)(1-t_v), \quad w_{10} = t_u(1-t_v), \quad w_{01} = (1-t_u)t_v, \quad w_{11} = t_u t_v \] w00 = (1-tu)(1-tv); w10 = tu(1-tv); w01 = (1-tu)tv; w11 = tu*tv

Two things follow immediately from that expansion, and both are worth more than the formula.

The order of the axes does not matter. Do v first and u second and you multiply out to the identical four weights. There is no hidden asymmetry, no convention to remember. Checked over 200,000 random coordinates in verify_39 §D: worst disagreement 5.96e-08, which is float rounding.

The weights sum to 1. Expand: (1−tu)(1−tv) + tu(1−tv) + (1−tu)tv + tu·tv factors to (1−tv)[(1−tu) + tu] + tv[(1−tu) + tu] = (1−tv) + tv = 1. So this is an average, and an average cannot brighten or darken: filtering a constant image returns the constant, exactly. verify_39 §D checks that too, over 20,000 samples — worst deviation 0.000e+00 — because a weight bug that a busy image hides, a flat image exposes immediately.

You have seen this shape three times now

A weighted average of corner values whose weights sum to one, where the weights are products of distances. That is barycentric interpolation (Lesson 2.3), it is Gouraud shading (2.4), and it is this. The Math Toolbox has been calling them "three ideas wearing different clothes" since Module 2; this is the third of them arriving, and it is the same idea on a quadrilateral rather than a triangle.

The one real difference: barycentric weights are affine in position, so barycentric interpolation of a linear function is exact. Bilinear weights contain the product tu·tv, which is not affine — bilinear interpolation is only piecewise smooth, and its second derivative is discontinuous at every texel boundary. That is why a strongly magnified bilinear texture has a faint diamond-quilt look rather than looking genuinely smooth, and it is what bicubic filtering exists to fix.

3.6 Decode before filter

Here is a question with a wrong answer that produces a picture nobody can quite explain. A texture's texels are sRGB-encoded — that is what a PNG holds, that is what an artist authored, and it is what our Uint32 texels are, like every other stored colour in this engine. Bilinear filtering has to average four of them. Do you average the stored bytes and then decode, or decode each and then average?

Lesson 1.6 answered this and Lesson 2.4 answered it again for vertex colours. A stored value is not a measure of light; it has been through a curve. Averaging encoded values does not average light. So the decode must come first, and the filter operates on quantities of light.

Worked example — a black texel and a white texel, sampled between them

Stored values 0 and 255; the sample lands exactly halfway, so both weights are ½.

  • Decode, then filter (correct). The linear values are 0 and 1, so the average is 0.5 — half the light, which is what "halfway between black and white" physically means. Encoding 0.5 for storage gives 1.055 × 0.5^(1/2.4) − 0.055 = 0.7354, i.e. stored 188.
  • Filter, then decode (wrong). The average byte is 127.5, i.e. stored 128. Decoding that gives ((0.5 + 0.055)/1.055)^2.4 = 0.2139 — barely a fifth of the light, where half was wanted.

The wrong order delivers 42.8% of the light it should. Swept over all 65,536 possible texel pairs in verify_39 §E, the worst case is the pair (0, 255) itself, off by 0.286 of full light. That is not a rounding detail; it is a fifth of the dynamic range.

This is also, precisely, why GPUs have sRGB texture formats. An SDL_GPU_TEXTUREFORMAT_R8G8B8A8_UNORM_SRGB texture is decoded in the sampler, before filtering, in hardware, for free. Bind the same bytes as a plain UNORM format and the hardware filters the encoded values — the wrong answer above, at full speed. The format is not a hint about what the data means; it is an instruction about where the decode happens.

Which settles a design question for our sampler: sample() returns linear_rgb, not Uint32. The decode lives inside the sampler, which puts it before the filter automatically and leaves no way to get the order wrong at a call site.

3.7 Texture × light, and what albedo means

Now the payoff, and it is a one-line change that only looks trivial because the previous section did the work.

Lessons 3.6 to 3.8 evaluated shade(albedo, n, to_eye, …), and albedo was a colour interpolated from the corners. What is an albedo? It is a reflectance: the fraction of arriving light that a surface sends back, per channel. A red surface has a high albedo in red and a low one in green and blue, which is the whole of why it is red.

A fraction multiplies. That is what a fraction is for. So:

\[ L_{\text{out}} = \text{albedo} \times L_{\text{in}} \] outgoing light = albedo * incoming light

and both sides have to be linear quantities for the multiply to mean anything. Our sampler already returns linear light, so an image drops straight into the albedo slot with no conversion at all. Texture and light multiply — not by convention, but because that is what the two things are.

Measured, not asserted

verify_39 §F renders the floor three times: textured and unlit (that is the albedo), white and lit (that is the light), and textured and lit. Then it checks, per pixel, that the third is the product of the first two. Over 32,301 covered pixels, 0 are off by more than 0.02, and the worst disagreement is 0.0076 — which is two units of 8-bit quantisation (1/255 = 0.0039), i.e. the residue of storing three renders in bytes and nothing else.

Worked example — one lit texel, end to end

A texel stores 232 (0xE8, the checkerboard's light square). The fragment's Lambert term works out at 0.62.

  1. Decode. 232/255 = 0.909804, and ((0.909804 + 0.055)/1.055)^2.4 = 0.8070. So this texel reflects 80.7% of the light that hits it.
  2. Multiply. 0.8070 × 0.62 = 0.5003.
  3. Encode. 1.055 × 0.5003^(1/2.4) − 0.055 = 0.7355, so the pixel stores 188.

Notice what happened to the numbers: a 38% reduction in light came out as a reduction from 232 to 188 in stored value — 19%. Do that arithmetic on the stored bytes instead (232 × 0.62 = 144) and you get a surface far too dark. This is Lesson 1.6's error, and it is now reachable from three different directions.

3.8 What this lesson does not solve

Bilinear filtering fixes magnification: a texture stretched larger than its own resolution, where each screen pixel falls between a few texels and wants a smooth blend. That is the common case for a wall you are standing next to, and it is why bilinear is the default.

It does nothing whatsoever for minification — a texture sampled at less than its own resolution, where one screen pixel covers many texels. And the honest statement of why is a counting argument: bilinear reads exactly four texels, always, no matter how many the pixel covers.

@@FIG_MINIFY@@

Figure 6 traces that with real rays. On our floor running to the horizon, one screen pixel covers 0.60 texels at the bottom of the frame and 62.46 texels two rows below the horizon. Bilinear reads four of them. Near row 103 the two curves cross — that is the point at which "enough" stops being enough — and past it the sampler is reading a shrinking fraction of what the pixel actually covers: at the horizon, 4 / 62.46, about 6.4%.

The visible symptom is sparkle: move the camera by a fraction of a pixel and the four texels each pixel happens to land on change completely, so distant detail crawls and fizzes. Measured, with a camera nudge far smaller than one pixel:

Read that table carefully, because it contains a correction to the usual explanation. All four images are 64 texels across, so the footprint is identical in every row — 62.46 texels per pixel at the horizon, whichever one is bound. What changes is how much contrast sits inside that footprint. So aliasing is not caused by a pixel covering many texels; it is caused by a pixel covering many texels that disagree, and the sampler having no way to average them.

The ninety-percent picture

The fix is to have, precomputed, a smaller version of the texture in which those disagreeing texels have already been averaged, and to choose which version to read based on the footprint. That is mipmapping, it costs 33% more memory, and it is Module 6 — along with anisotropic filtering, which handles the case Figure 6 really shows: a footprint that is long in one direction and short in the other, where a single square average blurs more than it should.

What is worth taking from today is the diagnosis rather than the cure: when distant detail sparkles, the sampler is not broken. It is being asked a question it does not have the information to answer.
