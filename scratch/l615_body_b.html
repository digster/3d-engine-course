4The two integrals, and only one of them is exact

We have the environment stored and we know how to weight its texels. Now the integral, which we established in §2.1 cannot be evaluated at run time. Both halves of the BRDF get moved offline, and they get moved offline in completely different ways, because one of them permits it and the other does not.

@@FIG_SPLIT@@

4.1 The diffuse half factors, exactly

Start with Lambert, because Lambert is the easy case and the reason it is easy is worth naming. Lesson 6.2 derived its BRDF:

\[ f_r = \frac{\text{albedo}}{\pi} \]

Look at what is not in that expression: l. The Lambert BRDF does not depend on the incoming direction at all — that is what "diffuse" means. So it is a constant with respect to the integration variable, and a constant comes straight out of an integral:

\[ L_o = \int_\Omega \frac{\text{albedo}}{\pi} L_i(\mathbf{l}) (\mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{l}) \, d\omega = \frac{\text{albedo}}{\pi} \underbrace{\int_\Omega L_i(\mathbf{l}) (\mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{l}) \, d\omega}_{E(\mathbf{n})} \]
(pull the constant out, and what is left is the irradiance E(n))

And now look at what E(n) depends on: the environment, which is fixed, and n. Only the normal. Not the view, not the material, not the position. So it is a function on the sphere of directions, which is to say it is another cube map, which we can compute once and look up with one fetch.

There is no approximation anywhere in that paragraph. That is the sentence to take away. People say "IBL is approximate" and it is half true; the diffuse half is not approximate at all, it is a change in the order of two exact operations. The only error is discretisation, and we can measure it.

The measurement, and the identity it confirms

Feed the convolution a uniform environment of radiance 1 and it must return exactly π — because that is the integral of the cosine over the hemisphere and we did that arithmetic by hand in §2.2. Here is what the code returns:

4.2 The specular half does not factor, and we pretend it does

Now try the same move on the specular lobe. The Cook–Torrance BRDF from Lesson 6.4 is

\[ f_r(\mathbf{l}, \mathbf{v}) = \frac{D(\mathbf{h}) \, G(\mathbf{l}, \mathbf{v}) \, F(\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{h})} {4 (\mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{l})(\mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{v})} \]

and h is the halfway vector between l and v. Every term depends on l, and on l and v together. Nothing comes out of the integral. There is no E(n) to precompute.

The split-sum approximation (Karis, 2013) factors it anyway:

\[ \int_\Omega f_r L_i (\mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{l}) \, d\omega \;\approx\; \underbrace{\left[ \frac{\sum_k L_i(\mathbf{l}_k)(\mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{l}_k)}{\sum_k (\mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{l}_k)} \right]}_{\text{prefiltered environment}} \times \underbrace{\left[ \int_\Omega f_r (\mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{l}) \, d\omega \right]}_{\text{BRDF integral}} \]
(a weighted average of the environment over the lobe, times the integral of the BRDF alone)

Say plainly what just happened: an integral of a product was replaced by a product of integrals. That is not an identity. It is exactly true when one factor is constant over the region the other one cares about, and approximately true when it is nearly so — which for a narrow lobe on a smooth environment it very nearly is, and for a wide lobe on an environment with a sun in it very much is not. We will measure both cases.

What each bracket buys:

  • The left bracket is a blur. It is the environment averaged over the specular lobe's footprint, which is a function of roughness — so it becomes a mip chain indexed by roughness rather than by screen footprint. That is the direct connection to Lesson 6.10, and §5 makes the indexing derivable rather than chosen.
  • The right bracket is a two-dimensional table, and this is the part that is genuinely surprising. It contains no Li, so it does not depend on the environment. And — see below — Schlick's Fresnel makes it independent of F0 too. What is left depends only on (n·v) and roughness. One 64×64 image serves every material in every scene this engine will ever render.

Why the table is two-dimensional and not five

Schlick's approximation to Fresnel, from Lesson 6.4:

\[ F = F_0 + (1 - F_0)(1 - \mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{h})^5 \]

Write fc = (1 − v·h)⁵ and rearrange:

\[ F = F_0 (1 - f_c) + f_c \]

It is linear in F0, with the two coefficients depending only on the geometry. So the integral splits into a part that multiplies F0 and a part that does not, and neither of them mentions F0:

\[ \int f_r (\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{l}) \, d\omega = F_0 \underbrace{\int w (1 - f_c)}_{\text{scale}} + \underbrace{\int w f_c}_{\text{bias}} \]

and the shader does specular = prefiltered × (F0 × scale + bias). Gold, chrome and plastic differ only in F0, which never entered the table. This is the single nicest consequence of Schlick's approximation and it is a large part of why that approximation is universal.

Where D went

One detail in the implementation surprises everybody the first time: the BRDF integrator contains no NDF at all. Here is the estimator, and the whole of the algebra behind it:

We sample directions from GGX's own distribution — the inverse CDF is ggx_importance_sample — so the probability density of a sample is proportional to D(h)(n·h), times the Jacobian 1/(4 v·h) of the change of variables from half-vectors to light directions. A Monte Carlo estimator divides the integrand by the pdf, so

\[ \frac{f_r (\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{l})}{p(\mathbf{l})} = \frac{\dfrac{D G F}{4(\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{l})(\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{v})}(\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{l})} {\dfrac{D (\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{h})}{4(\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{h})}} = \frac{G F (\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{h})}{(\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{h})(\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{v})} \]

The D cancels exactly. So does the 4, and so do both (n·l) factors. What is left is four dot products and the geometry term — which is why the reference implementations of this function look like they forgot the most important part of the BRDF. They did not; importance sampling ate it.

The bake column is 1.0000 all the way down, on a smooth sky: at 128×128 a gradient is captured perfectly. So every deviation in the last column belongs to the approximation, and now look at its shape:

  • At roughness 0.10 it is essentially exact — 0.08% at worst. A narrow lobe barely notices which direction it was centred on. The technique degrades smoothly away from a mirror, which is the opposite of how most approximations behave and is why it is used.
  • At near-normal incidence it never exceeds 5.8% at any roughness.
  • At a grazing view it reaches 29.0% dark. More than four times the error, on exactly the same material.

That asymmetry is not noise. It is the second approximation, which most treatments mention in passing and which does most of the visible damage.

The second assumption: n = v = r

The prefiltered chain is a cube map, so a lookup into it is indexed by one direction. But the true integral depends on two — the normal and the view. There is no way to index a cube map by a pair of directions, so the prefilter has to assume they coincide:

\[ \mathbf{n} = \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{r} \]

which is exactly true when you look straight down at a surface and progressively wronger as you look along it. Two things go wrong at a grazing view, and the measurement above shows both:

  1. The lobe is baked round when it should be stretched. A rough surface seen at a grazing angle shows a long, comet-shaped smear, not a disc. The prefilter cannot represent that, so you get a disc.
  2. The lobe is centred on the wrong thing. It is baked around the reflection direction, and at a grazing view the reflection points nearly along the horizon — so the prefilter's lobe reaches below it and averages in ground the real surface, whose normal points up, never sees at all. Our ground is dark, so the answer comes out dark. That is precisely the 29% in the last row.

And a third cause, which only appears with a sun

Run the same table with the sun disc in the sky and the error grows to 73.0%. That is a different mechanism again, and it is visible in the left bracket of the split sum: the prefilter weights radiance by (n·l) alone, where the true integral weights it by the whole BRDF. Those two weightings disagree most where the environment is nearly a delta function — which is exactly what a sun is.

So the error of this technique depends on the environment, not only on the material. That is not something you can read off the paper, and it is the practical reason production renderers keep the sun as a separate analytic light rather than baking it into the environment map. Our own engine already does that — the key light in scene_light_uniforms is separate — so in practice you would bake a sky without its sun and let the directional light handle it. The exercises ask you to measure the difference.

5Which level? Deriving what everybody guesses

The prefiltered chain has levels, each blurred by a different roughness. A shader with a material of roughness r has to pick one. Open any engine and you will find

…with no argument attached. It is a reasonable-looking line and it is in every reference implementation. Where does it come from, and how wrong is it?

Lesson 6.14 gives us the tools to answer that, which is the nicest piece of continuity in this module. It derived the GGX lobe's half-width in closed form:

\[ \sin t = \alpha \sqrt{\frac{\sqrt2 - 1}{1 - \alpha^2}} \]

Three lines of algebra, no fitting, exact at every roughness. What we need now is to compare that against a texel — and a half-angle is not commensurable with a texel. A solid angle is.

5.1 The derivation

The cap of a cone of half-angle t subtends

\[ \Omega_{\text{lobe}} = 2\pi (1 - \cos t) \]
(the spherical-cap area — the one piece of solid-angle geometry worth memorising; at t = 90° it gives 2π, the whole hemisphere, which is the check to run first)

And a texel at level k of a cube whose base face is n across: each level halves the side, so it quadruples the solid angle.

\[ \Omega_{\text{texel}}(k) = \omega_0 \cdot 4^k \]

Set them equal — which level's texels are about as wide as this lobe? — and take logs:

\[ k = \log_4\!\left( \frac{\Omega_{\text{lobe}}}{\omega_0} \right) = \tfrac12 \log_2\!\left( \frac{\Omega_{\text{lobe}}}{\omega_0} \right) \]

That is prefilter_level_for, and it is three lines of code with no free parameters in it.

One choice inside it has to be stated. ω0 is the solid angle of which level-0 texel? §3.2 showed they differ by 5.196. The code uses the centre texel, and the reason is that we are asking what resolution the map achieves where it is best: a level that cannot resolve the lobe even at the face centre certainly cannot at the corner. Using the corner instead would shift every answer by log₄(5.196) = 1.19 levels — which is more than the entire disagreement we are about to measure, so the choice is not a detail.

The folklore is not arbitrary. Over the whole range the two never differ by more than 0.770 of a level (worst at roughness 0.45), and they agree exactly at both ends. For a line with no derivation attached, that is a good line. For comparison, sqrt(roughness) — which also circulates — is off by 2.214 levels, nearly three times as much.

But we now know something the folklore does not tell you, which is the direction of the error:

  • At low roughness it over-blurs. At 0.10 the lobe wants level 0 and the mapping reads 0.700 — most of a level of blur applied to a near-mirror, which is exactly where you would notice it.
  • From 0.3 to 0.9 it under-blurs, by up to 0.77 of a level. Less visible, because a rough reflection has no detail to lose.

There is also a hard limit hiding in that table. At roughness 0.10 the lobe subtends 0.000130 sr, while a level-0 texel on a 128 face subtends about 0.000244 sr — the lobe is smaller than one texel. Below roughness ≈0.11 the base resolution, not the prefilter, is what limits the reflection. If you want a crisper mirror, the answer is a bigger cube, not a deeper chain.

So which one does the engine ship? The fitted one — and the engine ships the derived one too, as a separate function, with the boundary marked. This is 6.14's discipline applied again: that lesson kept its closed-form lobe width and Tokuyoshi & Kaplanyan's fitted constants in the same file with a comment saying which was which. The reason the lookup must use the linear mapping is not taste: the chain was built with roughness spread linearly across its levels, so reading it any other way would ask for level 4.2 of a table whose level 4.2 means something else. prefilter_level_for's job is to tell you whether your chain is deep enough, not to index it.

Everything above lands in one new pair of files, engine/gfx/cubemap.{hpp,cpp}, plus two small additions to headers that already exist. We will build it in the order the argument was made: the faces, the environment, the two integrals, and then the function that puts them together.

The major axis is +1 on even face indices and −1 on odd ones, which is a free gift from SDL's enum ordering (+X, −X, +Y, −Y, +Z, −Z): the sign is the low bit. That is a small thing to lean on, which is why §A asserts the enum against SDL's rather than trusting it.

6.2 A cube map is six hdr_buffers, and that is not a detail

The faces are hdr_buffer — Lesson 6.12's float container — and not engine::texture. The reason is the one number §D of the harness measures:

Radiance has no upper bound. The sky is around 0.5 and the sun in the same image is five thousand times brighter. Storing that in a [0,1] container clips the sun to white before any integral runs, and the prefilter then faithfully integrates a lie. This is the same argument gpu_post.hpp made for the HDR framebuffer in 6.12, arriving at the other end of the pipeline.

6.3 The irradiance convolution, and the one line it turns on

The diffuse integral is a double loop over output texels and input texels. The body is four lines; here is the part that matters:

p.weight is cube_texel_solid_angle, precomputed. Remove it and you have the 1.01% bias of §3.3, permanently.

Two implementation decisions in there are worth the words:

The input is precomputed into a flat array first. Every output texel integrates over every input texel, so anything depending only on the input — its direction and its solid angle — is hoisted. At 32 out and 128 in, that turns 6.0 × 10⁸ calls to cube_to_direction into 98,304 of them, and the convolution goes from minutes to about four seconds.

The accumulator is double. A sun disc contributes single samples around 6,000 into a sum whose other terms are around 10⁻⁴. In float, once the running total is large enough, the small terms are lost entirely — the classic catastrophic accumulation failure. The symptom would be an irradiance map that is correct near the sun and quietly too dark away from it, which is very hard to attribute.

6.4 Importance sampling, and why not rand()

The prefilter and the BRDF table both need to sample the GGX distribution. Sampling uniformly over the hemisphere would be catastrophic: at roughness 0.10 the lobe is 0.37° wide, so all but roughly one sample in 100,000 lands where D is numerically zero.

GGX's inverse CDF is a closed form — one square root, no rejection and no iteration — and that is a large part of why GGX won:

\[ \cos t = \sqrt{\frac{1 - u_2}{1 + (\alpha^2 - 1) u_2}}, \qquad \phi = 2\pi u_1 \]

The pair (u₁, u₂) comes from hammersley, not from rand(), and the difference is a convergence rate. Random sampling converges as 1/√N; a low-discrepancy sequence, which fills the square evenly rather than randomly, converges close to 1/N on these smooth integrands. §G of the harness shows the table converged to 8 × 10⁻⁴ at 1,024 samples and 3.7 × 10⁻⁴ at 4,096 — where random sampling would want roughly sixteen times as many for the same error.

The y coordinate is the radical inverse in base 2: write the index in binary and reflect it about the binary point, so 1 → 0.1₂ = 0.5, 2 → 0.01₂ = 0.25, 3 → 0.11₂ = 0.75. Consecutive points keep landing in the largest remaining gap, which is the whole idea, and the standard five-step bit twiddle does the reflection without a loop.

6.5 The one honest fudge in the prefilter

§4.2 said the prefilter averages radiance weighted by (n·l). Where does that weight come from? Importance sampling GGX already accounts for D and the Jacobian, and G and Fresnel belong to the other bracket of the split sum — so putting them here would double-count. What is left is (n·l), and the original 2013 course notes document it as producing a visibly better result than uniform weighting rather than as a consequence of anything.

So it is fitted, not derived, and the code says so at the point where it is applied. That is the second fitted step in this lesson — the first being the linear roughness-to-level mapping — and both are marked in the source, which is the habit 6.14 established when it separated its exact lobe width from Tokuyoshi & Kaplanyan's fitted constants.
