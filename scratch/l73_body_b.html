4. The product, derived — and what it does geometrically

We know what a + bi means as a transformation, and we know i² = −1. That is enough to multiply, and the multiplication rule is not invented: it is forced. Take two of these things and multiply them out the way you would multiply any two brackets, treating i as an ordinary symbol, and then use i² = −1 exactly once at the end:

\[ (a + bi)(c + di) = ac + adi + bci + bd\,i^2 = (ac - bd) + (ad + bc)i \] (a + bi)(c + di) = ac + adi + bci + bd i^2 = (ac - bd) + (ad + bc) i

Four multiplies and two adds, and exactly one surprising character in the answer: the minus sign in ac − bd. It is i², and it is nothing else. If you ever forget which term the minus goes on — and you will — do not try to remember it. Multiply the brackets out again; it takes eight seconds and it cannot be got wrong.

4.1 A worked example, by hand

Take z = 3 + 4i and w = 1 + 2i. Substituting a = 3, b = 4, c = 1, d = 2:

\[ (3 + 4i)(1 + 2i) = (3 \cdot 1 - 4 \cdot 2) + (3 \cdot 2 + 4 \cdot 1)i = -5 + 10i \] (3 + 4i)(1 + 2i) = (3*1 - 4*2) + (3*2 + 4*1) i = -5 + 10i

Now measure the three numbers. The modulus of a complex number is its distance from the origin — it is vec2's length, because a complex number and a point of the plane are the same thing:

  • |z| = √(3² + 4²) = √25 = 5
  • |w| = √(1² + 2²) = √5 ≈ 2.2360680
  • |zw| = √(25 + 100) = √125 = 5√5 ≈ 11.1803398

And 5 × 2.2360680 = 11.1803398. The moduli multiplied. Now the arguments — the angle each makes with the positive real axis, which is atan2(im, re):

  • arg z = atan2(4, 3) = 53.13010°
  • arg w = atan2(2, 1) = 63.43495°
  • arg zw = atan2(10, −5) = 116.56505°

And 53.13010 + 63.43495 = 116.56505. The arguments added. Two decimal coincidences in a row would be a coincidence; these are not.

\[ |zw| = |z|\,|w| \qquad\text{and}\qquad \arg(zw) = \arg z + \arg w \] |zw| = |z| |w|, and arg(zw) = arg z + arg w

That is the whole of complex multiplication, geometrically: scale by the modulus, turn by the argument. Which means multiplying by a complex number of modulus 1 is a pure rotation, and composing two rotations is a single multiplication — four multiplies, two adds, no trigonometry anywhere. Which is precisely what §1 asked for.

4.2 Why it is true, in four steps

The numbers above are evidence, not proof. Here is the proof, and it uses nothing but Lesson 2.5's idea that a linear map is determined by where the basis vectors land.

Step 1: multiplication by z is a linear map. Read the formula: each output component is a fixed linear combination of the input components. So z(v + w) = zv + zw and z(sv) = s(zv) for real s. Both are one line of algebra to check and the code makes them obvious.

Step 2: find where the basis vectors land. The basis of the plane is 1 and i. Multiplying 1 by z gives z. Multiplying i by z gives zi, which is z turned a quarter circle — because multiplying by i is what a quarter turn is, by §3's definition.

Step 3: look at what that pair of images is. The two images are z and z-turned-90°. They are perpendicular to each other, and they have the same length, namely |z|. A linear map that sends a perpendicular pair of unit vectors to a perpendicular pair of equal-length vectors is a similarity: it preserves every angle and multiplies every length by one fixed factor. That factor is |z|.

Step 4: find the angle. A similarity that preserves orientation is a rotation followed by a scale, and the rotation is determined by where any one vector goes. The vector 1 goes to z, so the rotation is by arg z. Done.

The triangle with vertices 0, 1, w and the triangle with vertices 0, z, zw are the same triangle — the second is the first scaled by |z| = 5 and turned by arg z = 53.130°. They are drawn at different scales on purpose, so that "same shape" is something your eye can check rather than something you take on trust; the factor of five is then stated between them.

4.4 It commutes, and that will matter enormously

Look at the multiplication rule once more: (ac − bd) + (ad + bc)i. Swap the two operands and you get (ca − db) + (cb + da)i — the same two products of the same four floats, in a different order. So zw = wz, exactly, bit for bit.

Geometrically that is the observation from §2: turn the paper 30° then 50°, or 50° then 30°, and it lands in the same place. Plane rotations commute, so the algebra that describes them may. It is worth noticing how easily this passes by unremarked — commutativity is so familiar from ordinary arithmetic that its presence here feels like nothing at all.

It is not nothing. It is the one structural fact that does not survive the move to three dimensions, and §12 will show that giving it up is not optional but forced. When you meet a quaternion library's warning that q₁q₂ ≠ q₂q₁, this is the property it is warning you that you have lost.

5. Complex numbers are 2×2 matrices

You may have been uneasy for two sections now. We invented a symbol, gave it a geometric meaning, derived a multiplication rule, and got a rotation out — but is any of this real, in the sense that the mat2 from Lesson 2.5 is real?

Yes, and here is the proof, which takes one paragraph. Lesson 2.5's whole idea is that a matrix is the answer to "where do the basis vectors land?", with the columns being those images. We worked that out in §4.2: multiplication by z = a + bi sends 1 to a + bi and sends i to zi = (a + bi)i = ai + bi² = −b + ai. Those are the two columns. Written on paper:

\[ M(a + bi) \;=\; \begin{pmatrix} a & -b \\ b & a \end{pmatrix} \] M(a + bi) = [[a, -b], [b, a]] in written (row) notation

Every complex number is such a matrix and every such matrix is a complex number. Set a = cos θ and b = sin θ and you have [[cos θ, −sin θ], [sin θ, cos θ]], which is mat2's rotation character for character — the same function this engine has had since Lesson 2.5, derived there by exactly the same argument about where (1,0) and (0,1) land.

So complex numbers are not a parallel invention. They are a two-parameter family of matrices the engine already had, stored without the redundancy.

5.1 The correspondence respects multiplication

Sharing a shape is not enough; two things are the same algebra only if the operations agree. They do:

\[ M(z)\,M(w) = M(zw) \] M(z) M(w) = M(zw)

You can verify that by hand in about a minute — multiply the two matrices out and compare the four entries against the product rule from §4 — and the harness verifies it over two thousand random pairs. This is what makes the correspondence an isomorphism rather than a coincidence of shape: anything you can say with one, you can say with the other.

5.2 So which two floats are redundant?

Look at [[cos θ, −sin θ], [sin θ, cos θ]] as four numbers in memory. In the engine's column-major storage (Conventions §6) they are c0.x = cos θ, c0.y = sin θ, c1.x = −sin θ, c1.y = cos θ. The third and fourth are the second and first again, one of them negated. A mat2 holding a rotation stores cos θ and sin θ twice. Figure 3 is that, drawn, with what the duplication costs.

5.3 What happens to a matrix that is not of that form

complex_from_mat2 goes the other way by reading the first column, which by §5's argument is the image of 1 and therefore is the number itself. But a general mat2 also shears and reflects, and neither is a complex multiplication. What does the function do when handed one? 

   a shear k=0.7 read as a complex:  +1.0000 +0.0000i  (0.00 deg)

It reports the identity, confidently and silently, and throws the entire shear away. That is not a bug — it is the documented behaviour of a function that reads one column — but it is exactly the kind of thing that must be on record rather than discovered. The measured 0.7000 is the shear factor, arriving back as the size of what was lost, and the control beside it rules out the other explanation: a rotation round-trips to zero, so C.3 is reporting a matrix outside the family rather than a broken round trip.

6. Euler's formula, from a picture

There is one more piece of standard equipment to derive, and it is the one most often presented as a magic trick:

\[ e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta \] exp(i theta) = cos(theta) + i sin(theta)

Most treatments get there by writing down the power series for exp, substituting iθ, and sorting the terms into the series for cos and sin. That derivation is correct and it explains nothing: at the end you believe the identity and have no idea why an exponential would have anything to do with a circle.

Here is the other route, which is three lines and is a picture.

Line one. Take a point going round the unit circle at unit speed, and let z(θ) be where it is after turning through θ. Its velocity — z′(θ), the derivative — is tangent to the circle, and the tangent to a circle is perpendicular to the radius. It also has length 1, because the point moves at unit speed.

Line two. "Perpendicular to z, and the same length as z" is exactly what iz is — that is §3's definition of i, applied to z. So:

\[ z'(\theta) = i\,z(\theta), \qquad z(0) = 1 \] z'(theta) = i z(theta), with z(0) = 1

Line three. That is the differential equation whose solution everybody already knows: the function whose derivative is itself times a constant k is e^{kθ}, and the initial condition z(0) = 1 picks out that one. So z(θ) = e^{iθ}. And we already know what z(θ) is by looking at the picture — it is the point at angle θ on the unit circle, which is cos θ + i sin θ. The two descriptions are of the same function, so they are equal. Figure 4 is the whole argument.

The ten per cent this skips

The step "the only function whose derivative is itself times a constant is the exponential" is doing real work, and doing it for a function that takes complex values needs a definition of e^w for complex w and a uniqueness theorem. The honest summary is that both are available and neither changes the picture: define e^w by its power series (which converges for every complex w), and uniqueness follows from differentiating z(θ)e^{−iθ} and finding it constant.

This is the ninety per cent picture. The missing ten per cent is that definition and that one-line uniqueness argument, and Needham's Visual Complex Analysis chapter 1 does both properly and geometrically.

6.1 A numeric check, and a free theorem

Take θ = 60° = π/3. Then e^{iπ/3} = cos 60° + i sin 60° = 0.5 + 0.8660254i. Multiply v = 2 + i by it, using §4's rule with a = 0.5, b = 0.8660254, c = 2, d = 1:

\[ \begin{aligned} (0.5 + 0.8660254i)(2 + i) &= (1.0 - 0.8660254) + (0.5 + 1.7320508)i \\ &= 0.1339746 + 2.2320508i \end{aligned} \] (0.5 + 0.8660254i)(2 + i) = (1.0 - 0.8660254) + (0.5 + 1.7320508)i = 0.1339746 + 2.2320508i

And through the matrix, as Lesson 2.5 would do it: [[0.5, −0.8660254], [0.8660254, 0.5]] applied to (2, 1) gives (2·0.5 − 1·0.8660254, 2·0.8660254 + 1·0.5) = (0.1339746, 2.2320508). The same two numbers, because §5 said they would be.

And now the free theorem, which is the first sign that this notation is going to pay for itself. Since moduli multiply and arguments add, e^{iα}·e^{iβ} = e^{i(α+β)}. Write both sides out in cos and sin:

\[ (\cos\alpha + i\sin\alpha)(\cos\beta + i\sin\beta) = \cos(\alpha+\beta) + i\sin(\alpha+\beta) \] (cos a + i sin a)(cos b + i sin b) = cos(a+b) + i sin(a+b)

Expand the left with §4's rule and match real parts against real and imaginary against imaginary:

\[ \begin{aligned} \cos(\alpha+\beta) &= \cos\alpha\cos\beta - \sin\alpha\sin\beta \\ \sin(\alpha+\beta) &= \sin\alpha\cos\beta + \cos\alpha\sin\beta \end{aligned} \] cos(a+b) = cos a cos b - sin a sin b; sin(a+b) = sin a cos b + cos a sin b

Those are the angle-addition formulas, the ones that are traditionally memorised. They are not a separate fact to learn. They are the complex product, written out in components — the minus sign in the first is the same i² as ever, and the reason the second has a plus is that ad + bc has a plus. If you have ever mixed up which one gets the minus, you now have a way to derive it in eight seconds that cannot go wrong.

What we have, after six sections

A representation of plane rotations in two floats that composes by multiplication — four multiplies, two adds, no trigonometry — whose inverse is a sign flip, and which is provably the same object as the rotation matrix the engine already had, minus the redundancy.

Three of 7.2 §10's four requirements, met. The fourth was interpolation, and §10 gets it. But first there is a fact hiding in the reflections that is worth more than all of them, because it is the one that makes Lesson 7.4 comprehensible instead of magical.
