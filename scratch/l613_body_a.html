Module 6 — Advanced Rendering · Lesson 6.13

Bloom and the Post-Processing Stack

Lesson 6.12 stopped the engine destroying bright values in the fragment shader. It did not make them visible. Measure where the clipping point actually went and the number is uncomfortable: under ACES — the best operator this engine has — the smallest linear value that still resolves to code 255 is 6.3774, so the polished metal from last lesson still spends 13.10 stops on a single code. No curve fixes that, because the constraint is not the curve: an 8-bit sRGB display spans code 1 to code 255, which is 11.69 stops, total. What fixes it is spending a different currency. A real lens scatters light, so a point brighter than white lands on the sensor as a spike with very wide skirts — and the skirts are below the lid even when the source is far above it. The display cannot put 10,000 in a pixel's intensity, so it puts it in how many pixels are lit. This lesson builds that, finds the pyramid is not only cheaper than the Gaussian it approximates but a better shape than it, and then answers the two questions gpu_post.hpp deferred in its own shipped words: how several passes compose, and who owns what between them.

1The problem: the lid moved, it did not leave

Last lesson opened by measuring what the engine was already doing wrong before proposing anything, and that turned out to be the right instinct — the framing it produced ("the engine has been avoiding the question by construction") was better than the one that had been planned. So let us do it again, and this time measure what the engine is doing after the fix.

Here is the question. tonemap::aces maps [0, ∞) into [0, 1] and never exceeds 1. Good. But the display does not have infinitely many codes between 0 and 1; it has 256. So there is some smallest input value that already produces the largest output code, and everything above that value is, once again, indistinguishable. What is that value?

It is not a hard number to get: the curve is monotonic, so bisect. verify_613 §A does exactly that, through apply_tonemap and then to_encoded — the engine's own encode, not a re-implementation — and asks where the result first reaches 255.

reinhard_white (W = 4)3.955613.79

That ACES figure is worth checking by hand rather than trusting, because a bisection that agrees with itself proves nothing. Code 255 needs an encoded value of at least 254.5/255, which is a linear 0.995544 (this is the same threshold that produced 6.12's famous 224, and the same one this module has now got wrong once already). Narkowicz's fit is a ratio of two quadratics, so setting it equal to 0.995544 gives

\[ 0.090828\,x^{2} - 0.557371\,x - 0.139376 = 0 \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad x = 6.3773 \]
0.090828x² − 0.557371x − 0.139376 = 0 → x = 6.3773

The harness bisects to 6.3774. Four figures, from two methods that share no code. That is what makes it a measurement.

The thing the table says that the numbers alone do not

Look at reinhard. It has by far the highest lid — 223.5, thirty-five times ACES's — and it is the operator everyone agrees looks worst. Those two facts are the same fact. Reinhard's curve approaches 1 asymptotically and never arrives, so it spends its precious codes creeping toward a white it does not reach, and the image comes out grey and flat. ACES clips earlier on purpose: it gives up the top four stops in exchange for spending its codes where an image has content.

Tonemapping is not the removal of clipping. It is the choice of where to clip, and how gracefully to approach it.

And once that is clear, so is the ceiling. The display has 11.69 stops — code 1 is linear 0.00030353, code 255 is 1.0 — and that is the entire budget, fixed by the hardware. The scene has more range than that. You may spend the budget differently; you may not enlarge it. Every curve in the table is a different way of allocating the same 256 numbers, and none of them can represent 6.38 and 55,917 as different pixels.

So the honest statement of the problem is not "the highlight is too bright". It is: this image contains information that no assignment of pixel intensities can carry. If we want to show it, we have to encode it in something other than the intensity of the pixel it came from.

2Building intuition: what a lens does that a clamp does not

Photograph a streetlight at night and it does not come out as a tidy white disc on black. It has a halo. Often it has spikes. Squint at a bright light with your own eyes and the same thing happens — the light spreads into a glow that is plainly not part of the lamp.

None of that is the camera failing. It is the camera working, in the only way an optical system can. Light entering a lens does not all arrive where geometry says it should: a fraction scatters off imperfections in the glass, off the edges of the aperture blades, between the elements, and inside the sensor stack itself. Your eye does the same, in the cornea and the vitreous humour — the effect has a name, veiling glare, and it has been measured carefully enough to have a standard model.

The right way to think about this is as a point spread function: the image of a single point of light. In a perfect system the PSF is a point. In a real one it is a sharp spike sitting on very broad, very dim skirts — and the skirts reach a long way.

Why that solves our problem

Here is the step worth pausing on, because everything else in the lesson follows from it.

The scattered light carries energy proportional to the source's luminance. But it is spread over a large area, so the amount landing on any one pixel is small. A source at 55,917 produces skirts that, a few dozen pixels away, are down at 0.3 — comfortably inside the display's range, and comfortably distinguishable from the skirts of a source at 500.

The information that could not survive in the intensity of one pixel survives in the extent of many.

And that is not a comforting metaphor. It is a claim with an exponent in it, which we will state precisely in §3.3 and test in §4: if the skirts fall off as r⁻², then the glow's area is linear in the source's luminance. The display is performing a change of variable — from an intensity it cannot represent into an area it can — and we can check whether the conversion is any good.

What shape are the skirts?

The obvious thing to reach for is a Gaussian blur. It is the blur everyone knows, it is separable, and it looks like the right shape.

It is the wrong shape, and the reason is its greatest virtue everywhere else. A Gaussian falls off as exp(−r²), which is extremely fast. At four standard deviations it is down by a factor of ten million. It essentially has no tails.

Measured glare does. Vos and van den Berg's model of the human glare PSF falls off roughly as 1/θ² over an enormous angular range — a power law, not an exponential — and lens flare behaves similarly. A power law is a fundamentally different kind of function from a Gaussian: it has no characteristic width, and it keeps contributing long after a Gaussian has given up.

So we want a wide, power-law-ish kernel. That sounds expensive, and the standard story says we approximate it with a cheap pyramid of small blurs. The standard story has it backwards, and §3.3 shows why: the pyramid is what a sum of Gaussians whose widths double looks like, and a geometric sum of Gaussians has an approximately power-law envelope. We are not approximating the thing we wanted with something cheaper. We are getting something closer to what we wanted, and it happens to be cheaper.

A note on honesty before we build. Real engines do not apply the PSF to the whole image, which is what physics would require. They threshold first, keep only the bright parts, and blur those — because blurring everything hazes the entire picture and costs bandwidth for light that will never be visible. That threshold is not physical, and §3.6 says exactly what it costs. This is the ninety-percent picture; the missing ten percent is thresholdless convolution bloom, which UE4 later added and which is genuinely more correct and genuinely more expensive.
