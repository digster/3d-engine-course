4Two Conversions, at the Edges

Lesson 1.6 established the rule and then, being Module 1, applied it the only way it could: per operation. Blending two colours? Decode both, blend, encode. Fading? Decode, scale, encode. It said so at the time and called it what it was — slow, and lossy at 8 bits, because every intermediate result gets rounded back into a byte.

A pipeline does it differently, and the difference is not "more careful". It is structural: convert at the edges, and make everything in between a region where ordinary arithmetic is valid.

@@FIG_PIPELINE@@

Three reasons this is better, and only one of them is speed:

  • It cannot be forgotten. Per-operation conversion is a rule every author of every new operation has to remember. Edge conversion is a property of the pipeline's shape — a new operation in the middle is automatically working on light, because there is nothing else there.
  • It does not round. Converting per operation means quantising to 8 bits after every step; a chain of five operations quantises five times. Converting once means one quantisation, at the end.
  • It is faster, but say this last and quietly, because it is the least important of the three and the easiest to over-claim.

So the question this lesson actually asks is narrow and answerable: where are this engine's edges, and does every one of them convert?

6The Bug, Measured

Reasoning is how you find a bug like this; measurement is how you are allowed to claim it. verify_61 §E renders one quad whose emitted light is exactly a value we choose, into two different target formats, and reads back what was stored.

Getting the light to be exactly a chosen number takes a small piece of experiment design worth naming: the key light is set to black, the albedo to white, and the specular to none, so the whole shading equation collapses to lit = base × (0 + ambient) = ambient. The question under test is the transfer function, and any shading arithmetic left in the path would be a second variable in a one-variable experiment.

6.1 How 4.8's comparison missed it

This deserves its own answer, because Lesson 4.8 spent a whole section comparing the two renderers pixel by pixel and reported 87% byte-identical. How does a comparison sit on top of a 60-code error and report agreement?

The harness rendered into an _SRGB target. So inside the test the encode happened — correctly, in hardware — and the two renderers genuinely did agree. The measurement was sound. It simply measured a configuration the shipped program did not use, because the shipped program renders to the swapchain and nobody had compared the two formats.

The lesson inside the lesson

A test that constructs its own environment is testing the environment it constructed. This one differed from production in a single enum, and that enum was the entire subject. When a harness sets up a resource the program also sets up, the two ways of setting it up are a thing to check — or better, the harness should ask the engine for it rather than writing its own literal.

Not a criticism of 4.8, which could not have known. It is the reason gpu_report now carries the answer as a field: a value the program computes and a harness can read is one that cannot silently differ.

7The Fix

7.1 Asking SDL for a linear swapchain

The right fix does not touch the shading at all. The shader is correct: it computes light and returns light. What is missing is the output edge, and SDL will provide it — the hardware has a transfer function in its write path, and using it costs nothing.

Four things about those eleven lines are worth saying out loud, because each is a habit rather than a detail.

Asked, never assumed. SDL guarantees only SDR; SDR_LINEAR may be refused. So we query support, attempt the change, and then read the format back rather than believing our own request took effect. On this machine the log now reads:

The answer becomes a field, not a comment. gpu_report::output_encodes_in_hardware is what a renderer reads to decide what to do. A comment saying "the swapchain is sRGB" would have been true on this machine and false on the next one; a field is true on both.

A predicate that two files need is not a private detail. is_srgb_format lived in an anonymous namespace in gpu_present.cpp while exactly one caller asked the question. There are two now — the present target, deciding whether its mirror texture must cancel the swapchain's decode, and the device, answering the field above — so it moved into the public header. Nothing about it changed except who can see it.

And a two-parameter setter must pass through the parameter it is not changing. set_present_mode() called SDL_SetGPUSwapchainParameters with a literal SDR, because when it was written that was the only composition there had ever been. 7.2 The fallback, and why it is second best

If SDR_LINEAR is refused, the swapchain's values are codes and somebody must encode. The only remaining somebody is the fragment shader.

Note that it is the exact piecewise curve, with the same four constants as engine::linear_to_srgb — not pow(x, 1/2.2). Two implementations of "the sRGB curve" that disagree produce a bug visible only in a diff, and §6's table is what proves these two do not.

The flag lives in the per-frame light uniform, and it cost nothing to add:

That slot was already there. It was pad2, and it existed because HLSL packs a float3 and a float into one 16-byte register — Lesson 4.6's packing rules. The padding was never wasted space; it was an unused field. The struct is still 64 bytes, every offset is unchanged, and 4.6's static_asserts did not move. A flag that costs zero bytes and zero repacking is one you can afford to read on every fragment.

@@FIG_TARGETS@@
The two right answers are not equally right

Hardware blending happens after the fragment shader. If the shader returns an encoded value, the blend unit is adding and interpolating codes — which is the exact mistake §2 is about, moved one stage later. It is correct for opaque geometry and wrong the moment anything is transparent.

The _SRGB target has no such problem: the blend happens in linear and the encode happens on the write, after it. So prefer the swapchain, keep the fallback, and know which one you are on — which is what the field is for.

The software path needed no change at all, and that is worth a sentence rather than silence. Its output edge — to_encoded in raster.cpp — has been correct since Lesson 1.6. The framebuffer's mirror texture does change format, because gpu_present.cpp matches it to the swapchain so that a decode on read cancels an encode on write; that logic was written in Lesson 4.2 and anticipated exactly this, so the fix cost it nothing. One small thing did improve for free: when the framebuffer is scaled up with SDL_GPU_FILTER_LINEAR, the interpolation now happens in linear light rather than between codes.

8What This Lesson Does Not Do

Lesson 1.6's note listed five things a real pipeline needs. This lesson delivered two — decode once in, encode once out — and deliberately left three. Naming them is part of the deliverable, because a reader who thinks colour is now "done" will be surprised twice.

8.1 Headroom above 1.0, and a float framebuffer

The output of the shading equation is clamped to [0, 1] because that is all an 8-bit target can hold. But real light does not stop at 1: a lamp seen directly, a specular highlight on chrome, the sun through a window are all far above it. Clamping them throws that information away before anything can use it, which is why the picture cannot yet have a bloom that means anything.

Fixing that means a floating-point render target and a whole extra stage, and the stage has a name — tonemapping — and its own lesson later in this module. Adding half of it here would mean building a float target with nothing to do with the extra range.

8.2 The software renderer's per-operation conversions

§5's three ⚠ rows are still per-operation. They are correct, and making them a pipeline means giving the software renderer a linear float framebuffer — the same change as §8.1, on the other path. It buys accuracy that nothing currently measures, and it is a large change to raster.cpp. The debt is real and it is smaller than it was; it is not urgent.

8.3 Colour spaces, as opposed to transfer functions

Everything above is about the transfer function — the curve between light and code. sRGB also specifies primaries: which physical red, green and blue the three channels mean. Two images can share a transfer function and still disagree about what "pure red" is, which is what a wide-gamut display exposes.

This course stays in sRGB primaries throughout and says so here rather than leaving you to discover the distinction from a bug report. If you go on to HDR displays or film work, the chapter you want next is about primaries and chromatic adaptation, and it is a genuinely separate subject from this one.
