9The GPU Port, and a Layout That Widened

Both renderers get this, in one commit, for the reason Lesson 6.4 gave when it changed the BRDF: a material field that one renderer honours and the other ignores is exactly the pair of opinions that can disagree. A material with a normal_map handle would be silently flat on the GPU, and nothing anywhere would say so.

The fragment shader is line-for-line the software one — same Gram-Schmidt, same decode, same basis change — so the interesting part of the port is everything around it.

9.1 — The flag that cost nothing

Lesson 6.4 left a `pad0` in material_uniforms, added purely to fill the second 16-byte register. This lesson spends it:

No binding code moved and no shader's register allocation changed, and the static_assert(sizeof(material_uniforms) == 32) is what says so rather than a comment. It is derived from material::normal_mapped() at the push, like textured and for the same reason (6.5 §5): a flag beside a handle is two opinions, and a flag computed from the handle is one.

9.2 — Two fallback texels, two identity elements

A declared sampler slot with nothing bound draws nothing at all — no pixels, no error message — which Lesson 4.7 hit once and solved with a 1×1 white texture. The normal map needs the same treatment with a different constant:

The two 1x1 fallback textures and what makes each the identity for its operation
SlotFallbacksRGB?Why it is the identity
t0 albedo(255, 255, 255)yes white is the identity for a multiply
t1 normal(128, 128, 255)no the lavender decodes to (0,0,1), and T·0 + B·0 + N·1 is N

That false in the second row is this lesson's entire subject on the GPU side. Pass true and the hardware asks for an _SRGB format, 128 decodes to 0.2159 rather than 0.5020, and every unmapped surface in the scene tilts by 38.8°.

9.3 — A shared vertex layout is an interface

gpu_vertex_pnu gained four floats, 32 bytes to 48. The static_assert Lesson 4.5 wrote caught it before a pixel was drawn:

That is the whole value of asserting a number you also wrote in a diagram: the alternative is a lesson page that says 32 bytes forever while the code says 48.

Then the interesting failure. gpu_mesh::describe now wants to emit a fourth attribute at location 3 — and vertex attribute locations are numbered across the whole pipeline, not per buffer. Lesson 4.6's instancing pipeline already puts its per-instance placement at location 3.

Caught by 4.5's check_layout, in verify_46

The harness compares the declared attributes against the shader's reflected inputs and reports extra and duplicate. It failed the moment describe widened — before any rendering, with a name.

Without it the symptom would have been placement silently fetching a tangent's bytes: seven instances stacked at nonsense positions, in a demo three modules old, with nothing pointing at the lesson that broke it.

Two fixes were available and the choice is instructive. Renumber every downstream consumer to 4, 5, 6 — which teaches that a shared layout is an interface, and is real churn. Or make the attribute optional:

The second is better engineering, and not because it is smaller. A vertex layout is per-pipeline state. A shader that never reads a tangent should not have one declared: it is a fetch paid for nothing, and the location it occupies is a resource other buffers want. The vertex buffer still carries the tangent either way — the pitch is sizeof(gpu_vertex_pnu) regardless — so a pipeline that opts out pays the memory and not the bandwidth, which is the honest description of the trade.

12Common Pitfalls & Debugging

Symptom, cause and fix for the failures this lesson's code can produce
SymptomCauseFix
Everything is lit as though the light moved, and reducing the map's strength helps but never fixes it. The normal map was read as sRGB. 128 decodes to 0.216 instead of 0.502. texel_space::linear, and create_sampled(…, false) on the GPU. The fingerprint is a uniform 38.8° tilt.
Bumps read as dents. The green channel's sense is inverted — a map baked for the other convention. Negate y after the decode. glTF fixes the convention (green is +v, which is down the image), so this is an asset problem, not a code one — but you will meet it.
One half of a symmetric model is lit as the mirror image of the other. The handedness was dropped — a vec3 tangent, or a hard-coded cross(N, T). Carry w and multiply the bitangent by it. verify_67 §C is the test.
The normal map looks rotated within the surface, but only on stretched objects. The tangent was transformed by the normal matrix. The model matrix. Both answers stay in the plane, which is why this is a skew rather than an obvious break — and why it looks fine on everything uniformly scaled.
Black pixels, or NaNs spreading through the shading. normalize of a zero tangent — geometry with no uvs, or a vertex all of whose faces had zero uv area. Guard the length before normalising, and fall back to the geometric normal. Both renderers do; the GPU one costs a dot product per fragment.
The render is very dark and reads as a lighting bug. The normal map is far too steep — an amplitude used where a slope was meant. Check what your strength parameter actually scales. A·cos(ku)cos(kv) has peak gradient A·k, so a fixed amplitude gets steeper as the cell count rises.
A cube (or any box-projected asset) comes out banded rather than bumpy. Faces with zero uv area have no tangent frame. Nothing to fix in the renderer — the asset's chart is degenerate. Check mesh_data::tangents for zero entries.

A frame appears to work but a test asserting an exact round trip fails at ~5.5e−03. Not a bug. 0.5 is not an 8-bit code, so a flat map tilts by 0.318°. Predict the floor from the encoding and assert equality with it, rather than widening the tolerance until it passes.

13Exercises

13.1 — Break each step on purpose guided

Five one-line edits, each producing a distinct and recognisable artefact. Make each, look at torus.obj, then run verify_67 and note which section catches it: read the map as srgb; skip the *2-1 decode; skip the fragment's Gram-Schmidt; force handed = 1; use normal_matrix for the tangent.

Hint. Two of the five leave verify_67 entirely green on the torus, and that is the point of the exercise rather than a flaw in the harness — the torus has no mirrored chart and is uniformly scaled. Work out which two, then write the asset that would catch them. That is how a test suite actually grows.

Solution sketch. the two that stay green are forcing handed = 1 and using normal_matrix for the tangent. The torus's UV chart is never mirrored, so every vertex already has handed = +1 and forcing it changes nothing; and its scale is uniform, so normal_matrix = (sR)−T = R/s is parallel to the model matrix and the normalisation erases the difference — the same theorem as Lesson 4.8 §3.5, met a second time. The assets that catch them: a quad whose right half mirrors the left half's UVs (u′ = 1 − u), where forcing the handedness turns every bump on the mirrored half into a dent; and the torus scaled (1.7, 0.5, 1.0), where the wrongly transformed tangent is re-orthogonalised against the correct normal and the whole bump pattern visibly rotates about it. The other three break on any asset: sRGB decoding moves 128 to 0.216 and tilts every normal, skipping *2−1 leans them all toward +x+y, and skipping Gram-Schmidt lets the frame shear wherever interpolation has pulled the tangent off perpendicular.

13.2 — Remove the duplicated frame guided

The TBN construction now exists three times: raster.cpp, scene.frag.hlsl, and verify_67's own perturb. The C++ two can be one. Lift the frame into a small function in microfacet.hpp or a new header, and have both the fragment and the harness call it.

Hint. Note what the duplication currently costs: if the fragment changes and the harness does not, verify_67 keeps passing while the renderer is wrong. That is the real argument, not tidiness. The HLSL copy cannot be shared — and thinking about why not is worth more than the refactor: what would it take, and what does Module 9's shader work need to exist first?

Solution sketch. the C++ function takes the interpolated normal, the four-component tangent and the decoded sample and returns the perturbed normal; raster.cpp and verify_67 both call it, so the harness now tests the renderer's arithmetic rather than a copy that agrees with it by construction. The HLSL cannot join, because it is a different language compiled by a different compiler — a common-subset header with macros for float3/vec3 and mul is possible and brittle. The durable answer is to test the HLSL itself: run the shader's function on the GPU over the same inputs and compare, the way Lesson 4.6's uniform probe asked the shader what it received. That needs a way to run shader code as a test — a probe pass today, a compute dispatch later — and a build that can rebuild shaders on demand, which is Module 9's shader work.

13.3 — Honour normalTexture.scale engine challenge

glTF's normal texture carries a scale that multiplies the sampled x and y before normalisation — so 2.0 doubles the apparent steepness and 0 flattens the surface. gltf_material_desc::normal_scale reads it and nothing applies it. Make it work.

Hint. It needs a per-material float in the fragment, and material_uniforms is exactly full at 32 bytes — so the interesting part is the decision, not the arithmetic. Growing to 48 bytes costs a third register on every draw; packing it into an existing field costs precision. Measure the push cost with verify_46's uniform benchmark before choosing, and write down which you picked and why.

Solution sketch. there is a third option, and it dissolves the decision: normal_mapped is a derived 0/1 flag, and a scale of 0 already means "shade with the geometric normal" — the sample's x and y become 0, and the frame returns N exactly. So replace the flag with the scale itself (0 for an unmapped material, the file's value otherwise) and multiply the sample's x and y by it. The fragment's lerp(geometric, mapped, normal_mapped) then disappears rather than changing — a scale of 0 already yields the geometric normal — and the block stays at 32 bytes with no precision lost. The measurement still belongs in the write-up: Lesson 4.6 found a push costs about 14 ns plus a copy at ~65 GB/s, so growing to 48 bytes would cost a quarter of a nanosecond per draw — which says the size was never the real argument; redundancy was.

13.4 — Two-channel normal maps open

§E notices that z is almost always near 1 and gets a full byte for the privilege, while x and y use only half their range either side of 128. Store two channels and reconstruct the third as z = sqrt(1 - x² - y²).

Hint. This is what BC5 compression assumes, which is why it is the standard format for normal maps. Two things to measure rather than assume: how much precision the two surviving channels gain if you rescale them to the full range, and what happens at the one place the reconstruction is ill-conditioned — x² + y² slightly above 1 after filtering, which is a negative square root in a shader.

Solution sketch. store x and y in a two-channel target (R8G8_UNORM, or BC5 when compressed) and reconstruct z = sqrt(saturate(1 − x² − y²)). If a map's x and y never exceed ±m, rescaling them by 1/m gains log₂(1/m) bits each — one bit, and half the quantisation step, for this map's ±0.5 — at the price of storing m with the texture. The saturate is not optional: rounding and bilinear filtering both produce x² + y² slightly above 1 near steep texels, and sqrt of a negative is NaN, which then spreads through every sum it touches. And the reconstruction is ill-conditioned exactly there: ∂z/∂x = −x/z, so a one-code error in x becomes an error in z that grows without bound as z → 0 — harmless in practice because real normal maps rarely lean past 60°, and worth measuring on the egg-carton's steepest texels anyway.

13.5 — Compress the tangent open

§9.4 prices the vertex growth at 50%. A unit vector plus a sign does not need sixteen bytes: octahedral encoding maps a direction to two numbers with excellent uniformity, so a tangent can be two 16-bit values with the handedness in a spare bit — four bytes.

Hint. Implement it on the CPU first and measure the worst angular error over a few million random directions before touching the vertex format — at 8 bits per component and at 16 — and predict the answer before you run it. Then note what the same trick would do to the normal, which has the same problem and twice the history.

Solution sketch. measured over 400,000 random directions with rounding quantisation (scratch/oct_67.py): 8 + 8 bits gives a worst case of 0.95° and a mean of 0.34°; 16 + 16 gives 0.0037° and 0.0013°; and 16 + 15, with a bit taken for the handedness, 0.0056°. So two 16-bit values are about twenty times better than a tenth of a degree — and 8 bits per component, not 16, is where the error becomes visible. Read the pair as SHORT2 (integers) and unpack in the shader, because a _NORM format gives you no bit to reach. The vertex goes from 48 bytes to 36; doing the same to the normal takes it to 28, below the 32 it was before this lesson — which is why production vertex formats carry octahedral normals and tangents, often as a single packed quaternion.

14Recap & Next

The engine can now describe a surface finer than its triangles. Concretely, it gained:

  • A tangent frame, derived from the uv chart rather than copied — with_tangents, with the handedness recorded, the degenerate chart handled as a case rather than a guard, and the whole thing checkable against a triangle solved on paper.
  • A texture that knows what it holds. texel_space closes a gap that had been open since Lesson 4.7 gave the GPU an srgb flag the software renderer never had a counterpart for — and it is part of the asset's identity, so the same PNG read two ways is two textures over one decode.
  • Per-pixel normals in both renderers, from the same four lines, with glTF's normalTexture finally loaded.
  • An analytic test image, whose closed form lets verify_67 check the sampled normal against the right answer rather than against a picture.
  • A layout that widened safely, because Lesson 4.5's check_layout turned an attribute-location collision into a named failure before anything rendered.
The through-line, if you take one thing from this lesson

Three of the hardest bugs available here — the colour space, the handedness, the transform matrix — share a shape: they produce a picture that is plausible. A uniform 38.8° tilt reads as an over-strong map. A dropped handedness reads as a modelling error. The wrong matrix reads as an asset authored at the wrong angle. None of them looks like the thing it is.

Which is why every one of them is a type or an assertion in this lesson rather than a comment: a vec4 that cannot lose its sign, an enum on the texture that no caller can forget, and a round trip whose error floor is predicted from the encoding.

Two things deferred, both named with reasons. Metallic-roughness textures are now unblocked — they are linear data and texel_space is what they were waiting for — but reading them changes the shading equation's inputs rather than its normal, and that belongs with the material work rather than here. And normalTexture.scale is read and not applied, because material_uniforms is exactly full; that is Exercise 3, and the exercise is the decision rather than the arithmetic.

Next: 6.8 — Shadow Mapping: Bias, Acne, and PCF. Every surface in this engine is lit as though nothing were between it and the light, which is why the torus's inner ring is as bright as its outer one. Shadow mapping fixes that by rendering the scene from the light and asking, per pixel, whether what the light can see is what the camera can see — and the whole lesson is that the comparison is between two floating-point depths that were computed differently, which produces the most famous artefact in real-time graphics. This lesson leaves you well placed for it: a normal map is the first time the engine has had a shading normal that disagrees with the geometry, and shadow bias is the first time that disagreement will matter.
