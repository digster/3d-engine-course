4. Euler's rotation theorem

The claim, stated properly: every rotation of three-dimensional space about a fixed origin is a rotation about some axis through that origin. Euler published it in 1775, and the modern proof is short enough to do here in full — which matters, because the short proof is also the one that tells you why it is a fact about odd dimensions.

4.1 "There is an axis" and "something does not move" are the same sentence

First, translate the claim into something we can compute with. "There is an axis" means there is a direction n that the rotation leaves alone:

\[ R\,\mathbf{n} = \mathbf{n}, \qquad \mathbf{n} \neq \mathbf{0} \] R n = n, for some non-zero n

In the language of Lesson 2.5 — a matrix is where the basis vectors land — this says there is some direction that lands exactly where it started. Rearranging, R n − n = 0, that is (R − I) n = 0: the matrix R − I sends a non-zero vector to zero. A matrix that flattens a non-zero vector to nothing has collapsed space, and the determinant is exactly the number that reports how much space survived (Lesson 2.6). So the whole theorem reduces to one arithmetic claim:

\[ \det(R - I) = 0 \] det(R - I) = 0

That is now a statement we can check, and one the engine already has every function for. Section A.1 of the harness checks it on twenty thousand random rotations.

4.2 The proof, in four lines

We need det(R − I) = 0 for every rotation. A rotation is an orthogonal matrix (RᵀR = I, so Rᵀ = R⁻¹) with det R = +1 — those two conditions are the definition, and the second is what separates a rotation from a reflection. Three determinant facts do the rest, all of which Lesson 2.6 established: det(AB) = det(A)·det(B), det(Aᵀ) = det(A), and det(−A) = (−1)ⁿ·det(A) for an n×n matrix.

\[ \begin{aligned} \det(R - I) &= \det(R^{\mathsf T})\,\det(R - I) && \text{since } \det(R^{\mathsf T}) = \det(R) = 1 \\ &= \det\!\big(R^{\mathsf T}(R - I)\big) && \text{product rule} \\ &= \det(I - R^{\mathsf T}) && \text{since } R^{\mathsf T}R = I \\ &= \det\!\big((I - R)^{\mathsf T}\big) \;=\; \det(I - R) \\ &= (-1)^3 \det(R - I) \;=\; -\det(R - I) \end{aligned} \] det(R - I) = det(R^T) det(R - I) = det(R^T R - R^T) = det(I - R^T) = det(I - R) = (-1)^3 det(R - I) = -det(R - I)

The first line multiplies by det(Rᵀ), which is 1, so nothing has changed. The second folds the product into one determinant. The third uses RᵀR = I. The fourth notices that I − Rᵀ is the transpose of I − R, and transposing does not change a determinant. The fifth pulls a minus sign out of a 3×3 matrix, which costs (−1)³ = −1.

So x = −x, where x = det(R − I). The only number equal to its own negative is zero. Therefore det(R − I) = 0, therefore R − I collapses some non-zero direction to nothing, therefore some direction satisfies R n = n, therefore there is an axis. □

Two loose ends, named rather than glossed

The argument gives a fixed direction; it does not by itself say the rotation is a turn about that direction by some angle. That last step is short: build a basis whose first vector is n, write R in it, and orthogonality plus R n = n force the other two columns to be a 2×2 rotation in the remaining plane — which is a turn by an angle. We use the consequence of that in §8.1 (the trace is 1 + 2cos θ) and do not re-derive it here; Lesson 7.1's angle_between_rotations already spells it out in full.

And the determinant proof shows a fixed direction exists without telling you which. That is normal for an existence proof and it is why §8 is a separate section: finding the axis is a different job from knowing there is one, and it is the harder of the two.

4.3 Why three, and what happens in four

Exactly one line of that proof mentions the dimension: (−1)³ = −1. In an even dimension it would be (−1)⁴ = +1, the last line would read x = x, and the argument would prove nothing whatsoever. That is not a technicality to skip past. It is the theorem's entire content, and the cleanest way to see it is to watch the theorem fail.

@@FIG_ODD@@

Figure 2 is the eigenvalue picture, which is the same proof from the other side. A rotation preserves lengths, so every eigenvalue has magnitude 1 — they all sit on the unit circle in the complex plane. And a matrix with real entries has eigenvalues that come in conjugate pairs: if λ is one, so is λ̄. A 3×3 matrix has three eigenvalues, and three is odd, so after pairing them up one is always left over — and an unpaired eigenvalue must be its own conjugate, hence real, hence (being on the unit circle) either +1 or −1. The determinant is the product of the eigenvalues, and for a rotation it is +1; the conjugate pair multiplies to |λ|² = 1, so the leftover one must be +1. An eigenvalue of +1 is a fixed direction. That is the axis.

In four dimensions nothing is left over. A 4-D rotation can turn in two completely independent planes at once — the xy plane by one angle and the zw plane by another — and its four eigenvalues form two conjugate pairs with none spare. Nothing is fixed. There is no axis, because there is nothing for an axis to be.

verify_72 §A.4 — a 4-D double rotation, 35° in one plane and 50° in the other. Its eigenvalues are e±35i and e±50i, so the determinant of R − I is the product of (λ − 1), which works out to (2 − 2cos 35°)(2 − 2cos 50°):

4-D double rotation (35°, 50°): det(R - I) = 0.25840, predicted 0.25840

Not zero — and it agrees with the eigenvalue prediction to five decimal places, so the determinant and the eigenvalue story are the same story. In four dimensions the sentence "every rotation is a rotation about an axis" is simply false, and this number is the counterexample. The harness computes it with its own 4×4 determinant, twenty lines of cofactor expansion, because mat3 cannot hold a disproof of a claim about mat3.

Ninety percent of the picture

The honest general statement is that rotation happens in a plane, not about an axis. In n dimensions a rotation decomposes into turns in ⌊n/2⌋ mutually perpendicular planes. Three dimensions is the special case where ⌊3/2⌋ = 1 — one plane — and a plane in 3-D has a unique normal direction, so you can name the plane by its normal and call it "the axis". Both coincidences are needed, and both fail in 4-D. The missing ten percent is the general spectral theorem for orthogonal matrices; Artin's Algebra §5.1 finishes it.

5. Rodrigues' rotation formula, derived

We know a single turn exists. Now we need to perform it: given a unit axis n, an angle θ, and a vector v, where does v go?

5.1 Split the vector, and one half does nothing

The whole derivation is one idea: separate the part of v that the rotation cannot touch from the part it acts on. Decompose v into a component along the axis and a component across it:

\[ \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v}_\parallel + \mathbf{v}_\perp, \qquad \mathbf{v}_\parallel = (\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{v})\,\mathbf{n} \] v = v_parallel + v_perp, with v_parallel = (n . v) n

The parallel part is the projection of v onto the axis — Lesson 1.7's dot product, doing the job it was introduced for. It lies on the axis, and the axis does not move, so v∥ does not move. Half the problem is already solved and we have not done any trigonometry.

What remains is v⊥ = v − v∥, which is perpendicular to the axis and therefore lives in the plane the rotation turns. Inside that plane, this is a two-dimensional rotation of a vector by θ, which Lesson 1.7 already solved — but to use that solution we need a pair of axes for the plane, and we need them to be perpendicular and the same length, or "rotate by θ" is not what the sines and cosines will do.

One of them is v⊥ itself. The other is the one place a cross product is the obvious tool: n × v is perpendicular to both n and v, so it lies in the plane and is perpendicular to v⊥. And its length is |n||v| sin α where α is the angle between n and v — which, with n unit, is exactly |v| sin α = |v⊥|. Same length. So {v⊥, n × v} is a perfectly good pair of axes for the disc, and the 2-D rotation is the one everybody knows:

\[ \mathbf{v}_\perp' = \mathbf{v}_\perp\cos\theta + (\mathbf{n}\times\mathbf{v})\sin\theta \] v_perp' = v_perp cos(theta) + (n x v) sin(theta)
@@FIG_RODRIGUES@@

Figure 3 is that sentence, drawn. The dashed disc is the plane v⊥ turns in, centred on the tip of v∥; the two in-plane arrows are the pair of axes; the blue arc is the sweep. Everything about the formula is visible in it, including the thing that is easiest to forget — that n × v and n × v⊥ are the same vector, because the parallel part of v contributes nothing to a cross product with n. That is why the formula can take the cross product of the axis with the whole of v and never has to compute v⊥ at all.

Add the fixed part back and substitute v⊥ = v − (n·v)n:

\[ \mathbf{v}' \;=\; \mathbf{v}\cos\theta \;+\; (\mathbf{n}\times\mathbf{v})\sin\theta \;+\; \mathbf{n}\,(\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{v})(1-\cos\theta) \] v' = v cos(theta) + (n x v) sin(theta) + n (n . v) (1 - cos(theta))

That is Rodrigues' rotation formula, and the three terms have three readings you can keep rather than memorise. The first is most of the vector just leans over. The second is and it swings sideways, along the one direction perpendicular to both. The third is the correction that puts back the along-axis part the cosine took away — which is why it carries (1 − cos θ) and vanishes at θ = 0, where nothing should be corrected.

A sanity check worth doing once

Set v = n. Then n × n = 0 and n·n = 1, so the formula gives n cos θ + 0 + n(1 − cos θ) = n. The axis does not move. A formula that failed this check would be wrong in the one way that matters most, and it takes ten seconds.

5.2 A worked example you can check by hand

Take the axis through the corner of a cube — the body diagonal (1, 1, 1), normalised — and turn by 120°. A cube has three-fold symmetry about that diagonal, so we should expect something clean, and what we get is the cleanest possible answer: the coordinate axes cycle. x̂ → ŷ → ẑ → x̂.

Push x̂ = (1, 0, 0) through by hand. With n = (0.57735, 0.57735, 0.57735), cos 120° = −0.5 and sin 120° = 0.866025:

Term by term, for v = x̂:

  • v cos θ = (1, 0, 0) × (−0.5) = (−0.5, 0, 0)
  • n × v = (ny·0 − nz·0, nz·1 − nx·0, nx·0 − ny·1) = (0, 0.57735, −0.57735), so (n × v) sin θ = (0, 0.5, −0.5)
  • n·v = 0.57735, and 1 − cos θ = 1.5, so n (n·v)(1 − cos θ) = n × 0.866025 = (0.5, 0.5, 0.5)

Add them:

  (-0.5,  0.0,  0.0)
+ ( 0.0,  0.5, -0.5)

x̂ lands exactly on ŷ. The three 0.5s cancelling in the z column and adding in the y column is the whole of it, and it is worth doing the other two by hand as well — ŷ → ẑ and ẑ → x̂ — because a formula you have pushed real numbers through three times stops being something you look up.

5.3 The matrix form, and why we do not write it

A renderer does not rotate one vector, it rotates thousands, and it wants a matrix so the trigonometry is paid for once. Lesson 2.5 gives the construction directly: a matrix is where the basis vectors land. Push x̂, ŷ and ẑ through Rodrigues, and the three answers are the three columns. The worked example above already computed the first column of the 120°-about-the-diagonal matrix without anybody calling it that.

Every reference states the result in closed form instead, and it is worth writing down once so that you recognise it in a paper:

\[ R \;=\; I\cos\theta \;+\; \sin\theta\,[\mathbf{n}]_\times \;+\; (1-\cos\theta)\,\mathbf{n}\mathbf{n}^{\mathsf T} \] R = I cos(theta) + sin(theta) [n]_x + (1 - cos(theta)) n n^T

where [n]ₓ is the cross-product matrix — the matrix that does what crossing with n does — and n nᵀ is the outer product, the matrix that projects onto n. Read the three terms and it is the same three sentences as before: scale everything by the cosine, add the sideways swing, restore the along-axis part.

\[ [\mathbf{n}]_\times = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -n_z & n_y \\ n_z & 0 & -n_x \\ -n_y & n_x & 0 \end{bmatrix} \] [n]_x = [[0, -nz, ny], [nz, 0, -nx], [-ny, nx, 0]] -- antisymmetric, zero diagonal

We implement the basis-vector version, not this one, and the reasons are worth separating because one of them is a matter of taste and the other is not.

The small reason is that mat3 has no operator+, no scalar multiply and no outer product. It has never needed them, and adding three operators to a core type so that one function can be spelled differently is the wrong trade at this point in the course. Module 8's inertia tensor genuinely needs an outer product — that is what an inertia tensor is — and that is the right moment to add them, at the point of need rather than in advance.

The large reason is that the basis-vector version is checkable by a reader who has the derivation in their head, and nine transcribed products of sines are not. You can look at three calls to rotate_about_axis and see the definition of a matrix. You cannot look at m21 = n.z * n.y * k + n.x * s and see anything at all — which is exactly why the harness transcribes the closed form separately and checks the two against each other across fifty thousand turns. A second, independent route is the only thing that makes a transcription trustworthy.
