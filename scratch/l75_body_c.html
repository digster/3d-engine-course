6. The double cover, twice: a sign and a bound

Everything so far would have worked in Lesson 7.3 with complex substituted for quat. This section would not, and it is the reason 7.4 deliberately left slerp unwritten.

6.1 Two names for one destination

Lesson 7.4 §8 established, and measured bitwise over 20,000 rotations, that q and −q are the same rotation. The sandwich q v q̄ is quadratic in q, so a global sign cannot survive it: |M(q) − M(−q)| = 0.000e+00, not a tolerance.

That is a fact about orientations. Interpolation is not about orientations; it is about points, and q and −q are antipodal points on the unit 3-sphere. So there are two great-circle arcs between any two orientations — one through b and one through −b — whose angles sum to 2π. Exactly one of them is the short way, and nothing in the two endpoints' identity as orientations says which.

6.2 The fix is one comparison

[[nodiscard]] constexpr quat nearest(quat reference, quat q)
{
    return (reference.w * q.w + dot(reference.v, q.v) < 0.0f) ? -q : q;
}

The four-component dot product is the cosine of the angle between the two points, and it is also the scalar part of conj(a)·b — one of this algebra's small coincidences, and the reason every slerp implementation in the world opens with a dot product. Negative means the angle between the points exceeds 90°, which means the rotation between them exceeds 180°, which means the other way round is shorter.

Worked, with numbers

Take a = 1 (the identity) and b a turn of 350° about +y. Then b = cos175° + sin175°·ŷ = (−0.99619, 0, 0.08716, 0). The dot product with the identity is just b.w = −0.99619, which is negative, so nearest returns −b = (0.99619, 0, −0.08716, 0).

Read that back: cos(5°) + sin(5°)·(−ŷ) — a 10° turn about −y. Which is the same place. The blend now covers ten degrees instead of three hundred and fifty, and the only thing that changed was a sign on a number the caller never sees.

6.3 The bug you ship without it

This is not a theoretical tidiness. Here is the shape it takes in practice, and it does not look like a sign error when you meet it.

An exporter writes an animation clip as a list of quaternion keyframes. It has no reason to make their signs consistent — q and −q are the same pose, the file format says nothing, and the maths that produced them (a matrix extraction, say, with a pivot that changed between frames) does not care. So two adjacent keyframes describing poses one degree apart can perfectly well be stored with opposite signs.

@@FIG_SIGN@@

Blend them without the comparison and the character takes the 359° route between two poses that were a degree apart: a whole-body spin lasting exactly one keyframe interval, in the middle of a walk cycle. It reads as "the animation is corrupt", not as "somebody forgot a dot product", and it is the single most common quaternion bug in shipped code.

The harness measures the frequency, because "sometimes" is not a design input: 9,923 of 20,000 uniformly sampled pairs — 49.6% — need the flip. It is not an edge case. It is a coin toss.

nlerp's version of the same failure is worse, and worth seeing separately. The chord between two antipodal representatives passes through the origin, where there is no direction left to normalise. On the keyframe pair above, the raw chord passes within 0.00436 of zero — so the blend does not merely take the long way, it goes through a point where the answer is whatever normalised's zero policy happens to be.

6.4 And now the dividend

Here is the part that is genuinely new, that 7.3 could not have found, and that turns the double cover from a tax into a guarantee.

nearest forces the four-component dot product to be non-negative. That is the same as saying the sphere arc Ω is at most 90°. Substitute into §5.3's closed form:

\[ \sec^2(\Omega/2) \;\le\; \sec^2(45°) \;=\; \frac{1}{\cos^2 45°} \;=\; \frac{1}{1/2} \;=\; 2 \]

Exactly 2. Not approximately, not usually — the worst speed ratio nlerp can ever have, over every pair of orientations that exists, is a factor of two.

@@FIG_CAP@@

Compare what the plane could do. In Lesson 7.3 there was no double cover to exploit: z and −z are genuinely different rotations of the plane, so Ω could reach 180°, sec²(Ω/2) could reach 13,131, and the positional gap could reach 26.34°. None of those are reachable here. The gap caps at 8.1491°, and the harness measures it over 20,000 random pairs: largest sphere arc seen 89.9969° against a bound of 90; largest gap seen 8.1345° against a bound of 8.1491.

The control is the same 20,000 pairs with the comparison deleted, where the arc reaches 176.3020° and no bound applies at all.

Why this is worth stopping on

"nlerp is an approximation" is the usual summary, and an approximation with no error bound is not something you can build on. This one has a bound, it is tight, and it comes from the very feature that made the sign comparison necessary. The cost and the guarantee are the same fact. That is worth more than either half: it means a system can use nlerp everywhere, know its worst case is 8.15° of pose, and decide with a threshold on the arc rather than with a preference.

7. Measuring it, and fixing the instrument first

Every claim above is now a number. Getting those numbers required rewriting the thing that measures them, which is the finding this lesson did not plan for.

7.1 The demo: a dial with two rows of ticks

gimbal gains S. It blends between two orientations 150° apart and puts three craft on the arc: slerp in pale blue, nlerp in amber, and — in magenta — the same pair of poses blended the way round that nearest exists to avoid.

The two poses are at most 8° apart, which at this radius is a third of a unit, so two aircraft would be indistinguishable. The ticks carry the argument. Eleven equally spaced values of t are marked twice on the same circle — outward for slerp, inward for nlerp — and the difference between an evenly spaced row and a bunched one is visible from across the room.

@@FIG_RENDER@@

Two drawing decisions in that figure are load-bearing and both were mistakes first. The rotation axis points at the camera, so the nose's path renders as a circle rather than an ellipse — an ellipse's arc lengths are not proportional to its angles, so the tick spacing would have been compressed at the ends by perspective and not by nlerp, and no reader could tell which. That is Lesson 7.4's dial lesson arriving a second time. And both rows of ticks touch the same circle, one pointing out and one in: drawing the second row on a smaller circle would have said the two paths differ, which is the single most common misconception about nlerp and the thing this mode exists to kill.

The right-hand panel is t = 0.5, and there is only one glyph in it. That is not a rendering failure: 

gimbal: sched t 0.500  arc 150.00 deg
gimbal:   slerp 75.0000, nlerp 75.0000 deg turned
gimbal:   nlerp lag 0.0000 deg
gimbal:   long way 105.0000 deg turned

nlerp and slerp agree exactly at the midpoint, and it is a symmetry rather than a coincidence: the chord's midpoint is equidistant from both endpoints, and so is the arc's, and on a circle there is only one such point on the short side. The harness checks it at seven arcs from 10° to 179° and the worst chord between the two midpoints is 6.664e−08.

Which makes the obvious unit test useless

A test that samples t = 0, t = 0.5 and t = 1 certifies nlerp as slerp, because those are precisely the three values at which they are the same function. The gap at t = 0.5 is 0.0000°; at t = 0.229 it is 4.5140°.

This is Lesson 7.4's finding one level up. That lesson found renormalised_fast exact at |q| = 1 and at |q| = 2 and catastrophic between, and drew the moral choose the sample points. Here the sample points that hide the difference are the three a reasonable person would choose first.

7.2 The path, against a number Lesson 7.1 measured

Total turning along a path, at a fixed step count, against the straight-line separation of the endpoints. Lesson 7.1 built this instrument for matrices and Lesson 7.2 used it to prove slerp's geodesic property. Here it is again, on the same pose pair, in a different representation: 

   geodesic  179.0527 deg
   slerp     179.0524 deg   excess   -0.00%
   nlerp     179.0524 deg   excess   -0.00%
   euler     204.2903 deg   excess  +14.10%

The Euler row is the check, and it is a stronger one than it looks. Lesson 7.2's harness measures the same three quantities on the same pose pair using mat3 throughout and angle_between_rotations as its metric — code that has never heard of a quaternion, written three lessons ago: 

  generic pair       path   geodesic     excess    speed
  Euler lerp      204.29°    179.05°     14.10%   1.556x  <- CONTROL (7.1's number)
  slerp           179.05°    179.05°     -0.00%   1.000x

Every figure agrees to the digit. Two representations, two metrics, two harnesses, no shared code — and that agreement is what makes the other two rows believable, because a path integral that reports −0.00% for a function you wrote yourself is exactly the kind of number that should be distrusted until something independent confirms it. This lesson's first attempt at that measurement did not survive that scepticism, and §7.3 is what it found.

7.3 The instrument that had to be rewritten first

Those numbers are the second set. The first set said this: 

   geodesic  179.0527 deg
   slerp     164.7134 deg   excess   -8.01%
   nlerp     162.4646 deg   excess   -9.26%
   euler     188.0517 deg   excess   +5.03%

A path integral reporting that slerp's own geodesic is 8% shorter than the distance between its endpoints is not a finding about slerp. It is a broken ruler, and it took a while to believe that rather than the numbers.

The culprit is angle_between(quat, quat), which Lesson 7.4 shipped as the formula every reference gives:

\[ \theta \;=\; 2\arccos\big|\,a \cdot b\,\big| \]
@@FIG_METRIC@@

Look at the left panel of figure 7. Near zero the cosine is quadratic in the angle and the sine is linear. So for a small separation the dot product lands within a few float ulps of 1, and acos is being asked to read the angle off a quantity that has already lost almost all of it. The noise floor is computable: the ulp below 1 is eps/2, so

\[ \theta_{\text{floor}} \;\approx\; 2\sqrt{2\,\varepsilon} \;=\; 2\sqrt{2 \times 1.192\times10^{-7}} \;=\; 0.0560° \]

A path integral over 4,096 steps of a 150° arc takes steps of 0.037° — every one of them below that floor. So every step is quantised, and quantised downward, and 4,096 biased errors do not average out. The right panel of figure 7 is the result: 

   endpoints are 150.0000 deg apart
   slerp path by 7.4's acos form: 88.3437 deg  (-41.10%)
   slerp path by the atan2 form:  150.0000 deg  (-0.00%)

−41.10%. The metric was failing slerp for not walking the path it was walking.

The repair is the form the same file already uses eleven lines higher. axis_angle_from_quat reads its angle with atan2(|v|, w) and its doc comment says why, in as many words — and then angle_between, directly below it, uses acos. Writing the difference quaternion's two parts out by hand:

\[ \operatorname{conj}(a)\,b \;=\; \big(\,a\!\cdot\!b,\;\; a_w b_v - b_w a_v - a_v \times b_v \,\big) \qquad \theta \;=\; 2\operatorname{atan2}\big(|v|,\;|a\!\cdot\!b|\,\big) \]

About twelve multiplies where the old form was four. For a metric — called in tests, in editors and in path integrals, and never per vertex — that is the right side of the trade, and the acos form is kept in the file as angle_between_by_cosine so the two can be measured against each other rather than argued about. That is exactly the arrangement math/rotation.hpp has had for matrices since Lesson 7.1.

The lesson underneath, which is not about acos

7.4 wrote angle_between for a separation — how far apart are these two poses — where the answer is large and acos is fine. A path integral asks the same function for a step, where the answer is tiny. The function did not change; the question did, and nothing in the type system or the name marks the difference. When you reuse a numerical routine at a different scale, re-derive its conditioning at that scale. Fourth appearance of this trap in Module 7, and the first inside the engine.

7.4 The textbook form, and a table that lied

Every reference prints slerp like this:

\[ \operatorname{slerp}(a,b,t) \;=\; \frac{\sin\big((1-t)\Omega\big)\,a \;+\; \sin\big(t\Omega\big)\,b}{\sin \Omega} \]

It is the same function — measured agreement 1.192e−07, one float ulp, over a 120° arc. It is also worse, and in a way that matters exactly where animation lives.

It divides by sin Ω. As the arc closes, sin Ω → 0 and so does the numerator, and the cancellation is not arranged — both parts are computed separately and then divided. Worse, Ω itself comes from an acos, so it becomes exactly 0 as soon as the dot product rounds to 1. Then sin 0 = 0 and the whole thing is 0/0.

We can predict where. The ulp below 1 is eps/2, so round-to-nearest takes anything within eps/4 of 1; 1 − cos Ω ≈ Ω²/2, so the cliff is at Ω = √(eps/2) and the rotation arc is twice that:

\[ \theta_{\text{cliff}} \;=\; 2\sqrt{\varepsilon/2} \;=\; 2\sqrt{5.96\times10^{-8}} \;=\; 0.0280° \]
   arc (deg)     textbook       ours err
      1.0000     1.1921e-07     5.7032e-10
      0.1000     1.1921e-07     7.1290e-11
      0.0100     101 NaN of 101   7.7173e-12
      0.0001     101 NaN of 101   6.5122e-14
      0.0000     101 NaN of 101   0.0000e+00
   textbook form goes NaN below an arc of 0.0279 deg
   predicted: 2*sqrt(eps/2) = 0.0280 deg

0.0279 measured against 0.0280 predicted. And a NaN pose does not stay local — it propagates through every child in the skeleton, and the symptom is an entire character disappearing on one frame.

And the first version of that table said 0.0000e+00 in all four rows

It accumulated the worst error with std::max. Every comparison against a NaN is false, so std::max(x, NaN) returns x: a NaN can never win a maximum. The textbook column therefore printed a clean, perfect, entirely fictional zero for a function that was failing in all 101 samples of all four rows — and the row it printed correctly, at 1°, showed it beating our version, which made the table look like an argument for the textbook form.

A NaN has to be counted, never maximised. Fifth blind instrument in Module 7, and the first one in a harness rather than in engine code.
