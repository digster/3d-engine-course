16. Build & run

Nothing new is fetched and no shaders are compiled, so the usual configure is enough. 

# Linux and macOS
cmake -S . -B build
cmake --build build --target gjk -j
./build/demos/gjk

# Windows (Visual Studio generator)
cmake -S . -B build
cmake --build build --target gjk --config Debug
.\build\demos\Debug\gjk.exe

What you should see. A 960×540 window split down the middle. On the left, two wireframe crates on a one-metre grid, the second drifting slowly through the first and back out; while they are apart, a gold segment joins the two nearest surface points. On the right, the Minkowski difference outlined in grey, a white cross at the origin, the current simplex in gold and a pink line from the origin to v. When the crates overlap the moving one turns red, the gold simplex becomes a tetrahedron around the cross, and the pink line disappears — there is no nearest point to draw when the origin is inside.

Things to do with it, in order:

  • Press [S] a few times. The simplex grows and the pink line shortens, and the panel's |v| readout only ever goes down. Press [R] to start the search over, [A] to let it run to completion again.
  • Press [2]: a capsule against a fourteen-vertex hull. Neither shape has a finite face list, so 8.4's test cannot be applied to either of them, and this one does not notice.
  • Press [3] and step the search. The simplex stays in one plane — §9's finding, live. Press [Z] to tilt one box and watch it lift out.

And the headless form, which is byte-for-byte reproducible because the demo loads nothing: 

./build/demos/gjk --preset 1 --t 1.2 --shot apart.ppm
./build/demos/gjk --preset 0 --t 3.2 --shot overlapping.ppm
./build/demos/gjk --preset 1 --t 1.2 --steps 1 --shot one-step.ppm

The harness is a separate build, and it wants a release library because §I quotes single-digit nanoseconds: 

cmake -S . -B build-rel -DCMAKE_BUILD_TYPE=Release
cmake --build build-rel --target engine -j
sh scratch/build_verify_85.sh

It prints nine lettered sections and finishes with 33 checks, 0 failures. If it refuses to run at all, read what it says: a tree configured without CMAKE_BUILD_TYPE carries no -O flag, and §I.3's whole finding — that the indirect call costs nothing — is a claim about inlining that a debug library inverts.

17. Common pitfalls & debugging

The distance is right but the direction is backwards

Symptom: objects are pushed together instead of apart, which looks like a solver instability rather than a sign error.
Cause: v runs from B's witness to A's, because v = pA − pB. The axis pointing from a toward b — conventions.html §9e, unchanged since 8.4 — is therefore −v̂.
Fix: the negation in gjk_distance's last lines is deliberate. Check it by translating b by +direction · distance and re-testing: the shapes should touch, not separate further.

The loop never terminates on one particular pair

Symptom: a hang, or an iteration counter pinned at the cap, always on the same two objects.
Cause: almost always the gap being tiny compared with the shapes — §11.1's floor — or a simplex solver returning a point that is not on the simplex.
Fix: the monotonicity check (§7.4). Print |v| per iteration before doing anything else; if it goes up, the solver is the problem and no amount of tolerance tuning will help. If it merely stops going down, you are at the floor and the answer you already have is correct.

Two shapes a centimetre apart are reported as touching

Symptom: phantom contacts, worse on large objects.
Cause: the contact margin is tolerance × |w|, so it scales with the shapes. On a 100 m object the default margin is about 2 cm.
Fix: this is working as designed (§11.2). If you need a tighter margin on large shapes, tighten gjk_config::tolerance — and read §10 first, because on a curved shape that costs iterations, and on a polytope it costs nothing because the tolerance was never what stopped the search.

A convex gives garbage after a few frames

Symptom: distances that are usually right and occasionally absurd.
Cause: the view outlived what it viewed — the classic being a std::vector of hull points that reallocated while a hull held a span into it.
Fix: the deleted rvalue overloads catch the temporary case at compile time. They cannot catch a reallocation, so a hull's points belong in storage that outlives every query against them; the demo keeps them in a member vector that is only rebuilt on reset().

The answer is fine near the origin and wrong far from it

Symptom: a level that collides correctly near its centre and produces jittery, imprecise contacts at its edges.
Cause: §12. If you wrote your own convex adapter and returned world support points with a zero origin, you have built the naive arm.
Fix: return the support point relative to a point inside the shape, and put that point in origin. Test it by translating both shapes by a kilometre and checking the answer does not move.

EPA has nothing to expand

Symptom: (a preview of 8.6) a penetration solver that works on tumbling debris and fails on a crate resting on a floor.
Cause: §9 — a shared up axis confines the search to a plane, so terminal.count is 3 rather than 4 on precisely the arrangement that matters.
Fix: build the starting polytope rather than assuming one. It is on 8.6's critical path, not its error path.

18. Exercises

  1. Run the algorithm by hand. Take A = [3, 5] × [1, 2] and B = [8, 11] × [4, 6] — two axis-aligned rectangles in the plane. Write down the four vertices of each, compute the sixteen pairwise differences, and hull them by inspection. Then run GJK on that set with a pencil, starting from the support point in direction delta, and record |v| and dot(v, w)/|v| at each step until they meet.

    Hint: the difference of two axis-aligned rectangles is another axis-aligned rectangle, which makes the hull step trivial and the answer checkable against the two-interval formula. The true distance is √(3² + 2²).

    Solution sketch: with each rectangle's origin at its centre, delta = (9.5, 5) − (4, 1.5) = (5.5, 3.5), and the difference set is A ⊖ B = [3 − 11, 5 − 8] × [1 − 6, 2 − 4] = [−8, −3] × [−5, −2] — a rectangle whose corner nearest the origin is (−3, −2). The first support, in direction +delta, is A's corner (5, 2) minus B's corner (8, 4) = (−3, −2): the answer, on the first call. So v = (−3, −2), |v| = √13 = 3.6056; the next support, in −v = (3, 2), is the same corner, and dot(v, w)/|v| = 13/√13 = 3.6056. The bounds meet after one iteration. The seed did all the work, and that is §6.4's measurement in miniature; try the seed −delta and the first support is (−8, −5), the corner farthest away, and the hand run gets interesting.

  2. Add a cylinder. A cylinder is disc ⊕ segment, so its support function is two terms, like the capsule's — but the disc's term is a projection of d onto the plane perpendicular to the axis, normalised and scaled by the radius. Write support_local(const cylinder&, vec3), add an as_convex overload with its deleted rvalue partner, and check it against a dense sampling of the surface the way §B.2 checks the capsule.

    Hint: the degenerate case is a direction exactly along the axis, where the perpendicular component is zero and there is no preferred point on the rim. Return any rim point — the value is a tie, so only reproducibility is at stake. Then ask yourself why 8.4 could not have added a cylinder at all.

    Solution sketch: with unit axis â, half-height h and radius r, the support is sign(d · â) h â + r p̂, where p̂ is d − (d · â) â normalised; when that perpendicular part is (nearly) zero, return a fixed rim point — r times one chosen perpendicular — so the tie is at least reproducible. Check it the way §B.2 checks the capsule: sample the surface densely and confirm no sample projects further along d than the support does. 8.4 could not have added it because the SAT needs a finite list of candidate axes, and a curved rim against an edge offers infinitely many; GJK never asks for axes, only for the farthest point in a direction.

  3. Measure the seed. §6.4 claims the initial direction is worth 22% of the iteration count. Verify it: add a gjk_config whose initial_direction is −delta rather than +delta, run it over the same population as §F.1, and compare the histograms. Then explain, in one sentence, why the sphere column in §F.2 moves from 1.00 to 2.00 under that change and the box column moves by only about one.

    Hint: for a ball the first support point in the right direction is the answer; for a box it is a vertex that may or may not be the nearest feature. One seed wastes exactly one iteration; the other wastes one and then still has work to do.

    Solution sketch: the sentence: for a ball, the support in +delta already is the closest point, so the wrong seed costs exactly the one iteration it takes to turn round (1.00 → 2.00), while a box's first support is only a vertex that may not be the nearest feature — the wrong seed costs that same turn-round and the search that was left still has to happen, so the box column moves by about one. Compare means and full histograms, not just the mean: the 22% should appear as the whole distribution shifting by roughly one iteration.

  4. Break the simplex solver on purpose. Replace reduce_triangle with the textbook Voronoi-region version reproduced in §C.5 of the harness, run the full verify, and record which sections fail and which do not. Then turn off the monotonicity check in gjk_distance and run it again.

    Hint: expect §C.2 and §E to complain and §A.3 to stay green, because an AABB pair rarely produces a sliver. The pair of runs is the point: the second one shows how much of the damage the invariant was absorbing silently, which is the argument for having invariants you can check.

    Solution sketch: record, per section, pass or fail and the size of any error, in both runs. The first run shows what the invariant protects: with the textbook triangle case, sliver triangles can choose the wrong Voronoi region, and the monotonicity check catches the step at which |v| fails to decrease and stops with the last good answer — a correct distance with a lost certificate, the §11.1 kind of failure. The second run removes that net: the same slivers now run to the iteration cap or return a v that is not the minimum, and the sections that compare distances against the reference turn red. The difference between the two runs is the damage the invariant had been absorbing without a word — which is the case for invariants you can check rather than ones you believe.

  5. Engine challenge: conservative advancement. GJK gives a distance; a time-of-impact test needs to know when that distance reaches zero. Implement the simplest form: given two bodies with constant linear velocities over a step, repeatedly compute the distance d and the closing speed along the reported direction, advance time by d / closing_speed, and iterate until the distance is within the contact margin or the step is exhausted. Test it on a bullet fired at a wall thin enough for 8.4 §9's tunnelling case.

    Hint: the step is conservative because nothing can close faster than the maximum closing speed, so advancing by d / vmax can never step past the impact. Rotation makes vmax harder — you need a bound involving the angular velocity and the shape's bounding radius — so start with translation only and say so in the doc comment. Then notice which of this lesson's guarantees you are relying on — and check which side of the true distance GJK's number errs on before you step by it.

    Solution sketch: the loop: at time t, run GJK at the advanced positions; if the gap is within the margin, report t; otherwise take the closing speed along the reported direction n (a closing speed of zero or less means no impact this step) and advance t by gap / closing speed. The gap you divide must be a lower bound on the true distance, or the step can pass the impact — and GJK's distance is the other kind: §11.1 and §A.5 establish it is the gap between two real surface points, an upper bound. So compute the lower bound yourself from two more support calls, the slab between the supporting planes along n: n · sB(−n) − n · sA(n). Under a pure translation that slab narrows at exactly the closing speed, for any n, so the step is safe even when n is slightly wrong. Lesson 8.13 learned this the hard way: its first draft stepped from the upper bound, and 377 of 6,049 casts finished inside the skin. The thin wall from 8.4 §9 is the test — it must be hit at the right time, not tunnelled.

19. Recap & next

The engine can now measure the distance between any two convex shapes and say where. Not a bound on it — the distance, with two surface points that realise it and two bounds that prove it. Box, sphere, capsule, arbitrary point set, in any of the ten pairings, through one function that names none of them.

The pieces, in the order they were needed:

  • The Minkowski difference turns a question about a pair into a question about a point, through two equivalences that each take two lines to prove.
  • The support function determines a convex set, so one function pointer is a complete shape interface — and the difference set's support function is two calls to its parents', which is what makes the whole thing computable.
  • GJK descends with a simplex, carrying an upper bound that only falls and a lower bound that only rises, so its answer arrives with a proof attached.
  • The simplex solver computes four candidates rather than choosing one, because the shortcut everyone copies fails exactly where the algorithm spends its time — 1,048 failures in 200,000 slivers, worst case 14.5% wrong.
  • A monotone invariant does double duty, terminating the loop and catching a solver that goes backwards; and a proven lower bound outranks a containment test computed on a degenerate tetrahedron.

And five measurements worth carrying forward. GJK finishes on polytopes and only converges on curves, so a capsule costs one pass per decade of tolerance and a box costs none. The tolerance is a relative quantity that implies a contact margin of 2.11 × tol × size across four decades, which means it is not a distance and must not be set as one. The certificate gives out before the answer does, at ε·|w|/tol, and a stalled answer is still an upper bound. Taking support points relative to each shape's own centre makes the error 96,270× smaller a thousand kilometres from the origin. And two boxes on a floor produce a search trapped in a plane, so the tetrahedron everyone assumes GJK leaves behind is absent from 100,000 consecutive queries.

Next: 8.6 asks the question this lesson refused. When the origin is inside the difference set, how far inside, and which way out? That is a distance to the set's boundary rather than to its interior, so the search has to run the other way — outward, expanding a polytope until it reaches the surface. The Expanding Polytope Algorithm starts from the simplex GJK left behind, and §9 is the reason its first job is to build one.

20. Further reading

  • Gilbert, Johnson and Keerthi, “A Fast Procedure for Computing the Distance Between Complex Objects in Three-Dimensional Space” (IEEE J. Robotics and Automation, 1988). The original. Worth reading for its framing rather than its pseudocode — the algorithm is presented as an optimisation over a convex set, which is the view that makes the two bounds obvious.
  • Gino van den Bergen, “A Fast and Robust GJK Implementation for Collision Detection of Convex Objects” (Journal of Graphics Tools, 1999). The paper that turned the algorithm into shippable code. Its termination conditions and its treatment of relative error are the direct ancestors of §6.1 and §11.
  • Gino van den Bergen, Collision Detection in Interactive 3D Environments (2003), chapter 4. The book-length version, including the separating-axis cache and the ray-cast variant.
  • Christer Ericson, Real-Time Collision Detection (2005), §5.1 and §9.5. The closest-point routines, including the ClosestPtPointTriangle this lesson deliberately does not ship — read §7.2 with the book open, because the code is not wrong, it is conditioned differently than a GJK simplex requires.
  • Montanari, Petrinic and Barbieri, “Improving the GJK Algorithm for Faster and More Reliable Distance Queries Between Convex Objects” (ACM TOG, 2017). The signed-volume reformulation of the simplex solver, and a careful treatment of exactly the degeneracies §7 measures.
  • Casey Muratori, “Implementing GJK” (2006). A two-hour whiteboard derivation of the boolean version, and the clearest available explanation of why the search direction constrains which Voronoi regions are reachable — the optimisation §7.1 declines to make.
  • Erin Catto, “Computing Distance” (GDC 2010). Box2D's author on GJK in two dimensions, with the barycentric formulation written out and the witness points derived the same way §6.3 does.
  • Sterbenz, Floating-Point Computation (1974), §4.3. The lemma §12 leans on: if y/2 ≤ x ≤ 2y then x − y is exact in floating point. One paragraph, and it is the reason the relative formulation works at all.
