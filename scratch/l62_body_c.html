7Implementation

Six changes, and every one of them is a renaming of something the code already did. That is unusual for this course and it is the right shape for this lesson: we are not adding a capability, we are giving the existing one a vocabulary it can be checked in.

7.1 The two constants

light.hpp needs 1/π. C++20 has it, and this is the first place in the course that has needed it, so here is the C++ note.

C++ in place — <numbers> and variable templates

M_PI was never standard C++. It is a POSIX extension, MSVC hides it behind _USE_MATH_DEFINES, and that is why every cross-platform codebase eventually grows its own copy of the digits — including this one, which has 3.14159265358979f written out in eleven places. C++20's <numbers> ends that.

The _v<float> suffix marks a variable template: a family of constants, one per type. pi_v<float> is correctly rounded to float, rather than being a double that gets narrowed at each point of use. That distinction is not academic in a shading function — a narrowing conversion in a per-pixel loop is a real instruction as well as a rounding question.

The constants live in light.hpp rather than a maths header because the BRDFs below are the only things in the engine that need them. Retiring the other eleven literals is Exercise 13.1.

7.2 The light gains a unit

intensity becomes irradiance, defaulting to the reference value, and the light gains a function that computes the light's own half of the shading equation.

irradiance_on computes nothing new. Its value is that the cosine now has a home on the light's side of the equation, which is §3's argument turned into a symbol — and it gives the next light type (a point light, in an exercise) an obvious place to put its inverse-square falloff, where a fused albedo * n_dot_l would have had none.

7.3 Two BRDFs, with units

Note what the second one is not called. Dividing the Blinn-Phong lobe by π is not a normalisation, and the header says so at length: it puts the lobe in the same units as the diffuse term so that one irradiance can multiply both, and that is all it does. §6 measured exactly how un-normalised the result remains. Calling it a normalisation would be the kind of comment that is worse than no comment, because it would stop the next reader from asking.

specular_term() is untouched. It still returns the bare coss, and the local variable in shade() that receives it is renamed from spec to lobe — because a lobe is a shape and a BRDF is a shape with units, and the two now have different names in a function that contains both.

7.4 The return statement

Read the three groups against §4.3's equation and they line up one for one. That is the deliverable of this lesson: not a faster or prettier renderer, but a return statement whose shape is an argument.

7.5 The shader, changed identically

scene.frag.hlsl gets the same three groups. HLSL has no <numbers>, so the constant is written out — and written to more digits than a float can hold, deliberately, so the compiler rounds once and lands on the same value the CPU has.

A constant that exists in two languages is a constant that can disagree in its last bit, and the symptom would be a one-code difference in a pixel diff six lessons from now. So verify_62 §F parses the shader source, converts the literal to a float, and compares the bit patterns against engine::k_inv_pi. Both are 0x3EA2F983. It is three lines of harness and it removes a whole category of afternoon.

7.6 The uniform block, which did not change

scene_light_uniforms::key is the lamp's colour times its scalar, computed on the CPU. Because the field was always the product rather than the two factors, giving one factor a physical unit could not reach it: the struct is unchanged, every offset is unchanged, and only its documentation moved.

That is worth a sentence, because it is the test of whether a uniform block was designed or merely filled in. Had it carried colour and intensity separately, this rename would have crossed the CPU/GPU boundary and the shader would have needed to know what a lamp's authoring model is. A boundary that carries results rather than inputs is one the far side cannot be wrong about.

8What Changed on Screen

Nothing. And this section is about why that is the result rather than the anticlimax.

The plan for this lesson, written down at the end of 6.1, predicted the opposite: "the golden is genuinely at risk this time… if the π lands in the BRDF, every shaded pixel moves". That prediction was right about the mechanism and wrong about the outcome, because it assumed the light would keep its old value. Once §5.2 derives that the light's value must be π, the two parameterisations are algebraically the same product — and the interesting question becomes whether they are the same product in floating point, which is a different question with a measurable answer.

verify_62 §E asks it over 342,225 channel samples, sweeping albedo, cosine, specular lobe and highlight colour against the reference render's own light:

Both halves matter. The first says the change was real: re-associating three multiplies moved 45% of the sampled results in their last one or two bits, and claiming otherwise would have been the easy lie. The second says the output stage absorbed all of it — through the exact encoder and the fast one, which the reference render actually uses.

And then the golden, which is the whole reference render rather than a sweep: seven frames, 1,209,616 bytes, hash 905BF27E, byte-identical for the thirteenth lesson.

Why an empty diff is evidence here

The claim of §5.1 is that the π was hiding inside intensity. That claim makes a prediction: take the π out of the light, put it into the BRDF, and set the light to π, and the picture must come back exactly. If the π had been somewhere else — folded into an albedo convention, absorbed by the ambient, or simply absent — this experiment would have produced a different image.

1,209,616 bytes of agreement is the negative control passing. It is only evidence because the float measurement above shows the arithmetic genuinely changed; a change that did nothing at all would prove nothing at all.

8.1 The harness bug the rename found

One call site did not take a mechanical fix. verify_48 §F — Lesson 4.8's pixel-by-pixel comparison of engine::shade() against scene.frag.hlsl — filled the GPU's light uniform like this:

The colour alone. The scalar is missing. That was silently correct for as long as the scalar was 1.0 — which it was, in every scene, because nobody had a reason to change it. The moment it became π the two sides disagreed by a factor of 3.14 and §F said so: worst |CPU − GPU| went from 1.2e−07 to 5.3e−01.

This is Lesson 6.1's finding again, in a new costume: a test that constructs its own environment tests the environment it constructed. There 4.8's harness built an _SRGB target while the program rendered to a UNORM swapchain; here it built a light the shipped renderer does not build — gpu_scene.cpp has always multiplied by the scalar. The instrument was wrong, in a way only detectable by changing the thing the instrument assumed was constant.

With the multiply restored, §F reports 1.788e−07 — one to two ULP, exactly what it reported before this lesson. The CPU and the GPU each moved their π independently and still agree.

9What This Lesson Does Not Do

Four things, named here rather than discovered as gaps later.

It does not normalise the specular. §6 measured the failure precisely and stopped there, because the correct fix is not a constant — it is Fresnel deciding the split between reflected and scattered light, which needs the microfacet machinery of Lessons 6.3 and 6.4. Bolting a normalising factor onto Blinn-Phong now would produce a model that passes the energy test and is still not a description of any surface, which is a worse place to be than honestly un-normalised with a number attached.

It does not make the BRDF reciprocal. A physical BRDF satisfies fr(l,v) = fr(v,l) — swap the light and the eye and the answer is unchanged, which follows from time-reversal symmetry. Blinn's term happens to be reciprocal (the halfway vector does not care which of the two it came from); Phong's is not. Reciprocity matters for bidirectional path tracing and for nothing this course builds, so it is named and set aside.

It does not give the light a real unit. irradiance is in "engine units" where a white surface reads 1.0. Lux would need an exposure and a tonemapper to get back to a display value; that is Lesson 6.12, and §5.2 says what changes when it arrives.

It does not touch transmission. The T in BRDF is for reflectance: light that goes into the surface and comes back out of the same side. Glass, water, leaves and skin also send light out the far side (a BTDF) or scatter under the surface before re-emerging (subsurface scattering). Both are out of scope for this course, and both are why a BRDF-only renderer cannot make a convincing wax candle.

12Common Pitfalls & Debugging

Everything is π times too dark — or π times too bright

Symptom: the scene looks flat and murky, or blown out, by an amount that is suspiciously uniform.

Cause: the π is in the BRDF and not in the light, or in both, or in neither. Factor 3.14 is the single most common magnitude of error in a renderer that has just been given units.

Fix: the white-surface test from §5.1. Render a quad with albedo 1 facing the light and read the pixel. It should be 255. If it is 81, the π is missing from the light (255 × 1/π ≈ 81). If it saturates immediately at a light value that used to be reasonable, the π is applied twice.

The hemisphere integral converges to something that is not 1

Symptom: your own energy-conservation harness reports 0.5, or 2, or 6.28 for a white Lambert BRDF, and refining the grid does not help.

Cause: a missing factor in the solid-angle element, and the value tells you which. 2.0 means the cos θ weight is missing — you integrated 1/π over 2π steradians. 0.5 means the sin θ in dω is missing. 6.28 means the π is missing from the BRDF.

Fix: integrate the constant 1 first and check you get 2π; then integrate cos θ and check you get π. Two lines, and they isolate the element from the integrand. Not converging is the useful signal: quadrature error shrinks with the grid, a wrong constant does not.

A rename that compiles everywhere

Symptom: you renamed the field, the build is green first try, and the picture changed.

Cause: something is reading the light through a defaulted struct, or through a copy made before the rename, so the compiler never had to tell anyone. This is the exact failure the rename exists to prevent, walked back in through a default.

Fix: grep for the type as well as the field — grep -rn "directional_light\|lighting " engine demos — and check what each construction site relies on. In this engine the default is k_reference_irradiance precisely so a defaulted light is still correctly exposed; if you choose 1.0 instead, every defaulted scene silently darkens.

The CPU and the GPU disagree by exactly π

Symptom: a comparison harness that agreed to a float ULP now reports a difference around 0.53, or a ratio of 3.14.

Cause: one side multiplies the light's scalar into the uniform and the other does not — §8.1's bug. It is invisible while the scalar is 1.

Fix: the ratio names it. Divide the two values; if you get π, you are looking for a missing multiply by the light's scalar, not a shading bug. And when you find it, check whether the shipped path has it too; here the renderer was always correct and only the instrument was wrong.

Two spellings of one constant

Symptom: a one-code difference between two renderers, in a diff taken months later, that resists every explanation.

Cause: HLSL has no <numbers>, so the shader carries its own digits, and somebody typed 0.3183f.

Fix: write more digits than a float can hold and let the compiler round once — and then assert it, as verify_62 §F does by parsing the shader source and comparing bit patterns. Three lines of harness against a category of bug that is otherwise found by accident.

13Exercises

  1. 13.1 — Retire the other eleven. grep -rn "3\.14159" engine demos finds eleven hand-written copies of π, in camera code, demo field-of-view constants and an angle-in-degrees helper. Replace them with std::numbers::pi_v<float>, and decide where the constant should live now that a second subsystem needs it.

    Hint

    light.hpp is the wrong home the moment camera.hpp wants it — a camera header should not include the lighting model to get a number. The choice is between a new engine/math/constants.hpp and using <numbers> directly at each site.

    Solution sketch

    Either is defensible; what is not defensible is leaving k_inv_pi in light.hpp and adding a maths header, so that the engine has two homes for the same idea. If you add the header, move k_inv_pi into it and leave k_reference_irradiance behind — that one really is a lighting constant, and the split is the useful part of the exercise. Watch the degree-to-radian sites: several are 50.0f * pi / 180.0f, which is worth a named radians() function rather than a shared constant.

  2. 13.2 — Break the π, and predict the picture first. Change k_reference_irradiance to 1.0 and, before rebuilding, write down what you expect: how much darker, and whether it is uniform.

    Hint

    The diffuse and specular terms both scale by 1/π. The ambient term does not — look at §4.4 and at which side of the equation it sits on.

    Solution sketch

    Not uniform. Lit surfaces fall to 1/π ≈ 32% of their light, but the ambient floor is untouched, so the contrast collapses as well as the brightness — shadows stay put while everything else comes down to meet them. Then remember that this is measured in light, and the display encodes it: 32% of the light is code 152, not code 81, so on screen it looks like a mild 40% darkening rather than the two-thirds cut it actually is. Lesson 6.1 is why those two sentences differ.

  3. 13.3 — Integrate a BRDF you have not been given. Write the hemisphere integrator yourself, from dω = sin θ dθ dφ, and use it to find the shininess at which the raw Blinn lobe's reflectance crosses 1. Then find it for Phong's lobe and explain the difference.

    Hint

    The crossover for Blinn is between 8 and 16 — verify_62 §D brackets it and asserts the bracket. Bisect. For Phong, note that verify_62's numbers put it well below Blinn's at the same exponent.

    Solution sketch

    Blinn crosses around shininess 11–12 (0.9500 at 12). Phong's lobe is narrower for the same exponent — the halfway vector moves at half the rate the mirror direction does, so cos^s about h covers roughly four times the solid angle of cos^s about R. That is the same factor-of-four Lesson 3.7 gave you when it said Blinn's exponent must be about 4× Phong's for the same visual tightness, arriving here as an energy statement instead of a visual one. Two lessons, two routes, one geometric fact.

  4. 13.4 — Give the light an inverse square. Add a point_light with a position rather than a direction, and the 1/d² falloff §2.3 derived. Put the falloff in irradiance_on's counterpart, not in the shading equation.

    Hint

    The signature has to change: a point light's irradiance depends on where the surface is, not only on which way it faces. And the naive 1/(d*d) goes to infinity at zero distance, which is a real problem in a scene where a lamp can be inside geometry.

    Solution sketch

    irradiance_on(vec3 surface_pos, vec3 normal_unit), returning irradiance * max(0, dot(n, l)) / max(d*d, r_min*r_min). The clamp is the standard trick and it is worth knowing it is a lie with a physical reading: a real lamp has a size, so beyond a certain closeness you are inside the emitter and the point approximation has failed. Choosing r_min as the lamp's radius makes the lie a model. Note also that "irradiance" for a point light is now a derived quantity — the authored number becomes radiant intensity, W/sr, which is the fourth word from §2.1 finally earning its place.

  5. 13.5 — The white furnace, for real. Build the classic test: a white Lambertian sphere lit by uniform radiance 1 from every direction, rendered with no directional light at all. A correct renderer produces a sphere that is indistinguishable from the background — a flat white disc with no shading whatsoever. Make our renderer do it, and explain what you had to change.

    Hint

    Set the albedo to 1 and the ambient to 1, and turn the key light off. §4.4 says what albedo * ambient evaluates to.

    Solution sketch

    You need change nothing, which is the point of the exercise: shade() returns exactly albedo * ambient = 1 for every normal, and the sphere vanishes. That is a genuine white-furnace pass for the diffuse term, and it works because of §4.4's cancellation. Now add a white specular and watch it fail — the sphere reappears, because the specular term reads the key light which is off, so it contributes nothing, and the ambient has no specular counterpart at all. That missing term is the specular half of image-based lighting, and it is Lesson 6.15's subject. A test that passes and then fails when you extend it is worth more than one that only passes.

14Recap & Next

The engine's shading equation now has units, and it has them in three separable places:

\[ L_o \;=\; \big(f_{\text{diffuse}} + f_{\text{specular}}\big)\, E_{\perp} \cos\theta \;+\; \text{albedo} \cdot L_{\text{ambient}} \]

In prose: outgoing radiance is the sum of the two BRDF lobes, times the light's perpendicular irradiance, times the cosine — plus the albedo times the ambient radiance, whose own π cancelled.

What the engine gained, concretely: a light whose scalar is a physical quantity with a name; lambert_brdf and specular_brdf, each in inverse steradians; an exposure convention that is written down and derived rather than implied; and — most useful for what comes next — a numerical hemisphere integrator that turns "is this model physically plausible?" into a number. That integrator is the shape every BRDF in the rest of this course gets checked with.

What it learned about itself: the π that Lambert's BRDF is supposed to carry has been sitting inside the light's intensity field since Lesson 3.6, and the proof is that moving it out and setting the light to π reproduces the reference render byte for byte, across 45% of sampled floats changing in their last bits. And the highlight it has drawn since 3.7 is not energy conserving — not because the lobe runs away (the 1/π stops that), but because diffuse and specular are added with no coupling, so a white glossy surface reflects 1.1386 of what hits it.

Next, Lesson 6.3: microfacet theory. That number — 1.1386 — is the door into it. The fix is not a better constant but a better story about what a surface is: not a smooth plane with an arbitrary shininess exponent, but a landscape of microscopic mirrors whose slopes have a statistical distribution. "Roughness" stops being a slider and becomes a statement about that distribution; the highlight's shape stops being pow(x, s) and becomes a consequence; and the coupling between the two lobes stops being missing, because light that bounced off a microfacet demonstrably did not go into the surface. Everything in this lesson — the ratio, the units, the integral that tests it — is the apparatus 6.3 needs to make that argument in numbers rather than adjectives.
