4. The product, and the two famous halves inside it

We now have the whole table, so we can multiply any two quaternions. Doing it by expanding sixteen products of components is correct and unreadable. There is a much better way to write it, and the better way is also where the lesson's next surprise lives.

4.1 Split it into a scalar and a vector

Write a quaternion as a real part and a vector part: q = w + v, where w is a number and v = xi + yj + zk is a vector of space wearing a coat. Then multiplying two of them is ordinary distribution:

\[ (w_1 + v_1)(w_2 + v_2) \;=\; w_1w_2 \;+\; w_1v_2 \;+\; v_1w_2 \;+\; v_1v_2. \]

The first three terms need no rules at all — a real times a real, and a real scaling a vector. Everything interesting is in the last one, v₁v₂, the product of two pure quaternions. Expand it against the table from §3, with v₁ = (a₁, b₁, c₁) and v₂ = (a₂, b₂, c₂):

\[ \begin{aligned} v_1v_2 = (a_1 i + b_1 j + c_1 k)(a_2 i + b_2 j + c_2 k) \end{aligned} \]

There are nine terms. Three of them are squares — a₁a₂ i², b₁b₂ j², c₁c₂ k² — and each contributes its coefficient with a minus sign, because all three units square to −1. Collect those three and you have

\[ -(a_1a_2 + b_1b_2 + c_1c_2) \;=\; -(v_1 \cdot v_2). \]

The other six are the mixed products, and they pair up. The k terms are a₁b₂\,ij + b₁a₂\,ji = (a₁b₂ − b₁a₂)k, using ji = −ij from §3.2 and nothing else. The i and j terms do the same thing with the indices rotated. So the six mixed terms collect into

\[ (b_1c_2 - c_1b_2)\,i + (c_1a_2 - a_1c_2)\,j + (a_1b_2 - b_1a_2)\,k \;=\; v_1 \times v_2. \]

Putting the two halves together:

\[ v_1 v_2 \;=\; -(v_1 \cdot v_2) \;+\; (v_1 \times v_2) \]
Where the dot and cross products came from

That line is not an analogy and it is not a repackaging. The dot product and the cross product are the two halves of the quaternion product, and they were extracted from exactly this expression by Josiah Willard Gibbs and Oliver Heaviside in the 1880s, who argued — persuasively, and they won — that most physics needs one half or the other and rarely both at once. Every vector-algebra course since has taught the two pieces without ever mentioning the thing they were cut out of. You have been using half a quaternion product since Lesson 1.7.

Substituting back, the whole product is:

\[ q\,p \;=\; \big(\, w_1w_2 - v_1\!\cdot\! v_2,\;\; w_1v_2 + w_2v_1 + v_1 \times v_2 \,\big) \]

That formula is the storage decision. It is why quat below is {float w; vec3 v;} rather than four loose floats: written that way, operator* is two lines that read as the formula, and written the other way it is sixteen indexed products that nobody can check. The bytes in memory are identical either way; the only thing the choice changes is whether the code can be read.

4.2 The one price, located exactly

Now swap the operands and go through the formula term by term. w₁w₂ is unchanged. v₁·v₂ is unchanged, because the dot product is symmetric. w₁v₂ + w₂v₁ is unchanged, because addition is. And v₁ × v₂ flips sign, because the cross product is antisymmetric. So:

\[ q\,p \;-\; p\,q \;=\; \big(\,0,\;\; 2\,(v_1 \times v_2)\,\big) \]

In prose: the difference between the two orders is purely a vector, and it is exactly twice the cross product of the two vector parts.

Three sentences, one fact

Quaternions do not commute. The cross product is antisymmetric. Rotations of space do not commute. These are not three related observations. They are one observation said three ways, and the equation above is the identification. The price the fourth dimension charges is a single term, and you have known that term's sign convention since Lesson 2.4.

4.3 A worked example, by hand

Take q = 2 + i − j + 3k and p = −1 + 2i + k. In the split notation, w₁ = 2, v₁ = (1, −1, 3), w₂ = −1, v₂ = (2, 0, 1).

The scalar part is w₁w₂ − v₁·v₂. The dot product is 1·2 + (−1)·0 + 3·1 = 5, so the scalar part is 2·(−1) − 5 = −7.

The vector part is w₁v₂ + w₂v₁ + v₁ × v₂, three vectors added:

  • w₁v₂ = 2 · (2, 0, 1) = (4, 0, 2)
  • w₂v₁ = −1 · (1, −1, 3) = (−1, 1, −3)
  • v₁ × v₂ = ((−1)(1) − (3)(0), (3)(2) − (1)(1), (1)(0) − (−1)(2)) = (−1, 5, 2)

Adding those three componentwise: (4 − 1 − 1, 0 + 1 + 5, 2 − 3 + 2) = (2, 6, 1). So

\[ q\,p \;=\; -7 + 2i + 6j + k. \]

Run the same arithmetic the other way round — only the cross product changes, to (1, −5, −2) — and you get p q = −7 + 4i − 4j − 3k. The scalar parts agree, as §4.2 said they must, and the difference is (0, −2, 10, 4) = 2 · (−1, 5, 2), which is twice the cross product exactly. The harness pushes the same numbers through the real type:

4.4 The control

§B.3's identity was measured on one example, and one example is a coincidence waiting to be discovered. §B.5 is the control that turns it into a claim: if the cross term really is the only thing that changes under a swap, then a pair of quaternions whose vector parts are parallel must commute exactly, since their cross product is zero. It does: 

   worst relative |qp|-|q||p|:        2.550e-07
  [PASS] B.4  the norm is multiplicative
  [PASS] B.5  CONTROL: parallel vector parts commute

B.4 is quietly one of the most important lines in the file. |qp| = |q||p| means the unit quaternions are closed under multiplication: compose two rotations and you get a rotation, with no renormalisation step in between and no drift beyond rounding. It is also the same demand as §3.1 in different clothes, which is why they force the same table.

5. Mirrors in space: the sandwich and the half-angle, together

§2 promised that a rotation is two reflections. This section does that in algebra, and three things fall out of one calculation that are normally presented as three separate facts you have to accept.

5.1 A reflection, as a quaternion product

Take a plane through the origin with unit normal n, and a vector v. Treat both as pure quaternions and form the product n v n. Expand it with §4.1's formula, twice.

First n v. Both are pure, so nv = −(n·v) + (n×v). Now multiply that on the right by n, which is a general quaternion times a pure one:

\[ \begin{aligned} (nv)n &= \big(-(n\cdot v) + (n\times v)\big)\,n \\ &= -(n\cdot v)\,n \;+\; \big(-\!(n\times v)\cdot n \;+\; (n\times v)\times n\big) \end{aligned} \]

The term (n×v)·n is zero — a cross product is perpendicular to both its arguments, so it cannot have a component along n. And (n×v)×n is the standard triple product, which expands to v(n·n) − n(n·v) = v − (n·v)n since n is unit. Adding up:

\[ n\,v\,n \;=\; v \;-\; 2(n\cdot v)\,n \]
You have had this function since Lesson 1.8

v − 2(n·v)n is engine::reflect(v, n), in math/vec3.hpp, derived in Module 1 for a ball bouncing off a wall and used ever since for mirror directions in gfx/light.hpp. It is the Householder reflection, and it is what n v n computes. Two routes to one answer is a check; one route is a hope, so §C.1 measures the engine's function against the quaternion spelling across 20,000 random normals and vectors: 

   worst |n v n - reflect(v, n)|:     1.046e-06
  [PASS] C.1  n v n is the Householder reflection

So a reflection is one multiplication on each side. Compare complex.hpp's reflect_in_line, which is m² v̄ — the shapes differ because a mirror in the plane is named by its direction and a mirror in space by its normal, and because conjugating a pure quaternion negates it. The content is the same.

5.2 Do it twice, and watch what appears

Reflect in the plane with normal n₀, then in the plane with normal n₁. That is n v n applied twice, reading left to right through the sequence:

\[ v \;\longmapsto\; n_1\,(n_0\,v\,n_0)\,n_1 \;=\; (n_1n_0)\,v\,(n_0n_1) \]

using nothing but associativity to move the brackets. Now look at the two outer factors. They are the same two things in opposite orders, and for pure quaternions there is a name for that relationship:

\[ \overline{n_1 n_0} \;=\; \overline{n_0}\,\overline{n_1} \;=\; (-n_0)(-n_1) \;=\; n_0 n_1 \]

Conjugation reverses a product — that is a one-line consequence of the definition, true in any such algebra — and conjugating a pure quaternion just negates it, so the two minus signs cancel. Therefore, writing q = n₁n₀:

\[ v \;\longmapsto\; q\,v\,\bar q \]
The sandwich was not chosen

It fell out of composing two reflections. Nobody decided that a rotation should be applied from both sides; that is simply what "do two reflections" looks like once you write a reflection as n v n, and the right-hand factor is a conjugate because reversing a product of pure quaternions is exactly what conjugation does.

5.3 And q itself is the half-angle object

We have a formula for the rotation in terms of q = n₁n₀. Work out what that quaternion actually is, using §4.1 once more, and let φ be the angle between the two normals:

\[ q \;=\; n_1 n_0 \;=\; -(n_1 \cdot n_0) \;+\; (n_1 \times n_0) \;=\; -\cos\varphi \;-\; \sin\varphi\;\hat n \]

where n̂ is the unit vector along n₀ × n₁ — the line where the two planes meet, which is the axis, exactly as §2's two hands predicted. (The sign on the second term is because n₁ × n₀ = −(n₀ × n₁), and |n₀ × n₁| = sin φ.)

So q = −(cos φ + sin φ · n̂). There is an overall minus sign sitting in front of the thing we want, and nothing in the derivation determines it — reflecting in −n₀ instead of n₀ gives the same mirror plane and flips it. Negate, which §8 will justify properly and which you can take on trust for two paragraphs, and write θ = 2φ for the rotation angle the two mirrors generate:

\[ \boxed{\;q \;=\; \cos(\theta/2) \;+\; \sin(\theta/2)\,\hat n\;} \]

In prose: the quaternion for a rotation of θ about the unit axis n̂ has real part cos(θ/2) and vector part sin(θ/2) times the axis.

Prediction 3 discharged — and prediction 4 arrived uninvited

The θ/2 is not a convention. It is there because the mirrors are φ apart and the rotation is 2φ, which is a fact about reflections that Lesson 7.3 demonstrated in the plane with a protractor. And the double cover appeared inside the derivation, as a sign nobody could pin down, before anybody stated it as a property. That is the honest order of discovery and it is worth noticing: q and −q are not two conventions for one object, they are two genuinely different pairs of mirrors that generate the same turn.

5.5 The control, and it is the sharpest one in the lesson

§C.5 says composing quaternions matches composing matrices in the same order — M(qp) = M(q)M(p). That is a claim about the sandwich's orientation, and here is why a naive test of it is worthless.

The other sandwich, q̄ v q, is also a rotation. It sends the basis vectors somewhere perfectly sensible, its matrix is orthonormal, its determinant is 1, and if you checked it on a single rotation you would see nothing wrong. What it gets wrong is the order: it is an anti-homomorphism, so every composition comes out backwards. §C.6 builds it deliberately and demands both halves of that statement: 

   conj(q) v q:  |W(qp) - W(p)W(q)| = 2.980e-07
                 |W(qp) - W(q)W(p)| = 1.975e+00
  [PASS] C.6  CONTROL: the other sandwich reverses order

The first number must be tiny and the second must be large, and requiring both is what makes this a control rather than a tautology. A test that only checked "the wrong sandwich differs from the right one" would pass on a typo.

This also settles a question that is easy to get wrong in your own code and painful to debug: q * p means "do p, then do q", the same right-to-left reading as A * B * v. That reading is not free — it is a consequence of choosing q v q̄ over q̄ v q, and the other choice reverses every composition in the engine.

6. Implementation: the type

Everything above is now code. math/quat.hpp is written to be read as a diff against math/complex.hpp, function for function, and that is not a stylistic conceit — Lesson 7.3 chose the names in that file with this mapping in mind, so that the harder header could be read as the easier one with one more imaginary unit and one crucial loss.

6.1 The type, and why the storage is the derivation

struct quat
{
    float w{1.0f};   ///< cos(θ/2) for a unit rotation
    vec3 v{};        ///< sin(θ/2)·n̂ for a unit rotation

    static constexpr quat identity() { return {}; }
    static constexpr quat i() { return {0.0f, {1, 0, 0}}; }
    static constexpr quat j() { return {0.0f, {0, 1, 0}}; }
    static constexpr quat k() { return {0.0f, {0, 0, 1}}; }
    static constexpr quat pure(vec3 p) { return {0.0f, p}; }
};

Four contiguous floats in the order w, x, y, z. A good deal of published code — and most GPU-side packing — uses x, y, z, w instead. Neither is more correct; they are incompatible, and code that memcpys a quaternion across that boundary without saying which it means has a bug that looks like a 180° error about a diagonal axis. It is on the Conventions page §8e, which is where this course puts things that cannot be deduced.

The three imaginary units are worth typing into a test the first time you meet them, because quat::i() is a half turn, not a quarter turn, and that catches everyone arriving from complex numbers. complex::i() is a quarter turn of the plane; quat::i() has w = cos(θ/2) = 0, so θ = π. Multiply it by itself and you get −1, which is the same rotation as +1 — the double cover, in the smallest example there is.

6.2 The product

Two lines, and they are §4.1's formula read straight across: 

[[nodiscard]] constexpr quat operator*(quat a, quat b)
{
    return {a.w * b.w - dot(a.v, b.v),
            b.v * a.w + a.v * b.w + cross(a.v, b.v)};
}

Sixteen multiplies and twelve adds. A mat3 product is twenty-seven and eighteen. §10 measures whether that operation count survives contact with a real processor, and the answer is a useful lesson in itself.

6.3 Conjugate, norm, inverse — the block that did not change

Every function in this block is complex.hpp's function with im widened to a vec3. Not "analogous to" — the same algebra and the same proofs, because none of those proofs used commutativity. That is worth noticing while reading: the things that broke on the way up are exactly the things that mentioned the order of a product, and nothing here does. 

constexpr quat conjugate(quat q) { return {q.w, -q.v}; }

constexpr float length_squared(quat q)
{
    return q.w * q.w + dot(q.v, q.v);
}

inline quat inverse(quat q)
{
    const float len_sq = length_squared(q);
    if (len_sq <= 0.0f) { return quat::identity(); }
    return conjugate(q) / len_sq;
}

One detail deserves a sentence because it looks as though it ought to be a problem and is not. inverse is a genuine two-sided inverse even though the algebra does not commute: q·q̄ and q̄·q are both |q|², because the cross product of v with itself is zero. The one place non-commutativity would have hurt most is the one place it cannot reach.

6.4 Applying: the sandwich, optimised — and a correction

Computing q v q̄ literally is two quaternion products: 24 multiplies and 17 adds, with a known-zero scalar part being carried through both. Expanding it symbolically for unit q and collecting gives a much better form:

\[ v' \;=\; v \;+\; 2w\,(u \times v) \;+\; 2\,\big(u \times (u \times v)\big), \qquad u = q.v \]
[[nodiscard]] constexpr vec3 rotate(quat q, vec3 v)
{
    const vec3 t = cross(q.v, v) * 2.0f;
    return v + t * q.w + cross(q.v, t);
}

Two crosses, two scales, two adds: 18 multiplies and 12 adds. It is Rodrigues' formula in disguise, which it must be, since §C.4 measured that they describe the same turn.

A correction, and the harness is what caught it

The first draft of this function's documentation said "a non-unit q scales the result by |q|², exactly as a non-unit z does in the plane". That is true of the literal q v q̄ and false of the form above. Getting from (w² − |u|²)v + 2w(u×v) + 2(u·v)u to the two crosses substitutes u×(u×v) = (u·v)u − |u|²v, and the two expressions agree only when w² + |u|² = 1. Off the unit sphere this is a different linear map — not a rotation, not a scaled rotation, not a similarity at all. §F.6 measures both: 

   |q|    literal/|q|^2 err   rotate() err
   1.000    3.332e-08           0.000e+00
   1.100    1.032e-07           1.352e-01
   1.500    7.451e-08           8.046e-01
  [PASS] F.6  CONTROL: the literal form scales, rotate() does not

This matters far more than a documentation slip usually does, and §10.5 is where it bites.

6.5 Axes and angles, in and out

inline quat quat_from_axis_angle(vec3 unit_axis, float angle)
{
    const float half = angle * 0.5f;
    return {std::cos(half), unit_axis * std::sin(half)};
}

That is the only trigonometry in the file, together with its inverse and the Euler bridge that calls it — and noticing that is the point of the whole representation. Trig appears exactly at the boundary where a human angle enters or leaves. Composing, inverting, applying and (in Lesson 7.5) interpolating are arithmetic. Lesson 7.2 measured 82% of an axis-angle slerp call going on trigonometry; that number is what this header exists to delete.

There are also quat_x, quat_y and quat_z — the twins of mat3's rotation_x/y/z, spelled deliberately in parallel so that a call site can move across by changing four characters: 

// before — 27 multiplies
t.rotation = rotation_y(a) * rotation_x(b);

// after — 16 multiplies
q = quat_y(a) * quat_x(b);

and quat_from_euler, which is rotation_from_euler with rotation_* replaced by quat_* and nothing else touched — same intrinsic y-x-z convention, same order, same argument names. That the two agree is the check that this file's product and mat3's product describe the same composition:

So nudge one component of the quaternion by 0.001, renormalise, and demand that the agreement break by a predictable amount — 0.1076°, which is neither zero nor large.
