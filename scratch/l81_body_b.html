4. The yardstick that cannot see the bug

Order of accuracy is the number every numerical-methods course teaches first, and it is easy to measure: run to a fixed simulated time, halve the step, and see what the error does. Halving means first order. Quartering means second. Section A of verify_81.cpp does it on our spring, running exactly one full period — which makes the reference free of approximation, because after one period the exact answer is the state we started in.

Explicit Euler halves when the step halves, so it is first order, as advertised. Velocity Verlet quarters, so it is second order, as advertised. And semi-implicit Euler also quarters, which is not what any textbook will tell you, because semi-implicit Euler is a first-order method.

That is worth stopping on rather than waving away, and the explanation is the whole lesson in advance. Semi-implicit Euler's error on this problem has two parts: an amplitude error (is the orbit the right size?) and a phase error (has it gone round at the right rate?). Section 5 will show that the amplitude error is exactly zero — not small, zero — which leaves only the phase error, and the phase error turns out to be O(h²). So what you are reading in that column is not a promotion. It is a fact about the harmonic oscillator, and it is a fact caused by the very property this lesson is about. Measure the same method on a problem with no such structure and it is first order again.

The last row is the instrument, not the result. At h = 1/1920 one period is 1,920 float additions on numbers of size 1, and float cannot resolve below about 1e-6 after that much accumulation. Verlet's 1.061e-06 is not four times better than the row above it; it is noise. The harness says so rather than letting the column be read, and it is the same discipline as Lesson 7.8's −160 dB harmonics: a number at the noise floor is a fact about the meter.

4.1 …and now the other question

Look again at what A.1 asked. It held the simulated time fixed at one period and shrank the step. That is the mathematician's question — behaviour as h → 0 — and it is the right question if you are choosing a step size for a one-off calculation.

It is not the question a game asks. A game fixes h at 1/60 and never changes it, then runs for an hour. Section A.3 asks that question instead: same step, growing time.

Order of accuracy is a statement about the limit as the step goes to zero at a fixed time. Stability is a statement about the limit as time goes to infinity at a fixed step. They are different questions, both of them legitimate, and the second one is the one that decides whether your game works after the player has been in the level for five minutes.

So we need a different instrument. And the useful thing about the harmonic oscillator is that it hands us one.

5. Phase space, and the one number

Stop plotting position against time. Plot position against velocity instead — every state of the system is one point in that plane, and the simulation is a point moving around it. This is called phase space, and it is the single most useful change of viewpoint in this lesson.

Why it helps: for a spring, the exact trajectory in phase space is a circle. With x = A cos(ωt) and v = −Aω sin(ωt), plotting x horizontally and v/ω vertically gives x² + (v/ω)² = A² — a circle of radius A, traversed once per period, forever. There is nothing to interpret. A correct integrator produces points on that circle. An incorrect one produces points that are not on it, and you can see which way they are wrong at a glance.

@@FIG_PHASE@@

Figure 3 takes one point on the circle and applies one step of each rule, with a deliberately coarse hω = 0.7 so the step is a visible arc rather than a dot. Look first at the grey dotted horizontal line: both endpoints are at the same height. That is not a coincidence and it is not an artefact of the drawing — both rules ran the identical velocity update, v ← v − hω²x, from the identical starting state. The only difference between them is which x the position line was told to move at, and it moves them apart horizontally and in no other way.

The red dot is outside the circle. Run the step again from there and it will be further outside, and again, and again. The green dot is not on the circle either — and that is the honest surprise of this figure, worth stating plainly before §6 makes it the main event: semi-implicit Euler does not put you back on the exact orbit. It puts you on the dashed green ellipse, which is a slightly sheared, slightly rotated cousin of the circle — and it keeps you there forever.

5.1 One step is a matrix

Here is the move that turns all of this from pictures into a number. For a linear force — a = −ω²x, which is the whole point of choosing this test — every integrator step is a linear transformation of the state. Write the state as a column of two numbers and the step is a matrix acting on it, exactly as in Lesson 2.5.

Explicit Euler, written out with the two lines in the order the code has them:

\[ x_{n+1} = x_n + h\,v_n, \qquad v_{n+1} = v_n - h\omega^{2}x_n \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \begin{bmatrix} x_{n+1} \\ v_{n+1} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & h \\ -h\omega^{2} & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x_n \\ v_n \end{bmatrix} \]

In plain text: the new state is [[1, h], [−hω², 1]] times the old one. The top row says "new position is old position plus h times old velocity"; the bottom row says "new velocity is old velocity minus h·ω² times old position".

Semi-implicit Euler differs in one place. Its position line uses vn+1, so substitute:

\[ x_{n+1} = x_n + h\,v_{n+1} = x_n + h\left(v_n - h\omega^{2}x_n\right) = \left(1 - h^{2}\omega^{2}\right)x_n + h\,v_n \]
\[ \begin{bmatrix} x_{n+1} \\ v_{n+1} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 - h^{2}\omega^{2} & h \\ -h\omega^{2} & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x_n \\ v_n \end{bmatrix} \]

In plain text: the same matrix except that the top-left entry is 1 − h²ω² instead of 1. That single entry is the whole difference between the two methods, and it appeared because the position line was allowed to see the velocity line's answer.

5.2 The determinant, and what it does to energy

A 2×2 matrix's determinant is the factor by which it scales area. Lesson 2.5 introduced a matrix as "where the basis vectors land"; take the unit square those basis vectors span, push it through the matrix, and the parallelogram that comes out has area equal to the determinant. The inset in figure 3 is literally that experiment, run on a small square of states.

Compute both, and it takes one line each:

\[ \det\begin{bmatrix} 1 & h \\ -h\omega^{2} & 1 \end{bmatrix} = 1\cdot 1 - h\cdot(-h\omega^{2}) = 1 + h^{2}\omega^{2} \]
\[ \det\begin{bmatrix} 1 - h^{2}\omega^{2} & h \\ -h\omega^{2} & 1 \end{bmatrix} = (1 - h^{2}\omega^{2}) - h\cdot(-h\omega^{2}) = 1 - h^{2}\omega^{2} + h^{2}\omega^{2} = 1 \]

In plain text: explicit Euler's determinant is 1 + h²ω², which is greater than 1 for any non-zero h and any non-zero ω. Semi-implicit Euler's is exactly 1 — the two h²ω² terms cancel, algebraically, with no approximation anywhere.

That cancellation is the whole lesson, so stare at it for a second. The −h²ω² in the top-left came from the position line using the new velocity. The +h²ω² came from the off-diagonal product. They are the same quantity because they have the same origin: the velocity update's effect, counted once in each place. Explicit Euler only gets one of them and so has a surplus.

5.3 Backward Euler, the mirror image

The obvious next idea, once you know explicit Euler grows, is to use the new state on both lines: vn+1 = vn − hω²xn+1 and xn+1 = xn + h·vn+1. That is backward (implicit) Euler. It is an implicit equation — the unknown appears on both sides — and for this linear force it can be solved in closed form. Substituting and collecting gives

\[ \begin{bmatrix} x_{n+1} \\ v_{n+1} \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{1 + h^{2}\omega^{2}} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & h \\ -h\omega^{2} & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x_n \\ v_n \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \det = \frac{1 + h^{2}\omega^{2}}{\left(1 + h^{2}\omega^{2}\right)^{2}} = \frac{1}{1 + h^{2}\omega^{2}} \]

In plain text: backward Euler's matrix is explicit Euler's matrix divided by (1 + h²ω²), and its determinant is the exact reciprocal of explicit Euler's. Where one inflates, the other deflates, by precisely the same factor.

This is why backward Euler is described as unconditionally stable and why almost nothing in real time uses it. It cannot blow up, because it is losing energy on every step — a released pendulum grinds to a halt, a stack of boxes slowly sinks into the floor, and a ragdoll goes limp. And you pay for that damping: for anything other than a linear force there is no closed form, so each step becomes a root find. Costly and lossy. Where implicit methods do appear in games — cloth, hair — it is because that damping is exactly what is wanted. integrate.hpp therefore has no implicit_euler, and its enum says why in a comment rather than leaving the omission to look like an oversight.

A determinant that is never evaluated is a claim you are being asked to believe. Section B of the harness takes three states forming a small parallelogram in the (x, v) plane, steps all three with the same rule, and computes the area that comes out.

The parallelogram is half a metre across, not infinitesimal, and that is deliberate. The update is a matrix — it is exactly linear — so the parallelogram's size cancels out of the ratio algebraically, and any size gives the same answer. The first version of this section used ε = 1e-3 on the reasonable-sounding grounds that a derivative wants a small step, and threw away three digits of precision to cancellation between nearly equal floats: it reported the control at 0.999974 instead of 1.000000. When the thing you are probing is genuinely linear, a small probe buys nothing and costs precision.

Now connect the determinant to something you care about. The area enclosed by a phase-space orbit of a spring is π·A·(Aω) = πωA², and the energy per unit mass is E = ½ω²A² — so the enclosed area is 2πE/ω, which is to say area is energy, up to a constant that never changes. A step that multiplies area by 1 + h²ω² multiplies energy by 1 + h²ω². After N steps it has multiplied energy by (1 + h²ω²)ᴺ, and that is not an estimate; it is what the matrix does.

At h = 1/60 and ω = 2π, h²ω² = 0.010966229. Over one second — 60 steps — that is 1.010966229⁶⁰ = 1.924. Over one minute, 3,600 steps:

5.5 And a smaller step does not fix it

The instinct at this point is to reach for h. It does not work, and the reason is visible in B.2: the determinant is 1 + h²ω², which approaches 1 as h shrinks but never reaches it. Every step still adds energy; you have only made the steps smaller and bought more of them.

Quantify it. Growth over a fixed simulated time T is (1 + h²ω²)T/h, and for small h the exponent times the log is about (T/h)·h²ω² = T·h·ω². The growth per second is proportional to h — linear, not exponential — so halving the step halves the exponent, and the time to double the energy is proportional to 1/h:

     h = 4.201e-07 s, i.e. 2380529 steps per second.

Ten times the step rate buys ten times the delay. And read backwards — which is how an engineer would actually use it — holding the error to one per cent over a ten-minute level needs 2.4 million steps per second. For one spring, at 1 Hz, which is the gentlest restoring force anything in a game would ever have. The answer is not a smaller step. The answer is a different rule, and it costs nothing.

Why the 30 Hz row misses by 5% and the others do not. Nothing is wrong with the prediction; the measurement is quantised. The energy does not climb smoothly — it also wobbles once per oscillation, by hω in relative terms, which is 21% at 30 Hz. So the moment the run crosses 2× is caught at whatever phase of that wobble it happens to be in. By 120 Hz the wobble is 5% and the rows agree to three digits. The next section is where that wobble comes from, and it turns out to be the most interesting quantity in the lesson.
