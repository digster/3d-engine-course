Module 8 — Physics · Lesson 8.9

Impulse Response: Restitution and Friction

Five lessons of collision detection have built a machine that answers questions. 8.4 asks whether two boxes overlap; 8.5, how far apart two convex shapes are; 8.6, how deep they are inside each other and which way is out; 8.7, at which four points; 8.8, which pairs are worth asking about at all. Run the whole of it on a crate falling onto a floor and the crate falls through the floor, because nothing in engine::phys has ever written a velocity. This lesson writes velocities. The obvious way — a spring proportional to the overlap — is four lines long, and §1 gives it every chance and measures what it costs: 164 tuned settings, and the best of them lands the crate perfectly at 108 mm of penetration, or keeps it at 12.6 mm while it bounces 367 mm forever, and not both. A penalty contact is not broken; it is tuned, and the tuning belongs to the scene rather than to the material — the same spring under a 200 kg crate lets it 962 mm into a half-metre floor. The impulse formulation has no stiffness to be stable about, because it does not model a contact as a thing that lasts: a collision is an event, and the question is what single change in velocity leaves the surfaces moving the way a contact requires. That is one linear equation per contact in one scalar unknown, and the constant in it — the effective mass — is the whole of the lesson's arithmetic. We derive it, then rearrange it into a form that proves the division is safe, and check the two forms against each other over 200,000 random contacts. Restitution rides on the same equation as a change of target velocity, and brings the strangest bug in Module 8 with it: because gravity is applied before the solver looks, the approach speed is already too large by g·h, so a ball settles into a hop that never dies — 2.2 cm at e = 0.8, 58 cm at e = 0.95 — and §5 predicts the exact speed it converges to in closed form and measures it at 1.000×. Friction is a tangential impulse clipped to Coulomb's cone, and the square that every second engine uses instead of the disc is measured here at 42.7% direction-dependence, in a basis chosen from the floor's normal. Along the way: a slope test that disagrees with atan(μ) by four degrees and turns out to be measuring the fixture; a sphere that stops sliding at exactly 5/7 of the speed it landed with, whatever μ is, with the 0.06% it misses by predicted from the manifold's own contact-point convention; a solve order that, at one pass, has no friction at all; and an honest ending — one pass over four contact points does not hold a crate up. It sinks 275 mm in fifteen seconds. That is Lesson 8.10's reason to exist, arriving early enough to be measured rather than promised.

1. The problem: the spring you cannot afford

Here is the state of the engine at the end of Lesson 8.8. Drop a crate on a floor. The broadphase reports the pair; GJK says they are touching; EPA says how deep and which way out; the manifold gives four contact points with ids that survive across frames. Every one of those stages runs, correctly, at 60 Hz. And the crate goes straight through the floor, because not one line of engine::phys has ever assigned to a velocity. The whole subsystem could have been marked const.

So: something has to push. The response that occurs to everybody first — and it is a good instinct, because it reuses everything Lesson 8.2 built — is a spring. You know the overlap; push the bodies apart with a force proportional to it; hand that force to add_force_at and let F = ma do the rest. It is called a penalty method, it is four lines long, and the honest thing to do is to try it properly before rejecting it.

The stiffness is not a free parameter

Start with the easiest possible question: a crate of mass m resting on the floor, not moving. For it to rest, the spring must exactly carry its weight, so at a penetration x,

\[ k\,x = m g \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad k = \frac{mg}{x} \] k * x = m * g, so k = m * g / x

Choose how far you are willing to let things sink and the stiffness is decided for you. Now ask what that stiffness does to the simulation. A spring of stiffness k acting on a mass m oscillates at

\[ \omega = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}} = \sqrt{\frac{mg/x}{m}} = \sqrt{\frac{g}{x}} \] omega = sqrt(k/m) = sqrt(g/x)

The mass cancels. Read that again, because it is the whole argument: the rate at which a penalty contact oscillates does not depend on what is resting on it. You cannot buy stability by making the crate heavier, or by making it lighter. The penetration you are willing to see decides the rate, for every body in the scene, and Lesson 8.1's stability limit for semi-implicit Euler — h·ω < 2, derived there from a determinant and returned by max_stable_step — turns that rate into a minimum frame rate for the entire simulation.

Worked, with real numbers. A 20 kg crate, g = 9.81.
Allow 1 mm of sink: k = 20 × 9.81 / 0.001 = 196,200 N/m.
Then ω = √(9.81 / 0.001) = √9810 = 99.05 rad/s.
8.1's limit: h < 2/ω = 0.02019 s, which is 49.5 Hz.
Turn it round: at 60 Hz, ω < 2/h = 120 rad/s, so x > g/ω² = 9.81/14400 = 0.681 mm.
At 60 Hz, the finest a stable penalty spring can hold anything is 0.68 mm.

And “stable” is not “right”. 8.1 §6 measured an orbit at h·ω = 1.95 that was perfectly bounded and swung by a factor of forty. Sitting at the edge of the stability limit buys you a contact that does not explode and does visibly throb.

And the static number was the optimistic one

Everything above was about a crate that is already at rest. A crate that is arriving is a different question, and the spring has no say in it at all. All of the impact's kinetic energy has to go into the spring before the crate stops, so

\[ \tfrac12 m v^2 = \tfrac12 k x^2 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad x = v\sqrt{\frac{m}{k}} \] half m v squared = half k x squared, so x = v * sqrt(m/k)

There is no g in that and the mass does not cancel. The dynamic penetration is set by the impact speed and the weight, and the spring you tuned to hold a resting crate at 1 mm has no opinion about it.

Worked. Our crate falls 20 cm onto the floor, so it arrives at v = √(2 × 9.81 × 0.2) = 1.981 m/s.
With k = 196,200: x = 1.981 × √(20/196200) = 1.981 × 0.010096 = 20.0 mm.
Twenty times the millimetre it was tuned for, before any discretisation. The harness measures 44.1 mm, because a 60 Hz step overshoots the turning point.
To get 1 mm out of that impact you would need k = m v²/x² = 20 × 3.924 / 10⁻⁶ = 7.85 × 10⁷ N/m, which oscillates at ω = 1981 rad/s and demands at least 990 Hz.

Giving it its best shot

That is the argument as it is usually made, and it is not quite fair, because we have not turned the other knob. An undamped spring gives back every joule it took, so a penalty contact with no damping is a contact with e = 1: the crate returns to its drop height and keeps doing so. Damping is what turns a bounce into a landing. And a softer spring is calmer and sinks further, so there are two knobs and they trade against each other.

So §A of the harness sweeps both: four stiffnesses, forty-one dampings each, three seconds of simulation apiece, 164 settings, and reports the best result for each stiffness. The right-hand table of Figure 1 is what came back, and the section was drafted expecting to show that no setting works. It does not show that. 

     static sink      k (N/m)  best zeta      max depth     late swing
        50.00 mm       3924.0       0.40     108.600 mm      0.0000 mm
        10.00 mm      19620.0       0.20      50.092 mm      0.0000 mm
         1.00 mm     196200.0       0.35      18.910 mm    275.4744 mm
         0.10 mm    1962000.1       0.30      12.550 mm    366.7545 mm

  impulse solver, nothing tuned                        12.553 mm      0.0000 mm

Read the two middle columns together, because the shape of the result is the result. The soft springs settle the crate perfectly — zero residual bounce — at 109 mm and 50 mm of penetration. The crate is one metre tall, so the first of those has swallowed eleven per cent of it. The stiff springs keep the penetration respectable and cannot be made to settle at all: 275 mm and 367 mm of residual bounce, forever. At 60 Hz you may have a crate that sits still or a crate that sits on the surface, and not both.

The impulse solver's row has no knobs in it. It lands the crate at 12.55 mm with zero residual bounce — better than every one of the 164 settings on both axes at once.

The part that actually kills it

And then the measurement that settles the argument, which is not about any of the numbers above. Take the best setting found — the one tuned on a 20 kg crate dropped from 1.2 m — and apply it, unchanged, to scenes it was not tuned on: 

  scene                        penalty depth penalty swing impulse depth
  20 kg from 1.2 m (tuned)         108.60 mm      0.000 mm     12.554 mm
  200 kg from 1.2 m                962.13 mm    152.444 mm     12.554 mm
  2 kg from 1.2 m                   20.43 mm    314.988 mm     12.554 mm
  20 kg from 5 m                   369.54 mm      0.289 mm     46.627 mm

The 200 kg crate goes 962 mm into a floor half a metre thick: it is through, and out the other side. The 2 kg crate does not sink but bounces 315 mm forever. The same crate dropped from 5 m instead of 1.2 m goes 370 mm in. Meanwhile the impulse column does not move at all except where the physics says it should — the 5 m drop arrives faster, so it travels further in the step before anything notices, which is a statement about the time step rather than about the solver, and §10 comes back to it.

That is the case against penalty contacts, and it is not “they are unstable”. They are tunable, and the tuning is a property of the scene — of the masses in it and the speeds they arrive at — rather than of the materials in contact. A physics engine is supposed to take a designer's scene and simulate it; one whose contacts must be re-tuned every time somebody changes a crate's weight is not an engine, it is a rehearsal.

Penalty methods are not a historical curiosity, and this section is not a hit piece. Soft-body and cloth solvers use them constantly, because a cloth is a mesh of springs and the stiffness is the material rather than a tuning constant. Position-based dynamics — the family behind most cloth in shipping games — can be read as a penalty method moved into position space to dodge exactly the stability limit above. What they are bad at is rigid contact, where the “spring” has no physical stiffness to be and the correct answer is infinity.
