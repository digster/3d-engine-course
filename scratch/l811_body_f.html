14. What joints leave behind

§7's block made each joint exact, and §7 already said what it cannot do: a chain is a system that Gauss–Seidel crosses one joint per sweep, exactly as 8.10's tower was crossed one contact per sweep. A chain adds the one thing a tower of equal crates does not have, which is a mass ratio. A heavy lamp on a light chain, a wrecking ball on a cable, a character's torso on a hand: every sweep, the light links have to transmit the heavy one's weight, and an impulse that a light link can deliver barely moves the heavy one. The harness hangs ten 1 kg links from a fixed ceiling with an end weight of M kg, lets them settle, and measures how far the whole chain has stretched — the sum of every joint's gap, which ideally is zero. 

     end weight    8 sweeps    16         32         64        128
          1 kg      1.33 mm     0.60 mm     0.24 mm     0.08 mm     0.01 mm
         10 kg     18.36 mm     8.86 mm     4.13 mm     1.79 mm     0.66 mm
        100 kg    388.14 mm   158.73 mm    70.39 mm    34.66 mm    16.72 mm
       1000 kg   2471.50 mm   558.67 mm  1216.53 mm   341.77 mm   165.20 mm

At 1:1 eight sweeps leave a millimetre and a third over ten joints. The stretch grows roughly in proportion to the ratio — 18 mm at 10:1, 388 mm at 100:1, a chain stretched by 16% of its own length — and each doubling of the sweeps roughly halves it, which is the signature of a solve that has not converged rather than of a steady state it has reached. At 1000:1 the numbers stop meaning anything: the chain is not settling at all, it is bouncing, and the mean over half a second is not even monotone in the sweep count. That is the honest capability of this solver on a chain, and it is 8.10 §14's table again, in the other orientation.

The same work, spent differently

There is a better answer than more sweeps, and it follows from §5. The error a joint creates in a step is second order in the step — the drift is (ωh)²·d/2 — and the error a sweep has to remove is whatever the last step left. So consider spending the same number of joint visits per frame differently: instead of one 1/60 s step with eight sweeps, take eight 1/480 s steps with one sweep each. 

     schedule                       stretch
     8 sweeps x 1 step              388.142 mm
     4 sweeps x 2 steps             163.119 mm
     2 sweeps x 4 steps              49.051 mm
     1 sweep  x 8 steps              20.491 mm

Nineteen times less stretch for the same visits. Two things compound. Each small step starts from the answer the last one reached an eighth of a frame ago, so the error its single sweep must remove is small; and eight small steps each create an eighth of the drift of one big one. Iterating the big step addresses neither. This is the argument of Macklin and colleagues' "Small Steps in Physics Simulation" (2019) and of Box2D v3's soft step, and it is where real-time rigid-body solvers have been moving. It is not free here: every sub-step re-runs prepare, and in a scene with contacts it re-runs the narrow phase too, which 8.10 §13 priced at 1.1 ms for two thousand bodies. So it is named, measured, and left for Module 9's profiling lessons to price properly — and the demo's U key does it, for a chain, so you can watch the difference.

Against 8.10 §13's 129 ns per contact manifold per sweep, a hinge with a limit and a motor costs less than half of one crate resting on another, and a socket about a tenth. The rows-versus-block line is §7's finding in the solver's own loop: 35.8 ns against 13.7. A ragdoll of fifteen joints at eight sweeps is under 10 µs a frame even if every one of them is a hinge with a limit and a motor, which is to say that joints are not where a physics budget goes; contacts are, and 8.12's ragdoll lying on a floor will spend far more on its contacts than on its joints.

And the things named rather than built

Every one of these is measured in this lesson, and none is a bug. Each is a known limitation of solving joints by sequential impulses, with a known answer the engine does not yet have:

  • Chains and mass ratios (above). The answers are sub-stepping, which this section measured, and a direct solve over a tree of joints — Baraff's linear-time method, or Featherstone's reduced coordinates — which is exact for a ragdoll's tree in O(n) and is what several production engines use for articulated characters.
  • The limit–pin coupling (§10). A door bounces off its stop at 7.5% of its speed at eight sweeps. The fix at any sweep count is to solve the active limit and the pin as one 4×4 block, trying the limit both active and inactive — a block with one clamp, the easiest possible case of the LCP 8.10 said nobody ships for contacts.
  • Velocity-level dissipation (§5, §8). ρ(ωh)² per step: a 90° pendulum keeps 75.4% of its energy per period. Sub-stepping cuts it by the square of the sub-step count per step and linearly per second; a per-joint choice of correction, which is §18's second exercise, is the cheaper partial answer.
  • No swing or twist limits on a ball-socket. A shoulder needs a cone that limits how far the arm swings and a separate limit on how far it twists. That is 8.12's first job, and it will be built from the same one-sided angular row this lesson's hinge limit is.

17. Common pitfalls & debugging

A joint pulls the bodies apart, or does nothing at all.
Symptom: a rod that lengthens under load, or a rope that never catches; or a limit that pushes the wrong way and flings the door.
Cause: a sign in the Jacobian. J·V must be the rate at which C grows, with C ≥ 0 the satisfied side of a one-sided row. Flip it and an equality row still converges — the equation is the same — but a one-sided row's clamp is on the wrong side and it acts only when it should not.
Fix: check each row against a finite difference once: move the bodies by a small velocity for a tiny step and confirm ΔC ≈ h·J·V. §3's second control is what the wrong sign does to contacts.
A chain or a ragdoll jitters and then explodes.
Symptom: a chain that is fine in isolation shakes violently once it is in the same scene as the collision pipeline.
Cause: the two bodies a joint connects are also colliding. Adjacent links overlap at the joint by construction, and the contact solver pushes them apart every step while the joint pulls them together.
Fix: collision_filter — exclude every jointed pair in the caller's narrow phase, unless the joint sets collide_connected.
A door with restitution zero bounces off its stop.
Symptom: a hinged arm hits its limit and comes back off it at a few percent of the speed it arrived at; a turnstile hinged through its centre does not.
Cause: §10. The limit row converges against the pin at ρ = m·d²/(I_cm + m·d²) per sweep — 3/4 for a door — so eight sweeps leave 9.8% of the stop undone, and the warm start returns it as a bounce.
Fix: compute lever_ratio for the body and choose sweeps from ρⁿ; or solve the limit and pin as one block.
Pendulums and swinging lamps die away too quickly.
Symptom: a large swing loses a quarter of its energy per period; a small one barely any.
Cause: §5. A velocity-level joint dissipates ρ(ωh)² of its kinetic energy per step, so faster swings lose disproportionately more.
Fix: sub-steps (the loss per second is first order in the step), or Baumgarte for joints if you can guarantee they are never pulled far apart. Diagnose by halving the step: if the loss per second halves, it is this.
A limb twitches forever on a body that should be lying still.
Symptom: a ragdoll at rest has one part slowly spinning or rocking.
Cause: Baumgarte on a joint that was pulled apart — spawned in a pose the joints disagree with, or yanked by a heavy impact. The bias impulse off the centre of mass spins the body about the joint's free axes and nothing takes the energy back out. §6 measured 0.35 J.
Fix: split impulse, the default.
A chain hangs from a frozen link in mid-air after being hit.
Symptom: the struck link swings; everything above it stays exactly still.
Cause: the joints are not edges of the island graph, so each link slept as its own island and a sleeping body is an immovable body to its neighbours.
Fix: union joint edges in build_islands, by the same rule as contacts. The island count on two chains from one ceiling should be two.
A hinge limit fires at the wrong angle, or the angle jumps by a full turn.
Symptom: a door stops a few degrees early, or a wheel's limit trips as it passes half a turn.
Cause: the angle is measured from the wrong rest pose, or read from a quaternion in the w < 0 hemisphere — 7.4's double cover, which adds 2π.
Fix: record rest when the joint is made, and move the deviation to w ≥ 0 before atan2.
A hinge is solid until warm starting is on, then twists sideways now and then.
Symptom: an occasional kick perpendicular to the hinge axis.
Cause: the two perpendicular impulses cached as coordinates in a basis rebuilt from a world axis every step — 8.10 §4's bug. §9 counts 127 flips on a bridge.
Fix: cache a world vector, or choose the basis in the body's own frame. The engine does both.

18. Exercises

  1. Warm-up: how many sweeps does an arm need? A 0.1 × 1.2 × 0.1 m arm is hinged at one end. Compute ρ, then the number of sweeps for which a limit leaves less than 1% of its correction undone. Check it in the demo's second scene with Right.

    Hint

    I_cm about the hinge axis is m(L² + T²)/12, d = L/2, and you want ρⁿ < 0.01.

    Solution

    I_cm/m = (1.44 + 0.01)/12 = 0.12083 and d² = 0.36, so ρ = 0.36/0.48083 = 0.7487 — a hair under the 3/4 of a thin slab, because the arm's thickness adds to I_cm. Then n = ln 0.01 / ln 0.7487 = 15.9: sixteen sweeps. Note that the mass never appeared, and neither did the length on its own: ρ is a ratio of shapes.

  2. Let joints choose their correction. Add an override to joint_config so that joints can use Baumgarte while contacts use split impulse. Measure §8's 90° pendulum (target: 92.0% of its energy kept per period, against 75.4%) and §6's dislocated limb (0.35 J of spin). Then design a policy that gets the first without the second.

    Hint

    The energy Baumgarte injects is proportional to the error it corrects, and a healthy turning joint's error is δ/β — millimetres. A dislocated one's is centimetres.

    Solution sketch

    Apply the Baumgarte bias only while |C| is below a threshold of a few times the expected steady stretch, and use split impulse above it. The pendulum then keeps its Baumgarte energy balance and the limb is pulled home by a correction that throws its velocity away. The threshold is a length and so, like 8.10's slop, a scale decision.

  3. Engine challenge: the hoist. §3 found the row form 27% faster than 8.9's contact solve because it stores M⁻¹Jᵀ. Add the two angular halves — I_a⁻¹(r_a × n) and I_b⁻¹(r_b × n), and the same for both tangents — to contact_constraint, compute them in prepare_contacts, and use them in apply_impulse_pair. Target: 8.75 ns a point per sweep. Then run 8.10's harness and report how far its numbers moved.

    Hint

    The friction solve applies a vector impulse built from two tangents, so store the six vectors, not a matrix, and apply each tangent's share separately.

    Solution sketch

    The saving comes almost entirely from removing the two matrix-vector products from the apply; the memory grows by three vec3s per body side per point. 8.10's numbers move in their last printed digits and its conclusions do not — which is the evidence that the change is safe and the reason this lesson did not make it silently.

  4. Measure the order. The solver visits joints before contacts in every sweep, on an argument rather than a measurement (§13). Add a switch, and measure the bridge under five crates both ways: the deepest crate penetration and the worst hinge gap, at 4 and 8 sweeps.

    Solution sketch

    Expect each order to favour whichever constraint goes last: contacts-last gives shallower crates and larger hinge gaps, and the reverse. Which is right is a question about which error the player will notice — and the answer is the order that makes the most visible error the smallest one.

  5. Engine challenge: sub-stepping as a feature. §14 measured eight sub-steps of one sweep holding a 100:1 chain nineteen times tighter than one step of eight. Build it for a scene with contacts: decide which stages must run every sub-step and which can run once per frame, and measure the cost against the stretch.

    Hint

    The broadphase can run once with a margin wide enough for a frame's motion. The narrow phase is the question: re-running it is exact and expensive; reusing the frame's manifolds and updating only their separations along the normal is Box2D v3's answer.

    Solution sketch

    Integrate, prepare, one velocity sweep, a relaxation and the position update per sub-step; broadphase and narrow phase once per frame, with each contact's separation re-evaluated from the bodies' current poses. The chain and the bridge both hold far better than at eight sweeps for a similar cost, and a deep stack is the case to check, because a manifold computed once per frame is a manifold that can go stale inside it.

19. Recap & next

The engine has joints, and it has them without a second solver: a joint is a set of rows, a row is twelve numbers and two bounds, and the loop that stacked 8.10's crates visits them in the same sweeps, sorts them into the same islands and puts them to sleep by the same rule.

Getting there took one derivation and a lot of measurement. A constraint is a question about velocity: Ċ = J·V, the impulse is Jᵀλ because an ideal constraint does no work on what it allows, and the effective mass is 1/(J M⁻¹ Jᵀ) — which is 8.9's contact, checked to 1.9 × 10⁻⁷, and which runs 27% faster as a row than it did by hand. A joint is a choice of rows and bounds: a rod is a contact without its clamp, a rope is a contact turned inside out, a socket is three rows best solved as one exact block, a hinge adds two angular rows, a limit is a contact on an angle that can be speculative for free, and a motor is a clamp at a torque — which is what friction was all along.

And three things about solving joints at the velocity level that were measured rather than assumed. A joint drifts along a tangent, by a Pythagorean recurrence, and loses ρ(ωh)² of its energy each step. The position correction cuts both ways: Baumgarte conserves a turning joint's energy and invents energy in a dislocated one, and the default stays split impulse because damping is the failure a game survives. And one number, ρ = m·d²/(I_cm + m·d²), set a pendulum's period, a joint's dissipation, a motor's spin-up and a limit's convergence — the parallel-axis theorem from 8.3, turning up four lessons later as the organising fact of a solver.

Next: 8.12 — Ragdolls: Joints on a Skeleton. 7.6's skeleton becomes a tree of capsules joined by the sockets and hinges built here, with the two limits a shoulder needs and this lesson did not build — a swing cone and a twist range — and the hard part, which is handing a character from the animation system to the solver mid-motion and back. Everything this lesson measured arrives at once: a torso is ten times a hand, a ragdoll is a chain, and every limb is a body on a pin with its own ρ.

  • Erin Catto, “Iterative Dynamics with Temporal Coherence” (2005), and “Modeling and Solving Constraints” (GDC 2009). The Jacobian formulation of sequential impulses from the person who made it standard in games — the second is the clearest derivation of Jᵀλ and the effective mass there is, with the revolute joint worked through.
  • Erin Catto, “Soft Constraints” (GDC 2011) and “Solver2D” (2024). The first treats Baumgarte, the slop and joint softness as one idea; the second compares the solvers that replaced plain sequential impulses — including the soft step with sub-stepping that §14 measured the case for.
  • Miles Macklin et al., “Small Steps in Physics Simulation” (SCA 2019). The paper behind §14's second table: one iteration of many small steps against many iterations of one large step, with chains and mass ratios among the fixtures.
  • David Baraff, “Linear-Time Dynamics using Lagrange Multipliers” (SIGGRAPH 1996). The exact, O(n) solve for a tree of joints — what a ragdoll is — and the answer to §14's chains that sequential impulses cannot give.
  • Roy Featherstone, Rigid Body Dynamics Algorithms (2008). Reduced coordinates and the articulated-body algorithm: the other way to build joints, in which a hinge has one coordinate and cannot drift at all.
  • Andrew Witkin and David Baraff, Physically Based Modeling, SIGGRAPH course notes, the “Constrained Dynamics” chapter. The virtual-work argument of §2, done for forces rather than impulses, with the drift of §5 and its stabilisation discussed as a problem of the formulation.
  • The Jolt Physics source (Jorrit Rouwé), PointConstraintPart, HingeRotationConstraintPart and AngleConstraintPart. A production engine built from exactly this lesson's pieces — a 3×3 point block, a 2×2 hinge block, and scalar angular rows for limits and motors — and small enough to read.
  • Box2D's b2RevoluteJoint (v2.4). The speculative limit of §10 and the motor-limits-point order of §13, in two dimensions, in a few hundred lines.
  • J. M. Borwein and P. B. Borwein, Pi and the AGM (1987), chapter 1. Where §8's formula comes from: Gauss's arithmetic–geometric mean and its connection to the complete elliptic integral, and why it converges quadratically.
