Module 6 — Advanced Rendering · Lesson 6.12

HDR and Tonemapping

This engine does not have an HDR bug. It has been avoiding the question by construction, which is a different thing and worth seeing clearly before anything is built. Two choices have been holding the lid on: Lesson 6.2 set k_reference_irradiance to π precisely so a white surface renders at exactly 1.0, and the demo's roughness is 0.49 — which peaks at 0.8676, just under. Change the second number and the lid comes off at once: the same shading equation returns 11.59 at roughness 0.20 and 55,917 for a polished metal, and to_encoded stores every one of those as code 255. Not "a bit too bright" — the same code for all of them, with the highlight's entire shape replaced by a constant. Fixing it needs a buffer that can hold the values, an exposure that decides what maps to white, a curve that brings the range down, and — the part that is architecture rather than arithmetic — a second render pass.

1The problem: a lid, and what has been holding it on

Two lessons in this module left a note saying this one would arrive. It is worth reading them before reading any code, because between them they say exactly what is wrong and exactly why nobody has noticed.

The first is in light.hpp, beside the constant that sets a light's brightness:

…so it renders at exactly 1.0 when E_perp is pi. That is the physical meaning of "correctly exposed" for this engine, and it is the same convention a photographer's white card encodes. It is a choice, not a law — Lesson 6.12 replaces it with a real exposure control and a tonemapper, at which point lights get authored in lux and this constant becomes the default rather than the rule.

The second is forty lines further down, beside shade_encoded:

to_encoded clamps, which is where an over-bright result gets clipped to white… A specular highlight is the first thing in this engine that routinely exceeds 1.0, so the clamp stops being theoretical here: a bright highlight arrives at the screen as a flat white blob with its shape clipped off, which is exactly the information tonemapping exists to keep.

So why has nothing looked wrong?

Because of the first note. k_reference_irradiance = π was chosen so that a white Lambertian surface lit square-on renders at exactly 1.0 — and then the demo's materials came in at roughness 0.49, whose specular peak at the mirror angle is 0.8676.

The engine has been living just under the lid, on purpose, for six modules.

That is not a criticism of either decision. It is the observation that makes the rest of the lesson measurable: change one number and the situation changes completely, and we can say by how much.

The same problem wearing a suit: the units debt

Lesson 6.2 renamed intensity to irradiance and made a point of why: "intensity meant nothing: it was a number you turned up until the picture looked right. irradiance is a measurable physical quantity." It was right, and it was incomplete — because having made the field measurable, the engine could not then let anybody author a measurement into it.

Full daylight is about 120,000 lux. Put that number into directional_light::irradiance and the polished surface above returns 8,333,875, which is code 255, as is every other pixel in the scene.

You cannot author lights in physical units until something downstream decides what maps to white. That something is an exposure control, and its absence is why k_reference_irradiance had to exist.

2Building intuition: a camera, not a clamp

The real world has an enormous range of luminance in it — starlight to direct sun is something like twenty stops — and every device that has ever produced an image of it has had a much smaller range to work with. Film, paper, a phone screen: all of them narrow. So the problem is not new, and the solution is not new either.

A camera does exactly two things about it, and they are separate things:

  1. An exposure decides which part of the range you are interested in. Shutter speed, aperture, ISO — three dials that combine into one number, and what that number says is "this much light shall be white". Point a camera at a dim room and at a beach and you change the exposure, not the film.
  2. A response curve decides what happens to the light that is still too bright. Film does not clip; it rolls off, compressing the highlights into a shoulder so that a bright sky retains some structure rather than becoming a white hole.

Those are the two halves of this lesson, and keeping them separate is more than tidiness — they answer different questions, they are set by different people (an exposure is a shot decision, a curve is a look decision), and §3.5 shows that performing them in the wrong order destroys the ability of the first to do its job.

What "clamping" actually is

It is worth naming the status quo in the same vocabulary, because it makes the gap obvious: min(x, 1) is a response curve. A very bad one — it is the curve that maps everything above white to white — but it is a member of the same family, and treating it as one is how the lesson keeps it honest. That is why tonemap::clamp is a named operator in the code rather than the absence of one.

And one thing this is not about

"HDR" means two different things, and this lesson is only one of them

HDR rendering — this lesson — means computing and storing light values without an artificial ceiling, then mapping them down for a display. The display is an ordinary one; the pipeline is what changed.

HDR display — the label on a television — means the panel itself accepts a wider range and a different transfer function (PQ or HLG rather than sRGB), so the mapping down is to a bigger target. SDL exposes this: gpu_device's report carries an SDL_GPUSwapchainComposition, and Lesson 6.1 logged it.

The first is a prerequisite for the second, and only the first changes how you render. This lesson does the first and leaves the second as an exercise, because the interesting part of it — which is colour volume and metadata, not range — is a different subject.
