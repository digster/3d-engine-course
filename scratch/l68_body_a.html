Module 6 — Advanced Rendering · Lesson 6.8

Shadow Mapping: Bias, Acne, and PCF

Every light this engine has ever had is unoccluded. lambert() asks whether a surface faces the light and calls the answer illumination — a question about one surface's orientation and nothing else — so a floor in the open and the same floor under a table are shaded identically, and every object in every picture so far floats. The fix is a z-buffer rendered from the light, which the engine has owned since Lesson 3.1. What is genuinely hard is the comparison: the number stored in that buffer and the number tested against it describe the same point on the same surface and are computed differently. They disagree, always, and that disagreement has a name — shadow acne — and, it turns out, a magnitude you can derive on paper before you write a line.

1The Problem: Nothing Blocks Anything

Open any picture this course has produced since Lesson 3.6 and look at where the objects meet the ground. There is nothing there. The cube does not sit on the floor, it hovers above it; the torus in Lesson 6.7's demo could be one metre up or one centimetre and you cannot tell. That cue — the dark patch where a thing touches another thing — is the single strongest signal the human visual system uses for spatial arrangement, and we have never drawn one.

The reason is one line, and it has been the same line since Lesson 3.6:

\[ \text{lambert}(n, l) \;=\; \max(0,\; n \cdot l) \]

Read it as a question and it asks: does this surface face the light? That is a fact about one surface's orientation. It is not the question we want, which is: can this surface see the light? — a fact about the surface and every other object in the scene. Nothing in the shading equation has ever consulted the rest of the scene. It cannot. shade() receives an albedo, a normal, an eye direction and a light, and from those five arguments there is no possible way to discover that a wall is in the way.

@@FIG_PROBLEM@@

So the shading equation has to be given a sixth thing: a number saying how much of the light actually arrives. Where that number goes in the equation is not arbitrary and is worth settling now, because getting it wrong is a bug that looks like a material bug. Lesson 6.2 split the shading equation into three factors with three separate physical meanings:

\[ L_o \;=\; \underbrace{f_r}_{\text{what the surface does}} \cdot \underbrace{E_{\perp}\cos\theta}_{\text{what the light delivers}} \;+\; \underbrace{\text{albedo}\cdot L_a}_{\text{the ambient term}} \]

A shadow is a statement about whether the light arrives. It says nothing whatsoever about what the surface would do with the light if it did. So it multiplies the middle factor, beside the cosine — which is exactly where Lesson 6.2 put the cosine, and for the same reason.

And it must not multiply the ambient term

Ambient models light that has bounced off everything else in the room. An object standing between a surface and the sun does not stop the room from existing — indeed, the ambient term is precisely what a shadowed surface is left with. That is why lighting::ambient being a cool blue is what makes shadows read as blue rather than as holes.

Multiply the ambient by the shadow too and shadows render pure black, which is what a shadow looks like in vacuum and nowhere else. It is a one-character mistake and it is instantly recognisable once you have seen it.

2The Idea: A Z-Buffer, Pointed Somewhere Else

Here is the whole technique in one sentence, and the pleasure of it is that the engine already owns every part: render the scene from the light's point of view and keep only the depth.

Think about what that buffer contains. For every ray the light emits — one per texel — it holds the distance to the nearest surface along that ray. Which is to say: it is a record of exactly what the light can see. Anything further along a ray than the stored value is behind something, and behind something is what "in shadow" means.

And "the distance to the nearest surface along every ray from a viewpoint" is not a new algorithm. It is a z-buffer. Lesson 3.1 built one, Lesson 4.7 built the GPU's, and both have been computing this quantity every frame for three modules while we used it only to decide which fragment wins.

@@FIG_IDEA@@

So a frame gains a second pass:

  1. The depth pass. Rasterise the scene from the light. No colour, no shading, no textures — a surface's colour cannot change where the surface is. What comes out is a depth buffer, which we now call a shadow map.
  2. The scene pass. For every fragment, project its world position into the light's space, look up the stored depth for that direction, and compare. Further than stored means occluded.

On the CPU that first pass is collect_triangles followed by draw_triangles — Module 2's vertex stage and Module 3's rasterizer, unmodified, with a different camera. Not one line of the rasterizer changes. That is the cheapest claim in this lesson and also its biggest: a shadow map is not a new renderer, it is the renderer you already have, aimed somewhere else.

Two things this lesson deliberately does not do

One light. lighting has one key and this has one map. Two lights are two maps and two lookups: a linear cost and no new ideas.

One cascade. A single map stretched over a whole outdoor scene gives the near field far too few texels — §11 shows what that looks like. Splitting the camera's frustum into ranges and giving each its own map is cascaded shadow mapping, it is Lesson 6.9, and it is an extension of this file rather than a replacement for it.

3A Light With No Position

"Render from the light" needs a camera, and a camera needs an eye. Our light does not have one. directional_light carries a direction and an irradiance and nothing else, because that is what a directional light is: the sun, modelled as parallel rays arriving from infinitely far away. There is no point they converge on, so there is no place to put an eye and no field of view to ask for.

3.1 — What replaces the pyramid

A perspective frustum is a pyramid because rays converge on an eye. If the rays are parallel, the shape swept out is not a pyramid. It is a box.

@@FIG_ORTHO@@

And a box maps onto the clip cube by scale-and-offset alone — no divide, no w trick, nothing. Lesson 2.10 spent a whole lesson deriving perspective because the projection is genuinely non-linear and a matrix is linear; the entire difficulty there was arranging for the divide to happen afterwards. Here there is no divide to arrange.

3.2 — Deriving it, one axis at a time

We want the box's left edge l to land at NDC −1 and its right edge r at +1. Any map that takes one interval to another affinely is the same four steps: subtract the start, divide by the width, scale to the new width, add the new start.

\[ x_{ndc} \;=\; 2\,\frac{x - l}{r - l} - 1 \;=\; \left(\frac{2}{r-l}\right) x \;-\; \frac{r+l}{r-l} \]

In prose: multiply x by 2/(r-l) and add -(r+l)/(r-l). A multiply and an add — which fits in a matrix row with room to spare.

y is the same sentence with b and t. z is the one that carries this course's conventions: view space looks down −z (Conventions §5), the near plane sits at z = −near and must land at device depth 0, and the far plane at z = −far must land at 1 — SDL_GPU's depth range, not OpenGL's (Conventions §4).

\[ z_{ndc} \;=\; \frac{-z - \text{near}}{\text{far} - \text{near}} \;=\; \left(\frac{-1}{\text{far}-\text{near}}\right) z \;-\; \frac{\text{near}}{\text{far}-\text{near}} \]

Assembled, with each brace group a column in our column-major storage (Lesson 2.6):

\[ P_{ortho} = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{2}{r-l} & 0 & 0 & -\frac{r+l}{r-l} \\[2pt] 0 & \frac{2}{t-b} & 0 & -\frac{t+b}{t-b} \\[2pt] 0 & 0 & \frac{-1}{f-n} & \frac{-n}{f-n} \\[2pt] 0 & 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \]

Look at the bottom row. Perspective's bottom row is (0, 0, -1, 0), and that −1 is what copies −z into w and makes the divide happen. Here the bottom row is (0, 0, 0, 1), so w comes out exactly 1, and the perspective divide that follows is the identity.

3.3 — A worked example

Take a ten-unit-wide box: l = −5, r = 5, b = −5, t = 5, near = 1, far = 21. Then 2/(r-l) = 0.2, (r+l)/(r-l) = 0, the depth scale is −1/20 and the depth offset is −1/20.

Push the view-space point (2.5, 0, −6) through by hand:

Push the same point through perspective(50°, 1, 1, 21) and the depth comes out 0.792: three quarters of the depth range consumed by the first quarter of the distance. That crowding is Lesson 2.10's, and Lesson 4.9 measured it on real hardware.

A prediction this lesson's plan got wrong, corrected

The plan for this lesson said the projection's non-linear depth distribution is why shadow error grows with distance. That is true of perspective and it is exactly not true here. An orthographic projection spreads device depth evenly over the box, so one depth code is worth the same number of metres at the near plane and at the far one — verify_68 §A checks that the two are equal to six decimal places, where under perspective the ratio is over 100×.

The consequence is that the quantisation half of §4's bias is a constant across the whole map, which makes a directional shadow map the easy case. The non-linear story returns in full the day this light becomes a spot light and its projection becomes a perspective one. Naming that now is cheaper than discovering it in Lesson 6.9.

3.4 — Fitting the box, and a negative near plane

A box has to be fitted to something, and the something is the scene's world-space bounds. The procedure is four steps and each one has a decision in it:

  1. Pick the light's axes. direction is the direction of travel (Lesson 3.6's convention, stated in capitals in light.hpp because it is the commonest sign error in graphics), so it is the light camera's forward axis, with no negation. The up hint only has to be non-parallel to it.
  2. Put the eye at the scene's centre. This sounds wrong and is exactly right: a directional light has no position, so any point on the axis gives the same rays.
  3. Transform the scene's AABB into light view space and read the extents straight off it.
  4. Square the box in x and y. Every formula in §4 says "one texel" as though a texel had a single size; squaring is what earns that sentence. It costs resolution on the narrow axis and buys one honest number, world_per_texel.

Step 2 has a consequence worth stopping on. If the eye is in the middle of the scene, half the scene is behind it — at positive view-space z — so the near plane comes out negative. perspective could not accept that for a moment: a negative near plane means dividing by a quantity that changes sign, and everything explodes. orthographic does not care, because it has no divide. verify_68 §B measures the scene reaching z = +2.608 in light view space and every one of the eight corners still landing inside the clip cube.

Two numbers fall out of the fit and the whole of §4 is built from them:

\[ \text{world\_per\_texel} = \frac{\text{box side}}{\text{resolution}}, \qquad \text{depth\_range} = \text{far} - \text{near} \]

The first is the map's resolving power in metres. The second is how many metres one unit of device depth is worth — and because the projection is orthographic, it is a single number that is valid everywhere in the box.
