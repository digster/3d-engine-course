1The Problem

Lesson 4.6 gave the camera freedom, and freedom immediately exposed something that two lessons had been hiding. Orbit down towards the ring of tori until two of them overlap on screen, and whichever was drawn later wins — regardless of which is nearer.

That has been true since Lesson 4.4. It went unnoticed because Lesson 4.5 arranged its seven instances on a ring, at a scale and a camera angle where nothing overlapped, and Lesson 4.6 inherited that arrangement. This is worth naming as a general shape, because it will happen to you: a limitation you have designed around stops being visible, and starts being load-bearing. The scene was not built to hide the missing depth test — but it did, and the day the camera moved, two lessons' worth of quiet wrongness arrived at once.

The second thing is quieter still. The texture coordinate has been riding through the vertex buffer since Lesson 4.5, and its entire job so far has been to draw a diagnostic grid so we could tell it had arrived. Nothing has ever been painted.

So: a depth buffer and a texture. You wrote both, on the CPU, in Lessons 3.1 and 3.9 — and both of those lessons were written knowing this one was coming. Here is what depth_buffer.hpp said in Module 3, before any of Module 4 existed:

The hardware keeps them separate. SDL_GPU's SDL_BeginGPURenderPass takes an array of SDL_GPUColorTargetInfo and, as a separate parameter, a SDL_GPUDepthStencilTargetInfo* — which "may be NULL". […] Modelling that split now means Module 4 is a rename, not a redesign.

A promise made two modules early is a promise worth checking. Most of this lesson is that check — and it passes, which means the lesson can spend its length on the parts that are genuinely new: an image decoder, a sampler object, and the depth-precision question that nobody warns you about until a wall starts flickering at fifty metres.

2Building Intuition

2.1 Depth is an attachment, not a mode

On the CPU, turning depth testing on meant passing a depth_buffer* to draw_triangles instead of nullptr. On the GPU it is two separate decisions in two separate places, and keeping them straight is most of what goes wrong:

  • The render pass gets an attachment. A second image, the same size as the colour target, with its own load and store operations — cleared at the start of the pass exactly as the colour target is.
  • The pipeline gets three fields. Whether to test, whether to write, and what comparison to use. These have been sitting in SDL_GPUDepthStencilState, zero-filled, since Lesson 4.4.

Both are required and neither implies the other, which is exactly right: a shadow pass writes depth and no colour, and a transparent pass tests depth without writing it. Fuse them into one "depth on" switch and neither is expressible — which is the argument depth_buffer.hpp made in Lesson 3.1 for not putting the comparison inside the buffer class.

2.2 A sampler is an object

In Lesson 3.9, how to read a texture was an argument:

On the GPU it is an object, created once and bound alongside the texture. If that shape feels familiar, it should: it is the fourth time this module has made the same move. Render state became a pipeline object (4.4); the vertex layout became pipeline state (4.5); per-draw constants became a push (4.6); and now the rules for reading a texture become a sampler. Every one of them is a decision the hardware must specialise for, and a decision the hardware specialises for cannot be a parameter of the innermost loop.

@@FIG_SAMPLER@@

The separation into two objects is worth a moment, because neither OpenGL nor our CPU path modelled it that way and it is better than both. A texture is the pixels; a sampler is the rules. Keeping them apart means one image can be read three ways in a single frame by binding it with three samplers, and one sampler serves every texture in a material system without being duplicated per image. They meet only at the binding — SDL_GPUTextureSamplerBinding, t0 paired with s0.

2.3 The one thing we do not write

This course has hand-rolled the maths library, the rasterizer, the OBJ parser, and will hand-roll the ECS, the allocators and the collision system. It is about to use somebody else's PNG decoder, and the reason is not difficulty.

A baseline PNG decoder is an afternoon. PNG in the wild is DEFLATE, plus five per-scanline filter modes, plus Adam7 interlacing, plus sixteen-bit channels, plus palettes, plus tRNS transparency, plus gamma and colour-profile chunks. A decoder that handles the files you happened to test is worse than no decoder at all, because it fails on a user's asset rather than on yours. And crucially: there is nothing about game engines in the fifth filter mode.

Compare with parse_obj, which we did write. OBJ is a line-based text format whose difficulty is entirely in the thing this course is about — the mismatch between how a file describes a vertex and how hardware fetches one. That is the test: is the hard part the subject? For OBJ, yes. For PNG, no.

The approved list, and why it is a list

The master prompt names five third-party libraries and no others: stb_image, stb_truetype, Dear ImGui (tooling only), cgltf, and SDL_shadercross. stb_image enters here; the rest arrive when their problem does. Each carries the same obligation this section just discharged — say out loud why you are not writing it — because a dependency added without that sentence is a dependency nobody can later argue with.

3.1 The depth test, as three fields

That is the same reason Lesson 3.1's software test was a < and its cleared buffer was full of 1.0.

enable_depth_write is documented as ignored while the test is off, so the two travel together — but they are genuinely independent when the test is on, and the pair that matters is test without write: that is how a transparent pass sorts itself against opaque geometry without occluding other transparent surfaces.

3.2 Why precision falls off as the square of distance

Now the part that is not a port. A depth buffer stores a number per pixel, and that number is not the distance — it is the distance run through Lesson 2.10's projection. That distinction is the entire subject of this section, and it has a cost you can compute.

Start from the projection matrix on the Conventions page. For a view-space point at depth z, the third and fourth clip components are zclip = A(z + n) and w = −z, with A = f/(n−f). Write d = −z for the positive distance in front of the eye, and the perspective divide gives:

\[ z_{\text{ndc}} = \frac{A(n - d)}{d} = \frac{f}{f-n}\left(1 - \frac{n}{d}\right) \]

In words: depth in the buffer is not proportional to distance — it is proportional to one minus near-over-distance, which is a hyperbola.

Check it at the two ends. At d = n the bracket is 1 − 1 = 0, so z = 0. At d = f the bracket is 1 − n/f = (f−n)/f, which cancels the leading factor exactly and gives 1. Near maps to 0 and far maps to 1, as SDL_GPU requires.

Now differentiate, because what we want to know is not where a surface lands but how far it must move before the buffer notices:

\[ \frac{dz}{dd} = \frac{f}{f-n} \cdot \frac{n}{d^{2}} \]

The rate at which depth changes with distance is proportional to one over distance squared. Double the distance and you get a quarter of the resolution.

@@FIG_DEPTH_CURVE@@

Worked example, with our own frustum. Near 0.3, far 100. At one metre:

\[ z_{\text{ndc}}(1) = \frac{100}{99.7}\left(1 - \frac{0.3}{1}\right) = 1.00301 \times 0.7 = 0.7021 \]

Seventy per cent of the entire depth range is spent in the first metre. The remaining ninety-nine metres share the other thirty per cent. That single number is the whole of why depth buffers behave the way they do, and it follows from nothing more exotic than the perspective divide having a 1/d in it.

Turn that into the number an engine actually needs. If the format stores N evenly spaced codes across [0, 1], then one code is 1/N, and the smallest world-space separation that spans one code is:

\[ \Delta d = \frac{1}{N} \cdot \frac{dd}{dz} = \frac{(f-n)\,d^{2}}{f\,n\,N} \]

For D16 (N = 65,535) at ninety metres that is 0.41 metres. Two walls forty centimetres apart, ninety metres away, occupy the same depth code. Whichever is drawn second wins, per pixel, and as the camera moves the winner changes — which is z-fighting, with a number attached to it at last.

The measurement tracks the prediction to within a few per cent across two orders of magnitude — 0.050 mm measured against 0.051 predicted at one metre, 412 mm against 411 at ninety. Theory and hardware agreeing that closely is worth pausing on: it means the model in §3.2 is not an approximation of what the hardware does, it is what the hardware does.

D24_UNORM does not exist here

Look at the support table in the figure. D24_UNORM — the format a desktop renderer would have hard-coded — is not available on this device. SDL guarantees exactly one depth format, D16_UNORM, and everything else is a query. This is the same discipline Lesson 4.2 established for the swapchain format and the present modes, arriving in the one place where getting it wrong is not a missing feature but a failure to create a texture at startup.

3.4 Reversed-Z

§3.2's derivation has a consequence that looks like a trick and is really just arithmetic.

A _UNORM depth format spreads its codes evenly across [0, 1]. A float does not: floating point stores its precision near zero, with roughly as many representable values between 0.001 and 0.002 as between 0.5 and 1.0. So a D32_FLOAT depth buffer has enormous precision near z = 0 and comparatively little near z = 1.

And where does the ordinary mapping put the far plane? At z = 1 — the coarse end. Which is precisely where §3.2 already told us 1/d² has thrown the resolution away. The two effects compound instead of cancelling.

So reverse it: map near to 1 and far to 0. Now the float's fine spacing near zero lands at the far plane, where the projection is at its coarsest, and the two effects very nearly cancel instead. Three changes and no new concepts — one subtraction in the vertex shader, COMPAREOP_GREATER instead of LESS, and clearing the buffer to 0 instead of 1.

@@FIG_REVERSED@@

180× at ninety metres, from 2.2 mm to 0.012 mm. And note the control: D16_UNORM gains nothing at all — 412.4 mm either way — because its codes are evenly spaced and reversing which end is which moves none of them. That the measurement shows a large effect on exactly the format the theory predicts and no effect on the format the theory predicts nothing for is what makes it believable.

Why this engine does not use it yet

Reversed-Z is standard practice in production renderers and this engine does not use it, which deserves a reason rather than an omission. It touches the projection matrix, the compare op, the clear value, and every later pass that reads depth — so it is a change to make once, deliberately, with the shadow maps and depth-based post-processing of Module 6 in view, rather than now for a scene whose far plane is a hundred metres and whose deepest object is at nine. Exercise 4.7.4 does it properly.

3.5 Which way up, and the one flag that matters

Two questions about an image, and they can both be answered with numbers rather than by squinting at a screenshot.

Which way up? Lesson 3.9 established that Wavefront OBJ counts v upwards from the bottom while every GPU texture counts it downwards from the top, and applied the flip at import. That was reasoned from documentation, because no image existed to test it against. assets/uv_grid.png now exists, and it carries a differently-coloured block in each corner precisely so that a program can read the four corners back and say which way up the picture arrived.

And what does _SRGB do? Lesson 3.9 spent a section on the fact that an albedo is a reflectance, a reflectance multiplies a quantity of light, and both sides of that multiply must be in linear space. It measured what skipping the decode costs: two texels blended in encoded space give 0.2139 where 0.5 is correct — 43% of the light. On the GPU that decode is one enum.

The _UNORM column matches the file byte for byte, in every corner. That is a stronger statement than "it looked right": the decoder, the upload, the sampler and the readback all agree about which way v runs and which channel is which, and any one of them being wrong would have shown here.

The _SRGB column is darker, and by the right amount: 222 becomes 186, and sRGB-decoding 222/255 = 0.871 gives 0.7305, which is 186.3 out of 255. The sampler did Lesson 3.9's per-texel arithmetic for free — and, crucially, before the filter rather than after, which is the ordering the software path had to construct by hand and the whole reason _SRGB texture formats exist.
