Flat, Gouraud and Per-Pixel Shading

Lesson 3.7 ended with three tables showing a highlight that flickers, vanishes and arrives in the wrong shape. None of them was a bug in the shading equation. They were all the same bug: evaluating it in the wrong places.

1The Problem

Lesson 3.7 finished with three tables, and it is worth restating what they showed because this lesson is the answer to all three.

  • Evaluating the shading at vertices and interpolating the answers disagreed with evaluating it at the pixel by up to 0.722 of full strength, over 87.8% of the lit area of a 1,225-vertex torus.
  • A coarser mesh did not merely distort the highlight, it missed it: an 8×6 torus found 0.0% of the true peak.
  • Spinning a 12×8 torus, the highlight was absent entirely in 58 of 180 frames.

Not one of those is a defect in Lambert's law, in the halfway vector, or in the exponent. The equation was right every time. What was wrong was where we asked it.

The failure mode

A highlight that flickers on and off as an object turns, and a curved surface whose shading is subtly faceted — flat-looking bands meeting at visible creases even though the mesh has smooth normals and the numbers across each triangle are continuous.

Both come from the same cause and neither is fixable by improving the lighting model. You can implement a full microfacet BRDF and it will still flicker, because the flicker is a sampling artifact: a sharp function evaluated at a few points and joined with straight lines.

There is a second thing tangled up in this, and untangling it is half the lesson. Lesson 3.6 shipped an enum called shade_mode with the values palette / flat / smooth, and its own doc comment admitted the problem:

"Note what is NOT in this enum: per-pixel. Interpolating the colour across a triangle and interpolating the normal and shading each pixel are different things, and comparing them properly is Lesson 3.8."

That enum could not be extended, and the reason is not that it needed one more value. It is that it was holding two independent questions in one list. Today it becomes two enums, and the grid they span is where the lesson lives.

2Building Intuition

2.1 One knob that was secretly two

Look at what flat and smooth actually differed by in Lesson 3.6. Read the code and you find two differences, moving together:

  • flat took the normal from the triangle (a cross product of its edges) and evaluated the shading once per triangle.
  • smooth took the normal from each vertex (as the mesh authored it) and evaluated the shading once per vertex.

Those are two knobs taped together. Nothing forces them to move as one — you can perfectly well take a normal per vertex and evaluate once per triangle, or take one normal for the whole triangle and evaluate per pixel. The two questions are:

The two axes

Where does the normal come from? A property of the mesh — of how it was authored and where its vertices were split. Lesson 3.5 showed this is decided by the file: a cube's 8 positions become 24 vertices because each corner belongs to three faces that disagree about the normal.

Where is the equation evaluated? A property of the pipeline — of your renderer, decided at run time and identical for every mesh you draw. Nothing about a mesh says how often to shade it.

Two independent questions with two and three answers make a grid of six, and Figure 1 is that grid with real pixel counts in it.

The shaded row is the interesting part and §3.1 derives it: with a face normal, all three evaluation points give the identical picture — zero pixels differ, not "close". That is a strong statement and it comes with a condition attached, which is exactly the kind of thing worth getting precise about.

2.2 Sampling a function you already have

Here is the whole lesson in one sentence: shading a surface is evaluating a function over it, and the three modes differ only in how many samples you take.

The function is fixed. It is shade(), unchanged since 3.7, taking a normal and a position and returning a colour. Flat calls it once per triangle. Gouraud calls it three times, at the corners, and lets the rasterizer's interpolation fill in between. Per-pixel calls it for every covered fragment.

@@FIG2@@

Put that way, two things become obvious that were not obvious before.

Interpolating the answer is not the same as interpolating the inputs. shade() is not a linear function — it contains a clamped cosine and, since 3.7, a cosine raised to the 32nd power. Averaging the outputs of a curved function at two points is not the same as evaluating it at the average of the points, and the gap between those is exactly the chord-under-a-curve error this course has met three times already (2.4's sub-pixel bias, 3.2's affine texturing, 3.7's per-vertex specular).

The error grows as the function gets sharper. A chord approximates a gentle curve well and a sharp one badly, which is why 3.7's Table 5 got worse as shininess went up — 0.048 at exponent 4 and 0.722 at 128. Diffuse shading survives per-vertex evaluation because Lambert is a broad, gentle cosine. A highlight does not, because being sharp is the entire point of it.

3The Theory

3.1 The grid, and its degenerate cells

Six cells, but not six pictures. Work out which coincide, because doing so is the test of whether the two axes are really separate in your head.

Take the face-normal row. The normal is the same at every point of the triangle — that is what "face normal" means. Now ask what the shading equation depends on:

\[ \text{shade}\bigl(\text{albedo},\ \mathbf{n},\ \mathbf{e} - \mathbf{p}\bigr) \] shade(albedo, normal, eye − position)

The albedo is constant over the triangle. The normal is constant, by assumption. That leaves the position — and with a diffuse-only model the position does not appear in the answer at all, because Lambert compares two directions and a directional light's direction is the same everywhere (Lesson 3.6). So the function is constant across the triangle, and it makes not the slightest difference whether you sample it once, three times, or ten thousand times. Interpolating equal values returns that value.

Three cells collapse into one. And the bottom row does not: with vertex normals the function genuinely varies across the triangle, and where you sample it changes 5,285 and 4,344 pixels respectively.

…and the condition, which the first draft of this lesson got wrong

The argument above has a load-bearing clause in it: with a diffuse-only model the position does not appear. Lesson 3.7 is precisely where that stopped being true. A specular term needs eye − position, and the position varies across a face even when the normal does not.

So with the highlight on, the top row separates too:

CellDiffuse onlyWith Blinn-Phong, p = 32
face × flat vs per-pixel0 px1,231 px (worst 6/255)

I wrote is_degenerate the first time claiming face × gouraud was always degenerate. It is not, and verify_38 §A found the 761 pixels that say so. The corrected rule is simpler than the wrong one, which is usually the sign: with a face normal all three evaluation points agree, for exactly as long as the shading is view-independent.

3.2 Gouraud, named

You have been doing Gouraud shading since Lesson 3.6. Nothing in this section is new machinery; it is a name for something already built, and knowing that is worth more than the name.

Henri Gouraud's 1971 contribution was one idea: instead of shading a polygon uniformly, compute the intensity at each vertex and interpolate it linearly across the interior. That is:

  • Lesson 2.4 built the interpolation — barycentric weights carrying an arbitrary attribute across a triangle, in linear light.
  • Lesson 3.6 put a lit colour at each vertex.

Put those two together and you have Gouraud shading, complete, without ever having typed the word. That is a pleasant thing to discover and it is also a useful habit: a great deal of graphics vocabulary names combinations of things you can derive, and a named technique you can reconstruct is much harder to forget than one you memorised.

Why it mattered so much in 1971

Gouraud shading is the reason real-time 3-D looked the way it did for twenty-five years. Its cost is per vertex, and vertices are far fewer than pixels — so on hardware where a multiply was expensive it made curved surfaces affordable when per-pixel lighting was simply out of reach. Every PlayStation-era character is Gouraud-shaded.

§3.7 measures whether that trade still holds. The answer is "sometimes", and the boundary is not where you would guess.

3.3 Phong shading is not the Phong model

This is a naming collision that trips up nearly everyone, and it is worth ten seconds now to save an hour later. Bui Tuong Phong published two different things, in the same 1975 paper, and they sit on the two different axes of this lesson.

Which means the sentence describing what this engine now does is, correctly and confusingly: Phong shading with the Blinn-Phong reflection model. That is the standard modern combination and it sounds like a contradiction until the axes are separated, which is exactly why they are worth separating.

3.4 The interpolated normal is too short

Per-pixel shading interpolates the normal. Interpolation is a weighted average, and here is a fact about averaging unit vectors that catches people: the average of two unit vectors is not a unit vector.

The picture makes it obvious. Two unit vectors have their tips on a circle of radius 1. The straight line between those tips is a chord, and a chord passes inside the circle. Every interpolated value lies on that chord, so every one of them is short.

@@FIG3@@

How short is exact and worth deriving, because it is one line. Two unit vectors θ apart, averaged: by symmetry the result lies along the bisector, and its length is the component of either vector along that bisector — which is cos(θ/2).

\[ \left\|\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\mathbf{n}_0 + \mathbf{n}_1\right)\right\| = \cos\!\left(\tfrac{\theta}{2}\right) \] the length of the average of two unit normals theta apart is cos(theta/2)

Small, but note the shape of it: zero at the corners and largest in the middle, which is the signature this course has learned to recognise (3.2 §3.6) and which makes it invisible to anyone checking vertex values.

A debt that was already paid

This engine never suffers it, and not by luck. Lesson 2.4's header booked the debt — "returns an interpolated normal that is no longer unit length (renormalise per pixel, 3.6)" — and Lesson 3.6 paid it in advance by having shade() normalise its own argument, with the reasoning stated at the time: "a caller who forgets gets a brightness scaled by the normal's length, which looks like a lighting bug and is not one."

Today is the call site that cashes that in. The cost is one square root per fragment, and it is in §3.7's measurements. Writing a function that cannot be misused two lessons before anyone could misuse it is the cheapest engineering there is.

3.5 Mach bands, and why precision cannot fix them

Gouraud shading has a characteristic artifact, and it is a strange one: the numbers are continuous and the picture is not.

Consider a curved surface approximated by flat facets, with smooth normals at the shared vertices. Gouraud evaluates the intensity at each vertex and joins those samples with straight lines. The result is piecewise linear: continuous everywhere — adjacent facets agree exactly at the vertex they share — but with a kink at every join, where the slope changes abruptly.

@@FIG4@@

Now the strange part. Your visual system does not measure absolute brightness; it enhances edges, through a mechanism called lateral inhibition in which an excited receptor suppresses its neighbours. One consequence is that a discontinuity in the gradient of brightness is exaggerated into an apparent light or dark stripe that is not in the image at all. Ernst Mach described these in the 1860s and they are named after him.

That is why this artifact is worth its own section rather than a footnote:

  • More colour precision does not help. The bands are not quantisation. Go to 16 bits per channel and they are exactly as visible, because the piecewise-linear ramp is exactly as kinked.
  • More triangles help slowly. Doubling the tessellation halves the kink at each join but doubles the number of joins. It converges — this is the same O(h²) chord error as 3.2's affine texturing — but never terminates.
  • Per-pixel shading removes them, because it evaluates the true curve instead of a chord, and the true curve has no kinks.
Honest scope

Two caveats, because this is the kind of claim that gets overstated. First: Mach banding is a perceptual effect, so how visible it is depends on the viewer, the display and the contrast — you cannot count it in a framebuffer diff the way this course counts everything else, and the pixel counts in §3.1 measure the underlying numerical difference, not the perceived one. Second: per-pixel shading removes the banding caused by interpolating the shading; it does nothing about a genuine silhouette crease, because that one really is in the geometry.

3.6 What per-pixel shading cannot fix

Lesson 3.7 measured a highlight vanishing entirely as an object turned, and this lesson was supposed to fix it. Re-run on the same four meshes, counting frames of a 180-step rotation in which there is no highlight at all:

That second result is the more useful one, and it was not what I expected when I wrote the plan for this lesson. Per-pixel shading fixes an interpolation error. It cannot invent a normal the mesh does not have.

A cube has six distinct normals. Whether a highlight exists anywhere on it is decided before any shading happens, by whether one of those six points near the halfway vector — and if none does, evaluating the equation at ten thousand fragments instead of twenty-four vertices changes nothing, because all ten thousand get the same normal. The icosahedron is the same story with twenty.

So the two axes fix two different things, and neither substitutes for the other:

Which axis fixes what

The normal source decides whether the surface has smoothly varying normals at all — a property of the mesh, fixed by welding vertices and averaging (Lesson 3.5) or by authoring them.

The evaluation point decides how faithfully those normals are turned into pixels — a property of the pipeline.

Get the first wrong and no pipeline saves you. Get the second wrong and no mesh saves you. That is a much better reason for two enums than tidiness.

It also re-confirms Lesson 3.6's result from the other direction. On cube.obj, face normals and vertex normals give 0 differing pixels — still — because 3.5's vertex split already gave each of the cube's 24 vertices its own face's normal. On the welded torus the same comparison gives 4,122. A faceted mesh is faceted because of the split, not the shading model, and that sentence survives this lesson intact.

3.7 What it costs, measured

"Per-vertex is cheaper than per-pixel" is one of those things everyone knows. It is also, at the sizes this engine actually renders, false — and finding that out took one sweep.

The reasoning behind the folklore is sound as far as it goes: a mesh has fewer vertices than the pixels it covers, so shading per vertex is fewer calls. The unstated assumption is that a triangle covers many pixels. Our torus has 2,304 triangles and covers 6,346 pixels at 320×180 — 2.8 pixels per triangle, which means 6,912 vertex-shading calls against 6,346 fragment ones. The folklore inverts.

The asymptote is the real number. 2.15× is what per-pixel shading costs when triangles are large — a square root, a few dot products and a powf per fragment instead of per vertex. That is the honest headline, and it is a very affordable price for removing the artifacts in §3.1 and §3.5.

The crossover is real and modern meshes live near it. A character model with 50,000 triangles occupying a quarter of a 1080p screen averages about ten pixels per triangle — the second row. This is not a curiosity; it is why GPUs shade in 2×2 quads and why "too many small triangles" is a recognised performance problem with a name.

The measurement corrected the guess. I expected a number bigger than one on every row. Half of the value of writing a harness is the rows that come back wrong.
