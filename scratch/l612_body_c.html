4.6   The fill's sixth nullable pointer

fill_style::hdr is a hdr_buffer*, and null is the whole of "this fill is LDR" — the sixth field on that struct to make the bargain, after lights, albedo, normal_map, shadows and cascades. Every fill written in the previous sixty-six lessons passes null and behaves identically.

The framebuffer is still required, and not as a formality: it supplies the dimensions the bounding box is clipped against. That is exactly the situation depth_only documented in 6.8 — "the colour target still has to EXIST, because fill_triangle clips its bounding box against the framebuffer's dimensions and there is nowhere else for a pass to learn how big it is" — arriving a second time, which is a good sign that it is a real constraint rather than an accident.

In the write path it is one branch on a per-triangle constant:

if (depth_only) { /* nothing to store */ }
else if (hdr_row != nullptr) { hdr_row[x] = fragment_linear(f0, f1, f2, ignored); }
else                         { row[x]     = fragment(f0, f1, f2, ignored); }

And in the blend path, the payoff §3.5 described — no decode, no re-encode, just the operator: 

if (hdr_row != nullptr)
{
    hdr_row[x] = (style.src_storage == alpha_storage::premultiplied)
               ? over_premultiplied(lin, a, hdr_row[x])
               : over(lin, a, hdr_row[x]);
    return;
}

Note in passing that 6.11's blend_encoded failure has nowhere to live on this path. There are no stored codes to lerp. A knob that cannot express its mistake here is not being silently ignored — the mistake genuinely does not exist, which is a stronger statement than the engine has usually been able to make about one of its deliberate wrong answers.

--polish forces every material to a roughness the file did not ask for, and adds a polished metal torus. The torus is not decoration: at roughness 0.15 the specular lobe is a few degrees wide, so a scene of flat faces presents the mirror angle at no pixel at all and the HDR buffer comes back with a peak of 0.22 — which is how a renderer with a real clipping problem can look completely fine. Expected result

On the console, the diagnostic that says whether you have an HDR problem at all:

  polish        : every material forced to roughness 0.15, plus a metal torus to catch the lobe
  hdr           : max channel 411.8161 (8.7 stops), max luminance 398.9317
  over the lid  : 126 of 129600 pixels (0.10%) have a channel above 1 — every one of them would be code 255 under a clamp

0.10% of the frame. A tenth of one percent — and all of the information being destroyed is in it. Note also the last line: the arithmetic mean is 6.2× the log-average on this frame, which is §3.6's argument arriving in the demo rather than in a test.

On screen, the torus carries a long specular highlight. Under --tonemap clamp its core is a flat white region with a hard edge: 126 pixels of identical code 255, with the lobe's shape gone. Switch to reinhard and the core becomes a gradient — the same pixels, now carrying the shape they always had. Only 3 pixels remain at 255 rather than 126.

aces is visibly darker overall (its toe, costing 13 codes at the dark end) with the tightest roll-off; white sits between them and reaches true white where Reinhard cannot. And --ev 2 darkens everything by a little over two stops, which is what an exposure does and what a curve does not.

7Common pitfalls & debugging

The HDR buffer's peak is 0.22 and you were expecting thousands

Symptom. Everything is wired up, the scene is full of polished metal, and measure() reports nothing above 1.

Cause. A sharp specular lobe is a few degrees wide, and a scene of flat faces may present the mirror angle at no pixel at all. The highlight is not being clipped; it is not being hit.

Fix. Put a doubly-curved surface in the scene — this demo adds a torus — so that some point satisfies the mirror condition from every viewpoint. This is worth recognising because it is how a renderer with a real clipping problem can look completely fine right up until the day somebody adds a sphere.

Sharper surfaces report LOWER peaks than rough ones

Symptom. A probe sweeps the eye through a range of angles and finds that roughness 0.05 peaks lower than roughness 0.35, which is backwards.

Cause. The sweep misses the lobe. A narrow lobe needs the mirror direction exactly, and a sweep through a plane that does not contain it reports a peak that is an artefact of where you looked.

Fix. Evaluate at reflect(−to_light, n) rather than sweeping. And keep the symptom in mind as a diagnostic: a monotonic relationship coming out backwards usually means the measurement is missing the thing it is measuring, not that the physics is surprising. The first version of probe_612.cpp made exactly this mistake and the reversal is what gave it away.

The tonemapped image is washed out and never reaches white

Symptom. Everything looks slightly grey; nothing in the frame is code 255, even where the input was enormous.

Cause. Plain Reinhard. x/(1+x) is strictly below 1, and reaching code 255 needs an input of 224.

Fix. reinhard_white with a white point you choose, or ACES. It is worth knowing this is a property of the operator rather than a bug in your exposure — turning the exposure up to chase white just clips everything else.

The scene renders black, or the resolve samples garbage

Symptom. The HDR pass looks right in a capture and the resolve produces noise, black, or last frame's contents.

Cause. The HDR texture was created with COLOR_TARGET and not SAMPLER. It is a legal texture that cannot legally be read.

Fix. Both usage flags. And note there is no error to look for: SDL's GPU validation of this lives inside if (device->debug_mode) (Lesson 6.10), so on a release device the result is simply undefined.

Everything is far too bright, or far too dark, by exactly the gamma curve

Symptom. The resolve produces a picture that is recognisable but wrong in the familiar way — washed out or muddy across the whole frame.

Cause. Two encodes, or none. The scene shader is still encoding (encode_output = 1) while writing a float target, or the resolve is not encoding into a UNORM swapchain.

Fix. Exactly one encode, at the end. A float target never carries codes, so the scene pass writes linear; the resolve encodes if and only if its own target is not _SRGB. If you are unsure which you have, gpu_device's report prints the swapchain format and output_encodes_in_hardware.

The auto-exposure flickers whenever something shiny comes into view

Symptom. The whole image dims for a frame or two as the camera turns.

Cause. An exposure derived from the arithmetic mean luminance. One pixel in ten thousand at 5000 contributes ten times the rest of the frame put together.

Fix. The log-average, which is what hdr_stats reports beside the arithmetic mean precisely so the two can be compared. The gap on a frame with one bright highlight is 11×.

A saturated colour still clips after tonemapping

Symptom. Blues and reds go flat in bright areas even though the tonemapper is working.

Cause. Luminance-only tonemapping. The curve was applied to the luminance and landed in range; an individual channel did not. Blue is worst, at a luminance weight of 0.0722 — a pure blue of (0, 0, 8) arrives at b = 5.07.

Fix. Per-channel, which is in range by construction — or keep luminance-only and add a desaturation step, which is what production pipelines do and what this course names without building.

8Exercises

8.1 — Find your own lid guided

Run the demo at four roughnesses and record max channel and the over-the-lid count at each. Predict, before running, roughly where the count starts climbing — then check it against §A's table of peaks.

Hint

The peak scales roughly as 1/α² at the mirror angle, where α = roughness² (6.3's Disney remap) — so halving the roughness should multiply the peak by about sixteen. The table goes 1.74, 11.59, 177.03, 2824: ratios of 6.7, 15.3, 16.0. The agreement gets better as the approximation's assumptions get truer, which is itself worth noticing.

8.2 — Reach white with Reinhard guided

Compute by hand the input that plain Reinhard needs to produce code 254 rather than 255, and check it. Then work out what white point reinhard_white needs so that the demo's measured peak of 411.82 maps to exactly 1, and run it.

Hint

For the first: code 254 needs an encoded 253.5/255, invert the sRGB transform, then solve x/(1+x) = L for x. For the second, the answer is --white 411.82 — and the picture it produces is worth looking at, because mapping the brightest pixel in the frame to exactly white is almost never what you want.

8.3 — Auto-exposure engine

Use hdr_stats::log_mean_luminance to choose the exposure each frame instead of taking it from the command line: ev100_from_luminance(log_mean * K) for a K you pick. Then make it adapt — move the EV toward its target by a fixed rate per second rather than jumping.

Solution sketch

The standard K is 12.5/100 giving EV = log2(L · 100/12.5). The adaptation is one line — ev += (target − ev) · (1 − exp(−rate · dt)) — and the exp form rather than a plain lerp is what makes the rate frame-rate independent, which is Lesson 1.4's fixed-timestep argument arriving somewhere unexpected. Real implementations adapt up and down at different rates, because eyes do.

8.4 — Prove the ordering claim on a picture guided

Add a --wrong-order flag that applies the curve before the exposure, render the polished scene at EV 2 both ways, and diff. Describe what is lost in words before looking at the numbers.

Hint

The prediction: the wrong order will be darker and will have less variation in the highlight, because the curve has already mapped everything above about 4 into a narrow band and the exposure then scales that band. Look specifically at the range of codes inside the highlight, not at the mean.

8.5 — A real HDR display engine

gpu_device's report carries an SDL_GPUSwapchainComposition. Find out which compositions your machine claims to support, and write the branch that would use one — without implementing the PQ transfer function, just the structure and the honest list of what else would have to change.

Hint

SDL_WindowSupportsGPUSwapchainComposition is the query. The interesting part of the answer is that the tonemap curve's target changes: you are no longer mapping to [0,1] for an sRGB display but to an absolute luminance in nits, which makes the white point a display property rather than an artistic one. Most of the rest of this lesson survives unchanged, which is the point of having separated exposure from curve from encode.

9Recap & next

The engine stopped avoiding a question it had been avoiding for six modules.

  • A float render target in both renderers — hdr_buffer on the CPU at 3× the memory, R16G16B16A16_FLOAT on the GPU at 2×, and the difference between those multipliers is entirely the half float.
  • An exposure in real photographic units, paying a debt light.hpp recorded by name — and locating the engine's previous behaviour at EV −0.263 rather than treating it as a special case.
  • Four curves, two of them derived rather than copied, with the three properties that make one legal stated and checked.
  • A full-screen resolve pass — the first thing in this engine that draws no geometry and whose cost scales with pixels — with no vertex buffer, no depth and no blending.

Three numbers to carry forward. The demo's polished scene has 0.10% of its pixels over the lid, and all of the destroyed information is in them. The arithmetic mean of a frame's luminance is 11× its log-average when one specular pixel is present, which is why metering happens in log space. And compositing on a float target is 8.1× cheaper than on 8 bits, because Lesson 6.11's decode-and-re-encode round trip is not a cost of blending — it was a cost of storing something other than light.

It is worth stating what did not change, too. The reference render is byte-identical for the twenty-first lesson, and for a structural reason rather than a flag: the fixture renders into an 8-bit framebuffer through the path it has always used, and everything here operates on a different buffer type. This is not "the tonemapper is optional" — it is "the tonemapper is a stage over a target the fixture does not have".

Next: 6.13 — Bloom and the Post-Processing Stack. This lesson built one post pass and deliberately did not build a stack, because a stack is an architecture question and one user is not enough evidence to answer it. Bloom supplies the second user, and it also supplies the reason the ordering matters: a bloom operates on the pre-tonemap image, because it is looking for exactly the values above 1 that this lesson finally lets the engine keep. Which means the first thing 6.13 has to decide is who owns the intermediate targets — and that is the same question a frame graph (6.17) answers at a larger scale.
