Module 6 — Advanced Rendering · Lesson 6.9

Cascaded Shadow Maps

Lesson 6.8 fitted one orthographic box around the whole scene and rendered one depth map into it. That box has to contain the far field, so the near field — the part you are actually looking at — gets whatever texels are left over. The fix is a change of question: the scene is not what needs resolving, the camera's frustum is, and a frustum is mostly far away. Split it by distance, fit a box around each slice, give each its own map. What makes this a lesson rather than a loop is that a camera-fitted box moves, and 6.8's never did — so this lesson is mostly about two artefacts that only exist once it does, and about auditing whether 6.8's bias was really a formula or a fudge with a good story.

1The problem: one box, and where its texels go

Lesson 6.8 ended with shadows in both renderers and a bias derived from the sampling geometry rather than tuned by hand. It also ended with a line in shadow.hpp naming what it had not done, and this is the one we are here to fix:

One cascade. A single map stretched over a whole outdoor scene gives the near field far too few texels. Splitting the camera frustum into ranges and giving each its own map is cascaded shadow mapping, and it is an extension of this file rather than a replacement for it.

Take that seriously as arithmetic. fit_directional squares a box around the whole scene, so a 40-metre scene at 1024 texels gives

world_per_texel = 40 m ÷ 1024 ≈ 0.039 m

— and it gives that everywhere. The rock forty metres away that covers nine pixels of your screen gets 39-millimetre texels. So does the crate two metres from your eye that fills half the frame. The crate needs perhaps a hundred times the density and gets exactly the same, because the box was fitted to the thing that does not matter.

You can see this without any new code. gltf_view takes a resolution, so starve it deliberately — §6 has the full command — and watch the shadow edge under a nearby object turn into a staircase while the distant one looks fine. That is not a bug in 6.8's fit. The fit is doing exactly what it was asked: contain the scene. The question was wrong.

What "resolution" means for a shadow, and why it is not the map's size

It is tempting to answer this by making the map bigger. Doubling a 1024 map to 2048 costs four times the memory and four times the depth-pass fill, and buys a factor of two in world_per_texel — which the crate will spend in the first metre it moves closer. The quantity that matters is not texels, it is texels per screen pixel, and that is a ratio between the map's density and the camera's — so it is a fact about where the box is at least as much as about how many texels are in it. Cascades attack the numerator's placement. Raising the resolution attacks the denominator with money.

2Building intuition: resolve what you are looking at

Here is the whole idea in one sentence, and it is worth reading twice because everything difficult in this lesson is a consequence of it rather than part of it:

Fit the light's box around a slice of the camera's frustum instead of around the scene, and use several slices.

A frustum is a wedge. Near the eye it is small — a few metres across — and far away it is enormous. If you cut it into four pieces by distance and fit a box around each piece separately, the first box is small and its map is dense, and the last box is huge and its map is coarse. Coarse is the right answer at forty metres: a texel out there already covers a fraction of a screen pixel, and spending more on it would be spending it where nobody is looking.

Notice what this costs before we go further, because it is not free and the lesson should not pretend otherwise. Four cascades means four depth passes, four times the map memory, and a shader that has to decide which map to read. The demo below reports 3.22 ms for four cascades against 2.49 ms for one, on the same scene — so roughly 30% more time for four times the passes, because the depth pass is dominated by fixed costs rather than by triangles at this scale. Your scene will differ; measure it.

The three things that only exist once the box moves

6.8's box was fitted to the scene. Walk the camera around and the scene does not move, so the box does not move, so the map's texel grid is nailed to the world and stays there. That is why 6.8 could get away without a single line about stability, and it said so: "no texel snapping — the fit below depends on the SCENE's bounds and not the camera's, so the grid does not move when the camera does."

A camera-fitted box moves every frame. Three things follow, and together they are the lesson:

  1. A box that resizes makes every texel resize. Fit a box tightly to the eight corners of a slice and then turn the camera on the spot: the corners rotate within the box, so the box grows and shrinks — measured below at 30.2% across a full turn. Since world_per_texel is the box side over the resolution, every texel in the map changes size while you merely looked around, and every shadow edge in the picture breathes. The cure is to fit a sphere, which has no orientation.
  2. A box that slides makes every edge crawl. Even with a fixed size, the box translates as you walk, so its texel grid slides continuously under stationary geometry and each shadow edge lands on a different texel every frame. The cure is to snap the box to its own grid, so it advances in whole-texel jumps and lands where it landed last frame.
  3. Two cascades meeting is a new artefact with a new name. They have different texel grids and different biases, so they disagree along their shared distance, and that disagreement draws a straight line across your picture. It is called the seam, and the usual cure is to blend a band either side.
The claim this lesson is really testing

Lesson 6.8 spent its longest section deriving a bias from the sampling geometry — the argument being that shadow acne is not a mystery but a sampling error with a computable magnitude, and every term in that derivation was a multiple of world_per_texel.

Cascades change world_per_texel, by a factor of 7.3 across four of them. So this lesson is an audit. If 6.8's derivation was real, cascades need no new bias code at all — only their own light_camera. If it was a fudge with a good story attached, this is where it falls over and starts needing a per-cascade constant somebody tuned. §3.4 reports which of those happened, and the answer is more interesting than either.
