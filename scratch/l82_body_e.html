14. Build & run

# Linux / macOS
cmake -S . -B build
cmake --build build -j

# Windows (MSVC)
cmake -S . -B build
cmake --build build --config Debug

The configure step prints the umbrella lint's tally, and it moved by one: 

-- engine: umbrella lists 90 of 91 public headers (1 documented exception)

…and for the first time in eight lessons it printed that without having refused first. The line in engine.hpp went in during the same edit as the header it names, by somebody who had just read 8.1's paragraph about missing it. That is worth recording precisely because it is not evidence the habit has taken: seven of the previous eight lessons missed this file, and the streak broke on the lesson immediately after the one that wrote a paragraph about breaking it.

Then the demo. No assets, no shaders, nothing loaded from disk — everything on screen it computes. 

# play with it
./build/demos/bodies

# headless and deterministic: 2.9 simulated seconds, then a screenshot
./build/demos/bodies --mode 2 --t 2.9 --shot scratch/bodies.ppm

# start in a particular resistance mode (0 none, 1 damping, 2 drag)
./build/demos/bodies --mode 1 --rate 240

What you should see. On startup, mode none: a single arc rising from the bottom-left, crossing the orange ground line and continuing down through it, drawn as a dashed line whose colour cycles amber → green → blue. That cycling is three bodies of 0.1, 1 and 10 kg in exact agreement; there is no fourth body and none has been lost. On the right, four shorter tracks fanning apart from a common point at the top, looping every 1.5 s.

Now press M. The picture barely changes: mode damping shortens the arc and the three colours still cycle along one line, because damping has no mass in it. Press M again and the three separate violently — the amber 0.1 kg body barely clears the launch point before dropping almost vertically, the blue 10 kg one carries nearly to the arc it had in vacuum. Two of the three pictures are identical and the third is the physics, which is §6 in two keypresses.

Then drive it. 1, 2 and 3 hide individual masses, which is the fastest way to satisfy yourself that the coincident line really is three. Up and Down change the simulation rate — and note what does not happen, which is that the trajectories do not move, because everything here is either exact or an impulse. F freezes the frame panel so you can read the ImGui table's gain and tilt columns against figure 8.

Then the harness, which is where every number in this page came from: 

cmake -S . -B build-rel -DCMAKE_BUILD_TYPE=Release
cmake --build build-rel --target engine
sh scratch/build_verify_82.sh

The script refuses to run against a build tree with an empty CMAKE_BUILD_TYPE, which the repository's default cmake -S . -B build produces. Lesson 7.6 measured what ignoring that costs at 18.1× on skinning, and here it would be worse than wrong: §11's findings are ratios between two arrangements of the same loop, and an unoptimised libengine.a makes every arrangement equally slow, which reads as “no difference” and is the exact opposite of the answer.

15. Common pitfalls & debugging

  1. Symptom: an object drifts away forever after being pushed once.

    Cause: the force accumulator is never cleared. A force is an input to a step, not a property of a body, and a thruster fired for one frame keeps firing for the rest of the level.

    Fix: clear it in step, after the integration. Diagnosis: a body's force should be zero every time you look at it from outside step. If it is growing, count how many systems += to it per frame and compare against the number of times it is zeroed — the answer is usually zero.

  2. Symptom: a debug overlay says a resting crate has no forces on it.

    Cause: §5. This engine adds gravity as an acceleration, after the division, so rigid_body::force genuinely does not contain the weight. The overlay is correct and is showing you something true that is not what you asked.

    Fix: draw the weight separately as mass_of(b) * world.gravity(). It is worth knowing which side of this trade your engine is on before you spend an hour looking for a missing add_force.

  3. Symptom: the jump height changed when you got a new monitor.

    Cause: §7. Something that should be an impulse was written as a force applied for one step, so the velocity it produces is F·h/m and the height goes as h².

    Fix: add_impulse. Diagnosis: run the same input at two rates and compare. A ratio near 4 between 30 and 60 Hz is this bug and nothing else, because nothing else in a fixed-step simulation scales as the square of the step.

  4. Symptom: a heavy object and a light one fall identically, and that is wrong for your game.

    Cause: it is right for gravity, and you want air. You have reached for damping, which has no mass in it.

    Fix: a drag force, through the accumulator: add_force(b, b.state.velocity * -b_coeff). Diagnosis: compare the two terminal speeds. If a 0.1 kg body and a 100 kg body settle at the same speed, you are damping.

  5. Symptom: a body under a particular scene node falls at the wrong rate — and only under that node.

    Cause: §9. Something is integrating in a parent's local space, and the parent is scaled.

    Fix: integrate in world space and convert at the boundary with place_in_parent. Diagnosis: inspect_frame(parent_world, gravity) and read gain. A gain of 2 is a parent scaled by 2; a nonzero out_of_square is shear, and a uniform of false with a gain of 1 is §9.2's trap — a frame that is broken along an axis this particular vector does not touch.

  6. Symptom: the whole game feels floaty, or like a toy, and no amount of tuning fixes it.

    Cause: §8. One unit is not one metre.

    Fix: measure it rather than tune it. Put a body at a known height, time the fall, and compare against free_fall_time(d, 9.81). The ratio is 1/√s, so square it and you have the scale error. Then decide whether to rescale the art or rescale g — and if you rescale g, write down every absolute length in the project that has to move with it, because the solver tolerances will not.

  7. Symptom: a “very heavy” floor sinks slowly out of the level.

    Cause: §4.5. Gravity does not care what anything weighs; a million kilograms falls exactly as fast as ten grams.

    Fix: make_fixed, or inv_mass = 0. Diagnosis: if raising a mass made a problem smaller rather than removing it, you have the wrong tool — mass resists pushes, and gravity is not a push in this sense.

  8. Symptom: a fast object passes through a wall.

    Cause: nothing in this lesson, and nothing in the next five either. It is tunnelling, and it will remain possible until 8.13.

    Fix: not yet. Diagnosis: step_report::max_travel against the thinnest wall in the level. A bullet at 400 m/s covers 6.67 m per step at 60 Hz, and the number being larger than the wall is the whole explanation.

  9. Symptom: a replay diverges, or two machines in a lockstep session disagree.

    Cause: could be many things, and two of them are in this lesson. §3.4: the order in which systems call add_force changes the sum's last bits, and can drop a small force entirely. §5.4: a round trip through the mass can differ by an ulp between two bodies that should be identical.

    Fix: for the first, a fixed system order — which our design has, because the accumulator is a field rather than a list. For the second, gravity as an acceleration, which is what the engine now does. Diagnosis: hash the whole body table after each step and bisect on the first step at which two runs differ.

16. Exercises

  1. 8.2.1 — Draw the weight. §12 names the cost of the acceleration route: a force overlay no longer shows the one force that is always present. Add a weight_of(const rigid_body&, vec3 gravity) helper and use it in demos/bodies' ImGui panel to show total force including weight. Then answer the design question it raises: should step_report carry the total weight it applied, so the tool does not have to recompute it?

    Hint: the helper is one line. The interesting half is that a report field costs every caller and a helper costs only the one that asks — and §11 measured what a report field costs at 27% of the step.

  2. 8.2.2 — Find the mass that breaks it. §5.3 found that 16% of masses fail the round trip and that the first one is 1.000145 kg. Write a short program that, for a given mass, prints (g/w)·w against g in hex float (%a), and use it to find the largest mass under 10 kg that fails. Then answer: is the set of failing masses dense, or does it have structure?

    Hint: print the failures in a narrow band and look at their spacing in ulps of the mass rather than in kilograms. The reciprocal is a monotone map, so the structure you are looking for is about where 1/m lands relative to the boundaries between exponents.

  3. 8.2.3 — A kinematic platform, and the crate it does not carry. Add a kinematic body to demos/bodies that oscillates horizontally, and a dynamic one resting on it — resting in the sense of being placed at the right height, since nothing collides yet. Watch the crate fail to be carried. Then write down precisely what information 8.9 will need in order to carry it, and check that rigid_body already stores it.

    Hint: the answer is the platform's velocity, which is exactly why a kinematic body has one and why teleporting it every frame instead is the bug this exercise is about. The relative velocity at a contact point is the quantity 8.9 solves against.

  4. 8.2.4 — Quadratic drag. Real aerodynamic drag goes as v²: F = −½ρCdA|v|v. Implement it as a force in the demo and measure the terminal speed against the closed form √(2mg / ρCdA). Then find out what it costs you: the linear model's exact solution made apply_drag possible, and the quadratic one has no such step. At what h does an explicit quadratic drag step become unstable, and how does that limit compare to §6's?

    Hint: linearise about the current speed — the effective coefficient is 2·(½ρCdA|v|)/m, which grows with speed, so the stability limit tightens as the body speeds up. That is a different and nastier shape of bug than 8.1's, because the simulation is stable until it is fast.

  5. 8.2.5 — An engine challenge: make the frame rule enforceable. inspect_frame can tell you a frame is unsafe and nothing calls it. Design — and then build — a debug-only check that catches a body being integrated in a non-inertial frame. You will find there is no place to put it, because body_world deliberately has no idea a hierarchy exists. Resolve that, and defend your resolution.

    Hint: there are at least three defensible answers and they are not equivalent. A validation pass over the scene that runs on load and reports every node whose world matrix is non-inertial and has a body under it; an assertion inside place_in_parent, which is the one function that sees both spaces; or an ECS-side rule that a body component may not be attached below a scaled node. Ask of each: does it catch the spinning case? (§9.4 says nothing can.)

17. Recap & next

The engine has bodies. A rigid_body is 8.1's motion plus a mass, a force accumulator and two knobs, and body_world is 5.4's pool plus a walk. In one lesson this engine has gone from “a thing that can advance a state” to “a thing that can be told what is pushing on what”, and every one of the remaining eleven lessons in this module adds forces or impulses to that same accumulator.

What the lesson actually turned on, in four sentences. Forces add, which is why an accumulator and not a setter, and which is the reason four independent systems need no interface between them. The reciprocal is the quantity the arithmetic wants — not because of the divide, but because immovable is the common case, it is an exact zero, and 8.9's effective mass is literally a sum of inverse masses. An impulse has no h in it, which is a 23× difference in a jump and the reason every contact from 8.9 onward is an impulse. And the units and the frame are physics, not bookkeeping: a world at the wrong scale runs its clock at √s, and a body integrated under a scaled parent falls at the wrong rate with nothing anywhere reporting an error.

Two measurements were worth the harness on their own. A sweep of a million masses found that 16% of them fail a round trip that seven hand-picked ones all passed — the clearest case yet of test data chosen by a person agreeing with the code by construction. And of two candidate optimisations, the famous one (avoid a sqrt) was a tie at 1.036, 0.968, 0.969, while the one nobody talks about (do not compute a mass in order to cancel it) was worth 15% of the whole update.

Next: Lesson 8.3 — Angular Dynamics: Torque and the Inertia Tensor. Everything here has been the linear half, and it has been separable because momentum and angular momentum do not mix. 8.3 takes the other half, and the first thing it changes is the signature of add_force: a force applied at a point produces a torque r × F, whose sign is fixed by this course's right-handed convention rather than by taste. Then the quantity that plays the role mass played here — the inertia tensor, which is a 3×3 matrix rather than a scalar because a body resists being spun differently about different axes, and which therefore has to be carried into world space by a basis change R I Rᵀ every step. mat3 will gain the three operators an outer product needs. And the pleasing part: inv_mass = 0 has an exact analogue — a zero inverse inertia tensor is precisely what an immovable body wants, for exactly the reasons §4 gave, which is the second time this lesson's storage decision will pay for itself.

18. Further reading

  • Erin Catto, Box2D source and documentation. The reference for everything in this lesson. b2Body stores m_invMass beside m_mass, integrates gravity as an acceleration in the velocity update, and separates static, kinematic and dynamic exactly as §4.4 does. The manual's “Units” section is the source for the 0.1–10 m guidance in §8.4.
  • Glenn Fiedler, “Integration Basics” and “Fix Your Timestep” (gafferongames.com). The companion to 8.1, and the second of the two is what engine::fixed_step is. Fiedler's force-accumulator shape is the one every engine converges on.
  • Ian Millington, Game Physics Engine Development, 2nd ed., chapters 3–6. The clearest book-length treatment of the linear half specifically, and the only one that spends a full chapter on the difference between a force and an impulse before introducing contacts. Chapter 3 is where the inverseMass argument is made at length.
  • David Baraff, “Physically Based Modeling: Rigid Body Simulation” (SIGGRAPH course notes). Part I is the linear case, written by the person whose notation most of the field still uses. Worth reading now for the derivation and again after 8.3 for the angular half.
  • Christer Ericson, Real-Time Collision Detection, chapter 11 (“Numerical Robustness”). Not about forces, but it is the right place to follow up §3.4 and §5.3 — the section on catastrophic cancellation explains exactly why the smallest of four forces vanished into a partial sum of a million.
  • The Apollo 15 hammer–feather drop (NASA, 1971, AFJ archive). Ninety seconds of film, and the only direct demonstration of §2's point without an air pump. Worth watching once if you have not.
  • On miniatures and overcranking: the √s law in §8.1 is standard practice in visual effects and is usually stated as “shoot at √(1/scale) times speed”. Any practical VFX reference covering miniature photography derives it the same way; the physics is one line and the craft around it is a century old.
