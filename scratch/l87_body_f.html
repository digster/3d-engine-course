14. Build & run

The engine and the demo build the same way they have since 5.1. 

cmake -S . -B build
cmake --build build --target manifold
./build/demos/manifold

On Windows with MSVC, the executable lands in a configuration subdirectory: 

cmake -S . -B build
cmake --build build --config Debug --target manifold
.\build\demos\Debug\manifold.exe

The harness wants an optimised library, because §11 quotes the cost of a manifold against the depth query it is built on and both are calls into libengine.a, where a harness's own -O2 cannot reach. build_verify_87.sh refuses rather than quietly producing numbers — 7.6 measured the cost of ignoring that at 18.1× on skinning: 

cmake -S . -B build-rel -DCMAKE_BUILD_TYPE=Release
cmake --build build-rel --target engine
sh scratch/build_verify_87.sh

15. Common pitfalls & debugging

Symptom: the manifold is empty on a contact that plainly exists.
Cause: the reference polygon's winding is clockwise about its normal rather than counter-clockwise, so every side plane built as cross(edge, normal) faces inward and classifies everything as outside.
Fix: check one face by hand. Take three consecutive vertices a, b, c and confirm dot(cross(b − a, c − b), normal) > 0. §5's control does exactly this and reports 0 of 4 incident vertices accepted when the winding is reversed — which is the quietest way there is to lose a floor, because nothing errors.

Symptom: contact points are on the far side of one of the shapes.
Cause: one of the two face_of calls has the wrong sign. a looks along +n and b looks back along −n, because the normal points from a toward b.
Fix: assert that dot(fa.normal, n) > 0 and dot(fb.normal, −n) > 0. The failure looks, on screen, exactly like a body being flung across the level by a solver that is working perfectly, which is why it is worth an assertion rather than a comment.

Symptom: a crate rocks on a floor even with four contact points.
Cause: probably not the manifold. Print support_area(): if it is near zero the manifold has four points on a line, which resists nothing about that line. If it is the size of the face, the rocking is the solver's, and 8.9 and 8.10 are where it goes.
Fix: if the area really is collapsing, check face_cos — §6's table shows the mean contact count falling as it tightens, and a crate one hundredth of a degree off a face normal dropping to an edge contact is exactly this.

Symptom: warm starting does nothing; warm_points is always zero.
Cause: the two manifolds being matched were generated with the shapes the other way round. pair_key is order-independent and the manifold is not.
Fix: fix the order at the broadphase — lower body index first — and check by printing reference_on_b on consecutive frames. If it alternates, the arguments are alternating.

Symptom: a tessellated cylinder's side contact is two points, not four.
Cause: the gather tolerance is tighter than the angle between the cylinder's consecutive face normals, so the “face” recovered from the supporting plane is the single edge between two of them.
Fix: raise gather_sin for that asset, or — properly — give the hull a face list. §5 sweeps both ends and says plainly that there is no value that is right.

Symptom: contact depths are slightly wrong on hull contacts.
Cause: the face's normal is being taken as the query direction rather than computed from the gathered polygon. The two can differ by asin(k_face_gather_sin) — nearly three degrees — and the reference plane is what every depth is measured from.
Fix: Newell's method over the sorted polygon, oriented to agree with the query. And note that it has to be after the sort: Newell's sum over vertices in exporter order is not the face's normal.

Symptom: a contact point's position is nowhere near either surface.
Cause: you are measuring the midpoint. It is defined as halfway between the two surfaces, so on a deep overlap it is legitimately depth/2 from each.
Fix: recover the surface points — A's is position + normal·depth/2, B's is position − normal·depth/2 — and measure those. §7's first instrument made exactly this mistake and reported a 0.63 m “bug” that was a fact about the convention.

16. Exercises

  1. Do the statics by hand, then check it. A 20 kg crate 0.8 m wide rests on four contact points at its base corners. Write down the largest moment about its centre of mass that the contact set can resist, then set up the same crate in a simulate_rest-style loop and bisect for the value at which it tips. Explain the sign of the discrepancy.

    Hint. m·g·(w/2) = 20 × 9.81 × 0.4 = 78.48 N·m. The measurement should come out a couple of per cent low, for the reason §1 gives: the crate's effective lip is inside its geometric one by however much penetration the solver allows.

  2. Break the winding, and watch where it does not error. In support_face(const obb&, vec3), swap f.v[1] and f.v[3]. Rebuild and run the demo. What does preset 1 show? What does the harness say? Then swap them back and instead reverse the sign of side in the clipper, and compare the two failures.

    Hint. One of them empties the manifold; the other keeps everything, including points outside the reference face. Both are silent, and only one of them is caught by a test that checks the points are on the surfaces.

  3. Measure the fifth point yourself. Extend §8's brute-force comparison to ask what the best five points span, as a fraction of the whole clipped polygon. Then argue — from §2's arithmetic, not from the number — why the answer does not change what a solver can do.

    Hint. Five points span more area. The question a solver asks is whether the resultant can act anywhere in the support polygon, and it already could.

  4. Give hull its faces. Add an optional std::span<const face_range> to hull, where each entry names a run of indices into a shared index array. Build one by hand for a hexagonal prism, make support_face use it when present and fall back to the gather when it is not, and re-run §5's tolerance sweep. Report what the sweep now looks like.

    Hint. With real faces the sweep should be flat: the tolerance has nothing to decide, because the face is named rather than recovered. That flatness is the whole argument for doing it.

  5. An engine challenge: make the swing survivable. §1 measured a 90° normal swing between two consecutive frames. Write an experiment that runs a box sliding past a wall's end at a realistic speed, prints the manifold's ids and normal on every frame, and reports how many frames the ids survive on either side of the crossing. Then propose — and measure — a policy for what a solver should do on the frame the normal flips.

    Hint. Two candidate policies worth comparing: drop the warm impulses on a flip (safe, and the stack loses a frame of convergence), or rotate them into the new normal's basis (keeps magnitude, and is wrong if the flip is genuine). Measure which one settles a stack faster, and note that you now have a reason to prefer one rather than a preference.

17. Recap & next

The engine can now say where two shapes touch, not just how far into each other they are. A contact_manifold is up to four points sharing one normal, each with its own depth and its own identity, and the identity is what lets 8.10 begin each contact from the impulse that held it last frame.

The four things worth carrying forward:

  • A manifold is a support polygon, and a support polygon is a statics problem. A body rests when its centre of mass projects inside the convex hull of its contact points, and the moment that hull can carry is m·g·(w/2) — predicted at 49.05 N·m and bisected at 47.87. One contact point spans a hull of zero area and holds a crate up only by chattering, at 27.99 °/s of permanent rocking and 21 mm of sink.
  • A support function determines a shape and names no feature. Four thousand directions from a cone a thousandth of a degree wide return all four corners of the same face, deterministically and arbitrarily. The fix is one more query on the shapes, not a cleverer reading of EPA's output — and epa.hpp was not touched.
  • Clip, filter, reduce. Sutherland–Hodgman against the reference face's side planes, keep what is actually penetrating, and take the deepest point plus the three that span the most area. That reduction reaches 96.3% of the best four there are and loses no depth at all.
  • Match on identity, never on position. A crate's landing matches 0.00% of contacts at a ten-micrometre position tolerance and 82% by id, rising to 100% of 1,999 consecutive frames once it settles. And a position matcher passes every test you would write for it, because it only fails once things move.

Two smaller things that are really the same thing: a control that convicts the code of the test's own mistake looks exactly like a finding, and a knob written for a real hazard can measure zero and should then come out.

18. Further reading

  • Erin Catto, “Contact Manifolds” — GDC 2007. The clearest statement of the reference/incident split and of why persistence is keyed on ids. Two dimensional, which makes the ideas visible; every one of them generalises.
  • Erin Catto, “Modeling and Solving Constraints” — GDC 2009. The other half: what the solver does with a manifold, and why warm starting is the difference between a stack and a pile. 8.10's reference.
  • Dirk Gregorius, “Robust Contact Creation for Physics Simulations” — GDC 2015. The industrial version of §5 through §8, including face-list colliders, the Sutherland–Hodgman variants, and the numerical care a shipping engine takes over the reference choice.
  • Christer Ericson, Real-Time Collision Detection, §5.1.9 and §9 — closest points on two segments (the edge-contact path), and polygon clipping.
  • Sutherland & Hodgman, “Reentrant Polygon Clipping”, CACM 17(1), 1974 — the original, four pages, and worth reading for how little there is to it.
  • Box2D's b2CollidePolygons — the reference implementation of §6 and §7, in about two hundred lines. Note its bias on the reference choice, and §9's measurement of why this engine does not need one.
  • Real-Time Rendering, 4th ed., §22.13 — for the wider context of what a collision system owes a simulation.
  • Lesson 3.3 of this course — the near-plane clipper, which is §7's algorithm with one plane instead of four.
