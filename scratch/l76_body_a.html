Skeletal Animation: The Skinning Math

Skinning has more vocabulary than mathematics. Bind pose, inverse bind matrix, matrix palette, skin cluster, linear blend skinning — six terms for four multiply-adds and one matrix product, and the terms are what make it sound hard. This lesson throws the vocabulary away and derives the one matrix that matters from the naming convention Lesson 2.8 established: a skinning matrix is model_from_joint times joint_from_model, the inner labels cancel because they name the same joint at two different times, and the outer labels are the same space — so a skinning matrix is model_from_model, which is both why four of them may be added together and why the sum is not a rotation. Everything else follows. Building the inverse bind matrices turns out to need no matrix inversion at all, because inverting a product reverses it and the inverse chain composes with the same flat loop as the forward one — which finally writes the function Lesson 2.8 deliberately left out. At the bind pose every skinning matrix is exactly the identity, which is the cheapest complete test of a rig that exists and needs no reference image. Then the artifact: linear blend skinning collapses a twisted limb to cos(θ/2) of its radius — exactly zero at a half-turn, the same half-angle Lesson 7.3 found between two mirrors — and spreading the twist over n joints opens it back to cos(θ/2n), which is what a forearm twist bone is. A bend obeys the same cosine on the minor axis of an ellipse. And the one-line summary everybody repeats, that linear blend skinning is nlerp without the normalise, turns out to be wrong in both directions: it is worse, because its schedule error uses the whole angle rather than the half angle, and it is better, because a rotation matrix is unique so the double cover cannot bite.

1. The problem: a hierarchy moves objects, not surfaces

Lesson 5.9 built a transform hierarchy, and it is already most of a skeleton. It walks parents before children, it composes parent_from_local down the chain, and one line of it —

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathrm{world\_from\_local}(\text{child}) \;=\;\; &\mathrm{world\_from\_local}(\text{parent}) \\[2pt] \cdot\;\; &\mathrm{parent\_from\_local}(\text{child}) \end{aligned} \]

…a child's world matrix is its parent's world matrix times its own local one.

…is the whole of a scene graph. A tank's turret follows its hull with that and nothing else. So does a robot arm, a gun on a mount, a wheel on an axle, a camera on a boom. Every one of those is a set of rigid parts, and a rigid part is exactly what a transform and a mesh handle describe.

Now try a character's elbow. The upper arm turns one way, the forearm turns another, and the skin over the joint is one continuous surface. Ask the hierarchy where a vertex two centimetres above the elbow crease should go and it has no answer to give: that vertex does not belong to the upper arm, and it does not belong to the forearm. Assign it to either and the surface tears open at the seam as soon as the joint bends. Assign it to a third rigid part and you have made the problem smaller and not different.

The difficulty is not the mathematics. It is the geometry. Nothing about composing matrices got harder between a turret and an elbow; what changed is that the object stopped being a set of objects. A character is one mesh, and the thing that moves it has to be able to move parts of one mesh by different amounts.

Skinning is the answer and it is one sentence: let every vertex be moved by several joints at once, and blend the results by weight.

\[ v' \;=\; \sum_i w_i \, S_i \, v \]

…the deformed vertex is the weighted sum of what each influencing joint would have done to it on its own.

That equation is the entire technique. Everything in this lesson is either an explanation of what S_i has to be, or a consequence of the fact that a weighted sum of rotations is not a rotation. Neither of those is difficult; both of them are routinely presented in a vocabulary that makes them sound as if they are.

The vocabulary, translated once, and then dropped

Bind pose: the pose the mesh was modelled in. Rest pose: usually the same thing; occasionally a rig distinguishes them and then rest means the pose a joint returns to when nothing animates it. Inverse bind matrix: the matrix that carries a point from model space into a joint's space, measured at bind time. Matrix palette: the array of one skinning matrix per joint, rebuilt every frame. Skin cluster, skin deformer, skin modifier: three tools' names for the weighting. Linear blend skinning, smooth skinning, vertex blending, matrix palette skinning: four names for the sum above.

Notice how many of those name the thing by how it was made rather than by what it does. "Inverse bind matrix" tells you it was obtained by inverting something at bind time. In this engine's convention it is called joint_from_model, which tells you what it is for — and, as §3 shows, makes the formula it belongs in unmistakable.

2. Building intuition: write down where the vertex was

Before any algebra, here is the whole idea in a sentence you can check against your own arm.

A vertex on your forearm has a fixed position relative to your forearm bone. Bend your elbow and the vertex moves through the room, but its position relative to the bone does not change at all — that is what "attached to the bone" means. So if you knew where the vertex sat in the bone's own frame, and you knew where the bone is now, you could put the vertex back: carry it out of the bone's frame with the bone's current placement.

The trouble is that nobody ever told you where the vertex sits in the bone's frame. The artist modelled the character in one pose, and every vertex's coordinates are in model space — the character's own frame, the one its origin sits at, the one its exported file is written in. No vertex has ever been expressed in any joint's coordinates.

So write it down once. Pick the pose the mesh was modelled in, call it the bind pose, and in that pose compute where each joint is. Then each vertex's position in a joint's frame is just its model-space position carried backwards through that joint's placement — and that is a fixed matrix per joint, computed once, never again.

That is the inverse bind matrix and there is nothing else to it. It is not a correction, or a compensation, or a trick. It is the answer to "where was this joint when the mesh was built", so that the mesh's coordinates can be re-expressed relative to it.

Two frames of the same joint, and the whole of skinning between them

There are exactly two joint placements in play and they are the same joint at different times: where the joint was when the mesh was modelled, and where the joint is now. Take the vertex backwards through the first and forwards through the second. Every difficulty people have with skinning is a failure to notice that these are two different matrices for one joint — which is exactly what the vocabulary hides, because "inverse bind matrix" and "bone matrix" sound like two unrelated objects rather than one object at two times.

And now the blend. A vertex near the elbow is written down relative to both bones — two different frames, two different fixed positions, both computed at bind time. Ask each bone where that vertex should be now and you get two answers. Take a weighted average. The crease vertex is 90% forearm and 10% upper arm; the one above it is 50/50; the one further up is 10/90. The surface stays continuous because the weights vary continuously, and it stays attached to both bones because it is attached to both bones.
