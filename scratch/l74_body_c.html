7. The one price, made visible

"Quaternions do not commute" is a sentence. gimbal's new C mode turns it into a picture and a number, and the picture is better than the number.

Take two turns of the same size φ, one about +Y and one about +X, and perform them in both orders. There is exactly one parameter, so the whole family is one slider — and it is the case everybody already knows by heart, because it is the book you turn twice on a desk.

7.1 Derived first, so the measurement has something to disagree with

With c = cos(φ/2) and s = sin(φ/2), §4.1's formula gives the two composites directly, and they are startlingly close together:

\[ \begin{aligned} q_y(\varphi)\,q_x(\varphi) &= \big(c^2,\;\; cs\,\hat x + cs\,\hat y - s^2 \hat z\big) \\ q_x(\varphi)\,q_y(\varphi) &= \big(c^2,\;\; cs\,\hat x + cs\,\hat y + s^2 \hat z\big) \end{aligned} \]

Identical but for the sign of the z component — which is the cross product, and nothing else, exactly as §4.2 said. Their four-component dot product is therefore c⁴ + 2c²s² − s⁴, and since the angle between two unit quaternions is twice the arccosine of that (§8.2 explains the factor of two), the gap is

\[ \text{gap}(\varphi) \;=\; 2\arccos\big|\,c^4 + 2c^2s^2 - s^4\,\big| \]

At φ = 90°, c = s = 1/\sqrt2, so c² = s² = 1/2 and the dot product is 1/4 + 1/2 − 1/4 = 1/2. So

\[ \text{gap}(90°) \;=\; 2\arccos(1/2) \;=\; 2 \times 60° \;=\; 120° \]

Two perpendicular quarter-turns, performed in the two orders, leave you 120° apart. Exactly 120°, with no rounding anywhere in the derivation.

7.2 Then measured, three ways

gimbal: commute 90.00 deg -> gap 120.0000 deg
gimbal:   matrix 120.0000, closed form 120.0000

Three routes to one number: the quaternion metric angle_between, the matrix metric angle_between_rotations that Lesson 7.1 wrote and that has never heard of a quaternion, and the closed form above. They share no code. A single route reporting 120° is a hope; three agreeing is a fact.

7.3 And the picture is the better half

The first draft of this mode drew only the two destinations — two aircraft and an arc between them. That is an honest picture of a fact nobody disputes, and it cannot show why they differ. Drawing each order as the two arcs it actually is fixes that for free:

The four arcs form a quadrilateral that fails to close. Same two turns, same two axes, same starting nose; the only difference is which one you do first, and the journeys end 120° apart. It is the same picture as walking a mile south, a mile east, a mile north and a mile west on a sphere and not arriving home — and it is the same phenomenon, which is why people who have met differential geometry call the gap holonomy and everyone else calls it "rotations don't commute".

Try it

Press C and watch the gap open from nothing. The sweep runs φ from 0 to 180° and back, and the reason it goes back as well as out is that the interesting part is not the 120° at the far end — it is that the gap is present at every angle but zero, growing like φ² at first. Non-commutativity is not a threshold effect waiting at large angles. A pair of 5° turns do not commute either; they miss by 0.44°, which is small and is not nothing, and over a few thousand frames of integrating angular velocity it is Module 8's problem.

8. The double cover

§5.3 produced a sign that the derivation could not determine. This section is that observation, promoted to a property, measured, and then turned into the one API decision it forces.

8.1 Why q and −q are the same rotation, exactly

Look at the sandwich: q v q̄. Replace q with −q. The conjugate of −q is −q̄, so the expression becomes (−q) v (−q̄), and the two minus signs multiply to +1.

\[ (-q)\,v\,\overline{(-q)} \;=\; (-1)(-1)\,q\,v\,\bar q \;=\; q\,v\,\bar q \]

The sandwich is quadratic in q — every term carries one factor from each side — so a global sign cannot survive it. This is not "the same to within floating point"; it is the same sequence of operations on the same magnitudes, and the measurement says so: 

D. HALF ANGLES AND THE DOUBLE COVER
   worst |M(q) - M(-q)| over 20000:   0.000e+00
  [PASS] D.1  q and -q are the same rotation, bitwise

0.000e+00, not 1e-7. Over 20,000 random rotations, the two matrices are bit-identical.

So the map from unit quaternions to rotations is two-to-one. Every rotation has exactly two quaternions, differing by a sign, and no implementation can recover which one you started from. That is what "double cover" names, and Lesson 7.3 predicted it three lessons early from the mirrors: R and −R are different pairs of mirrors that generate the same turn, because a rotation remembers the angle between its mirrors and not which mirrors.

8.2 The bug you get if you forget

Here is the one place it must be handled, and it is the single most common quaternion bug in shipped code. The angle between two orientations is 

[[nodiscard]] inline float angle_between(quat a, quat b)
{
    const float d = a.w * b.w + dot(a.v, b.v);
    return 2.0f * std::acos(std::clamp(std::fabs(d), 0.0f, 1.0f));
}

Two things in that body deserve attention. The first is that the real part of q̄ₐ q_b is exactly the four-component dot product of a and b — one of the small miracles of this algebra, and the reason every slerp implementation you will ever read starts with a dot product. The second is the std::fabs, and it is the double cover: 

   angle_between(q, -q):    0.0000 deg
   with the fabs removed: 360.0000 deg
  [PASS] D.2  CONTROL: dropping the fabs reports 360 deg
Symptom → cause → fix

Symptom: a character occasionally spins all the way round between two keyframes that look adjacent in the authoring tool.
Cause: a metric or a blend that treats q and −q as far apart. The two poses are the same orientation; the two quaternions are antipodal.
Fix: take the absolute value of the dot product, or negate one input when the dot product is negative. Lesson 7.5 pays for this properly — the same sign choice, made once, is what makes slerp take the short way round.

8.4 Is the double cover a problem?

Almost never, and it is occasionally a feature. §D's other two checks make the point: 

   quat::i() is a turn of 180.0 deg
  [PASS] D.4  quat::i() is a half-turn, not a quarter
   quarter turn about x = (0.707107, 0.707107, 0, 0)
  [PASS] D.5  a quarter turn has w = cos(45 deg)

And axis_angle_from_quat deliberately returns an angle in [0, 2π) rather than [0, π], because a quaternion genuinely distinguishes "turn 350° about n̂" from "turn 10° about −n̂", which a rotation matrix cannot. That is exactly the information an animation system needs in order to take the long way round on purpose — a turntable, a winding mechanism, a character that must pirouette rather than snap. The double cover being useful rather than annoying.

9. Extraction: four candidates, and no bad case

Going from a quaternion to a matrix is three calls to rotate — column 0 is rotate(q, x̂) and so on, the definition of a matrix this course has used since Lesson 2.5. Going the other way is the interesting direction, and it is where the fourth component pays a dividend nobody advertises.

9.1 The naive route, and where it dies

The trace of a rotation matrix built from a unit quaternion is 4w² − 1, so

\[ w \;=\; \tfrac12\sqrt{1 + \operatorname{tr} R}, \qquad x = \frac{r_{21} - r_{12}}{4w}, \quad y = \frac{r_{02} - r_{20}}{4w}, \quad z = \frac{r_{10} - r_{01}}{4w} \]

Four lines, correct, and it is what almost every first implementation looks like. It divides by 4w, and w = cos(θ/2), so as the rotation approaches a half-turn it divides by nearly nothing, and at exactly 180° it divides by zero and loses the axis entirely.

9.2 The fix is Lesson 7.2's fix, which is why they are taught in this order

Lesson 7.2 hit exactly this shape and solved it by pivoting on the largest of three squared components, so that it never divided by something near zero. The three squares sum to 1, so the largest is at least 1/3 and the divisor at least 1/\sqrt3. That was good but not unconditional, and 7.2 had to derive a crossover at tan(θ/2) = \sqrt3, i.e. 120°, between two routes.

Here there are four squares to pivot on, and each is a trace-like combination of the diagonal:

\[ \begin{aligned} 4w^2 &= 1 + r_{00} + r_{11} + r_{22} \qquad & 4x^2 &= 1 + r_{00} - r_{11} - r_{22} \\ 4y^2 &= 1 - r_{00} + r_{11} - r_{22} \qquad & 4z^2 &= 1 - r_{00} - r_{11} + r_{22} \end{aligned} \]

Add the four. The diagonal terms cancel in pairs — r₀₀ appears twice with a plus and twice with a minus, and so does each of the others — leaving

\[ 4w^2 + 4x^2 + 4y^2 + 4z^2 \;=\; 4 \]

which is just |q| = 1 restated, and is the whole argument:

Four numbers summing to 4 leave the largest at least 1

So the divisor 2\sqrt{\text{largest}} is always at least 2, and this routine has no bad case anywhere on the range. No crossover to derive. No threshold to tune. No second route. Four candidates instead of three is what buys that — and four candidates is precisely what having a fourth component means.

The two routes are indistinguishable up to about 120°, which is the region a naive implementation is tested in and the region it ships from. Past that the naive route degrades, and at 179.99° it is off by 180 degrees — not a precision loss, a completely different orientation. At exactly 180° it returns the identity.

The method is named after Stanley Shepperd, whose 1978 note in the Journal of Guidance and Control is the usual citation. It is a page long and it is worth reading after this section, because it arrives at the same four candidates from the same argument.

9.4 A prediction the measurement refused

§G.3 was written to make a claim that turned out to be false, and the false version is more instructive than the true one would have been.

The prediction: extracting an axis from a quaternion should be better conditioned at small turns than extracting it from a matrix, because |v| = sin(θ/2) is a quantity of the size of the answer, whereas the matrix route divides by 2 sin θ formed as a difference of near-equal entries. Lesson 7.2 measured that difference costing an axis half a degree at θ = 10⁻⁵.

Feed both routes a matrix carrying 10⁻⁷ of absolute error in every entry — the same magnitude 7.2 used, so the two lessons' numbers are comparable — and measure each against the axis the matrix was built from: 

   turn      matrix err    quat err
      0.05   3.279e-03   3.281e-03
      1.00   2.464e-04   2.441e-04
     45.00   1.025e-05   8.538e-06
    119.00   3.415e-06   4.830e-06
    121.00   5.123e-06   1.093e-05
    170.00   8.365e-06   7.636e-06
    179.90   5.123e-06   3.818e-06
   worst: matrix 3.279e-03, quat 3.281e-03
   ratio at 0.05 deg: 1.0007
  [PASS] G.3  the two extractions are equally conditioned
Why the prediction was wrong, and it is worth more than the prediction

quat_from_rotation's input is a matrix. At a small turn it pivots on w, and the three vector components then come off exactly the same antisymmetric differences the matrix route uses. The information was already gone before either route ran. A quaternion's better conditioning is a property of holding one, not of extracting one, and Lesson 7.2's hole at θ = 0 is a hole in the matrix that no extraction can patch.

So what the four-candidate pivot actually buys is not accuracy. It is the absence of a decision — no crossover, no threshold, no second route, and no failure at exactly π, which the naive quaternion route does have and §E.2 measures at 180° of error. That is a real and different benefit, and stating it correctly is worth more than the plausible claim the check was written to confirm.

9.5 Two more controls

   worst round trip over 20000:      3.331e-05 deg
  [PASS] E.3  matrix -> quat -> matrix is exact
   worst |q - +/-extract(M(q))|:      1.192e-07
  [PASS] E.4  CONTROL: the components come back, up to sign

E.3 would pass trivially on a routine that happened to return something whose matrix is the identity for an identity input, so E.4 starts from a known quaternion, builds its matrix, extracts, and demands the components back — up to sign, because §8.1 proved no implementation can do better than that.
