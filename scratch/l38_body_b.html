Five files, and for the first time in three lessons one of them is raster.cpp. As always this section is for understanding; every file appears whole in §Complete Code Listings.

4.1 Two varyings, and a vertex that changed meaning

To evaluate the shading at a fragment, the fragment needs the shading's inputs. It has the albedo already — that is the interpolated corner colour — and it needs two more things:

Two fields, and they change what vertex is. Every field before them was either geometry (x, y, z, inv_w) or a finished answer (colour, u, v). These are inputs to a calculation that has not happened yet, carried across the triangle so that something downstream can do it.

That has a name. A GPU vertex shader emits a clip-space position plus an arbitrary bundle of values that the rasterizer interpolates and hands to the fragment shader, and those values are called varyings. Our vertex just became a position plus varyings, which is what it will be for the rest of the course.

One consequence is worth stating because it is easy to be sloppy about: vertex::colour now means different things in different pipelines. Under shading::vertex_colour it is a lit result. Under shading::lit it is the albedo, an input. That is not a wart — deciding what a varying means is precisely the vertex stage's job, and this is the first time this engine has had one whose meaning depends on what it is bound to.

The cost, said out loud

Six floats. vertex goes from 28 bytes to 52 — nearly double — and the fill interpolates six more values per pixel whether or not it uses them. That is not free and it is not hidden: it is the price of moving a calculation downstream, paid in bandwidth so it can be saved in accuracy. Module 4's GPU pipeline pays the same price, which is why minimising the number of varyings is a real optimisation there.

4.2 The clipper has to carry them too

Easy to miss, and it fails in a way that only shows up when you walk into geometry.

Lesson 3.3 established that clipping happens before the perspective divide, on its own vertex type. When the near plane cuts a triangle, the clipper creates a genuinely new corner and interpolates every attribute to it with the crossing parameter t. If the normal and the world position are not among those attributes, a clipped triangle gets whatever was left in the struct — and the symptom is that geometry lights correctly until it touches the near plane, then goes wrong at the edge of the screen.

Note what is not done: the normal is not renormalised here. It is going to be interpolated again by the fill on its way to the pixel, which will shorten it further, so normalising at this point fixes nothing that the fragment's own normalise would not. §3.4 is the whole argument; the code just declines to do the work twice.

4.3 The fragment loop

Here is the change the lesson is named for, and it is six lines in the middle of a loop that has not been touched since Lesson 3.2.

Three things about that are worth noticing, and none of them is the arithmetic.

The interpolation is the same interpolation. Those are the identical three multiply-adds and the identical w_recip that carry a colour or a texture coordinate. Lesson 3.2's derivation of perspective-correct interpolation never mentioned what the attribute was — it only required that the quantity be linear over the surface — which is why it covers a normal and a position with no new argument at all. The rasterizer still does not know what it is carrying.

The equation is the same equation. shade() is unchanged, byte for byte, from Lesson 3.7. Nothing about the lighting model moved. This is the distinction the whole lesson is drawing, and it is worth seeing that the diff says it too.

The albedo goes in linear and comes out encoded once. Under lit the corner colours are decoded to linear (they always were, under blend_space::linear), interpolated, multiplied by light, and encoded exactly once at the end — where an encode belongs. That also means blend_space::encoded has no meaning under lit, and rather than honour it into nonsense the fill ignores it:

And note the null check. A pipeline set to lit with no lights is a configuration mistake, not a crash: it falls back to the unlit path and draws an unlit surface, which is a thing you can see and diagnose. Dereferencing a null pointer per fragment is not.

4.4 The dependency this adds, named

raster.hpp now includes light.hpp. For seven lessons the rasterizer had no idea that light existed; today it calls the shading function directly.

That is a layering violation and it is being shipped on purpose. The rasterizer's job is coverage and interpolation; what a covered pixel should look like is somebody else's business. Lesson 2.4 said so when it created the shading enum:

"This enum is a placeholder for a fragment shader … A real renderer lets the caller supply the function; Module 4 does exactly that."

The clean fix is to make the fragment calculation a parameter — a callback, or a template, or a small object the caller supplies. That is what a fragment shader is, and building it here would mean inventing Module 4's answer three lessons before the question is fully formed. So the enum grows one more value and the coupling is written down instead.

This is exactly what happened historically

Fixed-function graphics hardware made this same trade and lost the same way. Lighting was baked into the pipeline as a fixed set of modes with fixed parameters, which was fast and entirely sufficient right up until somebody wanted a material the hardware designers had not anticipated. The whole programmable-shader era is the industry paying off this exact debt. Feeling the pull yourself, on twenty lines of your own code, is worth more than being told about it.

4.5 One material per triangle, which a GPU cannot do

Under Gouraud, the material was consumed in the vertex stage and never reached the fill. Under per-pixel it has to be available inside the fragment loop — and the demo draws the whole scene as one batch with one fill_style, while the three objects have three different materials.

The demo solves this by carrying the material on each triangle and rebinding it in the draw loop:

A GPU cannot do that, and the reason is the same one fill_style exists for. Material parameters live in a bound pipeline or a uniform buffer; changing them means ending the draw call and issuing another. Real renderers therefore batch by material, and a scene with three materials is three draws — which is why sorting draws by material is a standard optimisation and why "how many draw calls?" is a question people ask about a frame.

We take the shortcut because splitting the demo's single pass into per-object draws would break the painter's-algorithm comparison that has run since Lesson 3.1 (that one needs to sort across objects, so it cannot be done per object). It is named rather than hidden, and it is the third time this pressure has appeared from a new direction: 3.4 found it with cull modes, 3.7 found it with specular, and this is the same finger pointing at Module 6's material system.

4.6 Two keys for two axes

G cycles the evaluation point — palette, flat, Gouraud, per-pixel — and Q cycles the normal source between face and vertex. Two keys, because two questions, and the HUD prints them on two lines for the same reason. A single line reading [G] smooth was precisely the conflation this lesson exists to undo.

The HUD's third number is the grid's own check: how many pixels this cell differs from per-pixel by, rendered every frame. Two cells must read exactly 0, and watching them do so is how you confirm the axes are wired correctly:

The first version of that function had if (e == shade_eval::gouraud) return true; — degenerate unconditionally, no dependence on the specular model. It was wrong, the harness found 761 pixels that said so, and the corrected version is shorter. That is usually how it goes: a rule with an exception carved into it is often a rule stated at the wrong level.

Finally, the fill is timed. "Per-pixel shading is expensive" is a claim, and a claim wants a number:

It measures the fill only — not the projection, not the clip, not the HUD — because that is the part the evaluation point actually changes. Smoothed across frames the way clock::fps() is (Lesson 1.3) and for the same reason: one frame's timing on a desktop OS is mostly noise.

Drag the facet count down and watch the red line come apart. Then raise the shininess: the curve sharpens, the chords get worse faster than the tessellation can keep up, and at high exponents Gouraud misses the peak entirely — which is 3.7's Table 5 happening in front of you.

6Build & Run

What you should see

Press C to the model scene and L to the torus, then work the two new keys. In order:

  1. G through the four evaluation points. Palette, flat, Gouraud, per-pixel. The step from flat to Gouraud is the big visual one — 2,304 facets become a smooth surface. The step from Gouraud to per-pixel is subtler and it is the one to look at properly: the highlight becomes round and stops having faint creases across it.
  2. Q to face normals, then G through all four again. With the highlight off (H), flat, Gouraud and per-pixel become indistinguishable and the HUD reads DEGENERATE cell = flat, 0 px. That zero is §3.1's claim, live.
  3. Now press H to turn the highlight back on. With face normals still selected, the three stop matching — the HUD count jumps off zero. The normal is still constant; it is the viewer that varies across a face.
  4. L to cube.obj, and try to fix it with G. You cannot. Six normals is six normals, and no evaluation point invents a seventh (§3.6).
  5. Watch the microseconds. The HUD prints the fill's own time. At this resolution per-pixel is faster than Gouraud on the torus, for the reason §3.7 measures.
Checkpoint

On the torus with Q on VERTEX and H off: stepping G from Gouraud to per-pixel changes several thousand pixels, and the HUD's vs PER-PIXEL count drops to 0 when you arrive. Switch Q to FACE and all three lit cells read 0. If any of those zeros is not zero, the varyings are not reaching the fragment — check the clipper first (§4.2), because that is the one that only breaks on triangles touching the near plane.

7Common Pitfalls & Debugging

Per-pixel looks identical to Gouraud — the mode switch does nothing
Symptom
You wire up per-pixel shading, the code runs, and the picture does not change. The pixel-difference count reads 0.
Cause
Three candidates. (1) You are looking at a mesh whose normals are split — a cube, or any mesh with hard edges — where the normal is constant per triangle and the two modes genuinely agree (§3.1). That is correct behaviour, not a bug. (2) The varyings are not being written; the fill is interpolating three zero normals and normalised_or is handing back the fallback. (3) The shading is still being done in the vertex stage as well, so the fragment is lighting an already-lit colour.
Fix
Test on a mesh with genuinely smooth normals — the torus — before believing anything. For (3), remember that vertex::colour must be the raw albedo under lit: if it still holds a lit result you are shading twice, and the tell is that the object gets brighter and washes out rather than looking wrong.
Shading breaks only at the edge of the screen — and only when you walk into things
Symptom
Everything is fine until geometry touches the near plane, at which point the clipped triangles light wrongly — often black, sometimes wildly bright.
Cause
The clipper is not interpolating the new varyings (§4.2). A clipped triangle's new corner gets whatever the default-constructed clip_vertex had, which for a normal is the zero vector.
Fix
Add them to lerp. The general rule is worth internalising because it recurs every time you add a varying: anything the fragment reads must be interpolated by every stage between the vertex and the fragment, and the clipper is a stage that is easy to forget precisely because it usually does nothing.
Surfaces are dimmer in the middle of each triangle — a faint dark blotch per face
Symptom
A soft darkening toward the centre of every triangle, zero at the corners, giving a curved surface a subtly quilted look.
Cause
The interpolated normal is not renormalised (§3.4). Its length is cos(θ/2) at the midpoint for normals θ apart — 3.4% short at 30°, 13.4% at 60° — and an un-normalised normal scales brightness by its length.
Fix
Normalise in the fragment. In this engine shade() already does it, so the bug is unreachable — but recognise the signature: zero at the corners and maximal in the middle is a chord drawn under a curve, and it is the same fingerprint as 3.2's affine texturing and 2.4's sub-pixel bias.
Faint bright and dark stripes along triangle edges — that survive more colour depth
Symptom
A smoothly shaded curved surface shows narrow bright or dark bands exactly where facets meet. Sampling the framebuffer at those pixels shows nothing unusual.
Cause
Mach bands (§3.5). Gouraud's intensity ramp is continuous but its slope is not, and lateral inhibition in the visual system turns each slope break into an apparent stripe. The bands are not in the buffer, which is why measuring the pixels finds nothing.
Fix
Per-pixel shading, which evaluates a curve with no kinks. Do not reach for more colour precision — it changes nothing — and be sceptical of tessellating your way out: the error falls as O(h²) but the number of joins rises, and it never terminates.
Per-pixel is slower than expected, or unexpectedly faster — the numbers make no sense
Symptom
Switching to per-pixel costs far more than the "2×" everyone quotes, or — more confusingly — costs less.
Cause
You are measuring at a pixels-per-triangle ratio where the folklore does not apply (§3.7). Below about three pixels per triangle there are more vertices than covered pixels and per-vertex shading is the expensive one; measured here, 0.91× at 320×180 and 2.15× at 4K. Also check whether you are timing the fill or the whole frame: at low resolution the projection and clip dominate and neither changes.
Fix
Report the ratio alongside the timing, always. A shading cost quoted without the triangle density it was measured at is not a number anyone else can use.
The highlight still vanishes on some meshes — even with per-pixel on
Symptom
You implement per-pixel shading specifically to fix 3.7's flickering highlight, and on a cube or a low-poly prop it flickers exactly as much as before.
Cause
Wrong axis (§3.6). Per-pixel shading fixes an interpolation error. A cube has six normals, and whether any of them points near the halfway vector is settled by the geometry before shading begins. Measured: cube.obj goes from 157 blank frames out of 180 to… 157.
Fix
Fix the mesh, not the pipeline: weld the vertices and average the normals so the surface has smoothly varying normals to interpolate (Lesson 3.5). Then per-pixel has something to do — the 12×8 torus goes 58 → 0.

3.8.2Break the clipper, on purposeguided

Comment out the two lines §4.2 added to clip_vertex lerp, then find a camera position where the bug is visible and one where it is not. Measure the difference in pixels using the demo's comparison machinery.

Then answer the question that matters: how long could this bug survive in a codebase?

Hint

The floor scene, and = held down. Lesson 3.3 built that scene specifically because it is the only one where geometry passes the near plane.

Solution sketch

Indefinitely, is the answer. Clipping only fires when geometry crosses the near plane, which in a well-behaved scene is rare — so the bug is invisible in every screenshot, every static test, and most play sessions, and appears only when a player walks into a wall. That is the same detection profile as 2.7's w = 0 bug and 3.3's whole subject, and it is why the near plane deserves its own test scene rather than being exercised incidentally.

3.8.3Skip the renormaliseguided

shade() normalises its normal argument, so this engine cannot exhibit §3.4's artifact. Add a fill_style flag that pre-scales the interpolated normal so the normalise is a no-op, and reproduce the darkening. Then measure it on the torus, and find the tessellation at which it drops below one 8-bit code.

Hint

You cannot easily stop shade() normalising — but you can make its input already unit, and separately multiply the result by the length you would have lost. That reproduces the artifact exactly without touching a function two lessons of code depend on.

Solution sketch

Expect a very small number on a dense mesh — the shipped torus is 0.95% short at worst, which is about 2 codes out of 255 — and a large one on a coarse one, because the shortfall depends on the angle between adjacent normals rather than on the triangle's size in pixels. That dependence is the interesting part: it means the artifact gets worse on exactly the low-poly meshes where you are least likely to be looking for a subtle shading error.

3.8.4Make the fragment stage a parameterengine

§4.4 ships a layering violation on purpose. Fix it: instead of a shading enum with a lit value, let the caller supply the fragment calculation — as a function pointer, a std::function, or a template parameter on fill_triangle. Then measure all three against the enum.

Hint

The three options are not equivalent and the differences are the exercise. One costs an indirect call per pixel; one costs an indirect call plus a possible allocation; one costs nothing at run time and a copy of the inner loop per shader in your binary. Guess the ranking before measuring.

Solution sketch

The template wins on speed and loses on compile time and code size — and it cannot express a shader chosen at run time, which is exactly what a real engine needs. That tension is why GPUs compile shaders to machine code at load time: it is the template approach with the compilation deferred until the shader is known. Seeing why the obvious C++ answers are all slightly wrong is the best possible preparation for Module 4.

3.8.5Find your own crossoveropen

§3.7 measured per-pixel at 0.91× the cost of Gouraud at 320×180 and 2.15× at 4K, on one machine with one mesh. Reproduce it on yours, then push on it: does the crossover move if you subdivide the mesh instead of changing the resolution? If you drop the specular term? If you add a second light?

Solution sketch

Subdividing and lowering the resolution should land on the same curve, because both move the same ratio — confirming that pixels-per-triangle really is the variable, rather than either quantity separately, is the point of the exercise. Dropping the specular removes the powf and flattens the per-pixel cost sharply. A second light multiplies the fragment cost and leaves the vertex cost per-vertex, so it pushes the crossover left — which is the beginning of the argument for deferred shading, where the lighting cost is decoupled from the geometry entirely. That is Module 6 territory.

9Recap & Next

What the engine can do now that it could not before

  • Shade per fragment — the first time raster.cpp's inner loop has changed since Lesson 3.2, and the first time it has known that light exists.
  • Carry varyings: arbitrary per-vertex data interpolated to the pixel, through the clipper and the perspective divide, which is the shape every GPU pipeline has.
  • Choose the normal source and the evaluation point independently, and predict which combinations coincide.
  • Report what the choice costs, in microseconds, every frame.

Three things here will keep paying. Separating two questions that arrived as one knob is a move you will make again — the same shape produced fill_style in 3.2 and will produce the material system in Module 6. A varying is the abstraction the entire programmable pipeline is built on, and you now have one because you needed one. And the folklore was wrong about the cost: per-pixel shading is cheaper than per-vertex below three pixels per triangle, which is a fact you can only have by measuring.

Next: 3.9 — Texture Mapping and Bilinear Filtering. The floor is still shaded by checker_at, a procedural pattern with no image behind it, and it is the one surface in the scene this lesson deliberately left unlit — because a texture and a light have to multiply, and there is no texture yet. 3.9 replaces the rule with a real sampler, settles the uv-origin question Lesson 3.5 left open, and derives bilinear filtering as what it is: a weighted average of weighted averages, which is the third thing in this course to turn out to be a lerp.
