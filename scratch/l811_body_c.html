6. Correcting a joint, and what Baumgarte does to one

A joint that drifts needs a position correction, and 8.10 built two. Both compute a bias velocity −β·C/h — "remove a fraction β of the error this step" — and differ only in which velocity it is applied to. Baumgarte adds it to the target of the ordinary velocity solve, so the body gains a real velocity; split impulse drives a shadow velocity that moves the position at the end of the step and is then thrown away. 8.10 §7 measured Baumgarte throwing a crate spawned 200 mm inside a floor 99 mm into the air, and settled on split impulse as the default. This section set out to show the same failure on a joint.

First the part that is the same. Each step removes a fraction β of the error and the motion adds a drift δ, so the error obeys e_{n+1} = (1 − β)·e_n + δ, whose fixed point is where what is removed equals what is added:

\[ e^{*} \;=\; (1-\beta)\,e^{*} + \delta \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad e^{*} \;=\; \frac{\delta}{\beta} \] e* = (1 - beta) e* + delta, so e* = delta / beta

A turning joint does not sit at zero error under either correction; it sits stretched by five times its per-step drift at β = 0.2. §5's rod again, with the corrections on: 

     correction      stretch at 1 s     delta/beta     |v| at 1 s    energy per step
     none                163.9531 mm       0.0000 mm      4.3039 m/s     0.00386  (w h)^2 0.00380
     Baumgarte            16.5538 mm      16.5268 mm      4.9226 m/s     0.00000  (w h)^2 0.00651
     split impulse        12.4993 mm      12.2235 mm      4.2241 m/s     0.00488  (w h)^2 0.00483

The stretch lands on δ/β under both: 16.55 mm against 16.53 predicted, 12.50 against 12.22. So far, so expected. The last column is the one that refused the section. Without a correction the rod loses (ω·h)² of its energy per step, exactly §5's projection. Under split impulse it loses (ω·h)² per step too — 0.00488 against 0.00483 — because split impulse moves the bob back onto the circle without touching its velocity, and the projection happens anyway. Under Baumgarte the rod loses nothing. A second in, it is still turning at 4.92 m/s where split impulse has let it fall to 4.22.

The reason is Figure 5's picture, and it is the kind of result a derivation would not have gone looking for. At the steady state Baumgarte's bias is a real inward velocity of β·e*/h = δ/h. Next step the rod has turned through ω·h, so a fraction ω·h of that inward velocity now lies along the new tangent:

\[ \frac{\delta}{h}\cdot\omega h \;=\; \frac{v^2 h}{2r}\cdot\frac{v h}{r} \;=\; v\,\frac{(\omega h)^2}{2} \] (delta/h)(w h) = v (w h)^2 / 2

which is precisely the speed the projection removes, since v·(1 − cos ωh) ≈ v(ωh)²/2. Baumgarte adds energy — 8.10 was right about that — and on a joint that is turning, it adds it exactly where the velocity projection takes it away. The stretch δ/β is, in effect, the joint storing the centripetal term a velocity-level constraint leaves out, and feeding it back one step late.

And the dislocated limb

So is Baumgarte the better correction for joints? Not on the evidence of the other half of this section. 8.10's crate left the floor because a contact stops acting once the overlap is gone, and the body keeps the last bias velocity it was given. A joint never stops acting, so the bias should decay with the error — and along the joint's constrained directions it does. The question is the directions a joint leaves free. The fixture is a limb spawned out of its socket, which a ragdoll does every time an animation hands it a pose the joints disagree with: a 0.5 m, 10 kg cube, gravity off, held by a ball-socket at one corner from an anchor 0.2 m away. 

     correction       anchor gap      kinetic energy     spin
     none              0.20000 m         0.000000 J    0.000 rad/s
     Baumgarte         0.00009 m         0.349951 J    0.553 rad/s
     split impulse     0.00000 m         0.000000 J    0.000 rad/s
  CONTROL, the socket at the cube's CENTRE instead (r = 0):
     none              0.20000 m         0.000000 J    0.000 rad/s
     Baumgarte         0.00000 m         0.000000 J    0.000 rad/s
     split impulse     0.00000 m         0.000000 J    0.000 rad/s

Baumgarte's pull on the corner is a real impulse applied off the centre of mass, so it spins the cube, and a ball-socket has no row that resists spin. The energy lands in the free degrees of freedom and stays there: 0.35 J of rotation the cube was never given, forever, because nothing damps a ball-socket. Split impulse closes the same gap with a velocity it throws away and leaves the cube exactly at rest. The control puts the socket through the centre: no lever arm, nothing to spin, and Baumgarte leaves nothing behind either — which is the confirmation that the energy went into the free directions and not into the constrained one.

So the two corrections fail differently, and the engine has to choose. Split impulse adds no energy to anything and costs every turning joint ρ(ωh)² per step. Baumgarte conserves a turning joint's energy and invents energy in any joint that is pulled apart. A joint that loses energy reads on screen as damping; a joint that invents it reads as a limb twitching on a body lying still, which is the bug report every ragdoll system receives. The default stays split impulse, for joints and contacts alike, and §8 puts a number on what that costs a pendulum. Letting joints choose their own correction is §18's second exercise.

7. The ball-socket, and the block

A ball-socket holds two anchor points together: C = p_b − p_a = 0, three components, three rows. Each is the point row of §2 along one world axis, J_k = [−e_k, −(r_a × e_k), e_k, r_b × e_k] — a ball-socket is literally three contacts along x, y and z with their clamps removed. Solve them one after another and it works. It also leaves something behind, for a reason 8.10 §2 already met on a crate's four contact points: the rows are coupled. An impulse along x at an anchor off the centre of mass spins the body, and the spin moves the anchor in y and z, so solving the x row disturbs the other two.

Stack the three rows and J M⁻¹ Jᵀ stops being a number. It is a 3×3 matrix, and expanding it with 8.3's cross-product matrix [r]ₓ (the matrix whose action is v ↦ r × v) gives

\[ K \;=\; J M^{-1} J^{\mathsf T} \;=\; \Bigl(\tfrac{1}{m_a} + \tfrac{1}{m_b}\Bigr)\,\mathbf 1 \;-\; [r_a]_\times\, I_a^{-1}\, [r_a]_\times \;-\; [r_b]_\times\, I_b^{-1}\, [r_b]_\times \] K = (1/m_a + 1/m_b) 1 - [r_a]x Ia^-1 [r_a]x - [r_b]x Ib^-1 [r_b]x

The first term is diagonal: a push through the centre of mass moves the anchor along the push and nowhere else. The other two are not, and their off-diagonal entries are exactly the coupling. Solving the rows one at a time is Gauss–Seidel on K·λ = −Ċ, and Gauss–Seidel's error after each sweep is multiplied by a fixed matrix whose largest eigenvalue — its spectral radius — is computable from K alone, without running anything. Solving it as a block is one matrix inverse at prepare time and one matrix–vector product per visit:

\[ \lambda \;=\; K^{-1}\,\bigl(\text{bias} - \dot C\bigr) \] lambda = K^-1 (bias - dC/dt), a vector impulse applied at the two anchors

Worked. §2's crate on a fixed anchor at its corner, r_b = (0.25, −0.25, 0.25), 1/m = 0.1, I⁻¹ = 2.4·1. For a multiple of the identity, −[r]ₓ(c·1)[r]ₓ = c·(|r|²·1 − r rᵀ), the identity 8.3 §4 checked the inertia tensor with. With |r|² = 0.1875 the diagonal of K is 0.1 + 2.4 × (0.1875 − 0.0625) = 0.4 — each axis alone is §2's 2.5 kg contact — and the off-diagonals are −2.4 × r_i r_j = ±0.15. A diagonal of 0.4 against off-diagonals of 0.15 is strong coupling: the Gauss–Seidel iteration matrix of that K has a spectral radius of 0.568, so three rows solved in turn leave 57% of the error after each visit — about eight visits to reach 1%, where the block needs one. A cube hung by its corner is close to the worst case: the thousand random sockets below never exceed 0.574.

The block is at 1.2 × 10⁻⁷ after one visit and exactly zero after three. Over a thousand random sockets — random box, mass, pose, corner and velocity — one visit of the block leaves at worst 5.9 × 10⁻⁷ of the violation it started with; one visit of the rows leaves up to 92% of it, with a median radius of 0.443 and a worst of 0.574.

Two further measurements decide what ships. The block is cheaper as well as exact — 10.8 ns a visit against 23.1, and 58 ns to prepare against 65, because one 3×3 inverse is less work than three row preparations and one matrix–vector product is less than three row solves. And the control: put the socket through the body's centre of mass, r = 0, and K is 1/m times the identity. Nothing couples, Gauss–Seidel's radius is zero, and the rows are exact in one visit too (9.7 × 10⁻⁸). The coupling lives in the lever arms, and so does everything the block is for.

A block is affordable here and not for contacts, and the reason is the clamp. The three rows of a socket are equalities: λ may be any vector, so the block is a linear solve. A manifold's four normal rows are inequalities, each λ ≥ 0, so a block of them is a small linear complementarity problem — Box2D solves the two-point case by enumerating its four sign patterns, and nobody ships the four-point one. That is 8.10's open question about block solvers, answered for the case where the answer is easy. The engine solves a socket's point as a 3×3 block and a hinge's two alignment rows as a 2×2 one, and keeps the switch (joint_config::block_solve) so that this section can measure the difference.

And the honest limit of the block: it makes each joint exact and does nothing for a chain of them. On a twelve-link chain released from horizontal, eight sweeps leave a worst anchor gap of 94 mm with the block against 102 with rows. A sweep still carries information across one joint at a time, which is 8.10's tower lying on its side, and §14's subject.

8. The pendulum, and the parallel axis

A physical pendulum is the best test an engine's joints can be given, because its period has a closed form that contains the mass, the inertia tensor, the joint and gravity, and gets every one of them wrong in a different, recognisable way. Hang a 0.2 × 1.0 × 0.2 m, 2 kg box from a ball-socket at the middle of its top face, so its centre is d = 0.5 m below the pin. A point mass at the same distance would swing with T = 2π√(d/g). A body swings about the pin, so its inertia is the inertia about the pin:

\[ T \;=\; 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{I_{\text{pivot}}}{m\,g\,d}}, \qquad I_{\text{pivot}} \;=\; I_{\text{cm}} + m\,d^2 \] T = 2 pi sqrt(I_pivot / (m g d)), I_pivot = I_cm + m d^2

Worked. About the swing axis the box's inertia is m·(w² + h²)/12 = 2 × (0.04 + 1.0)/12 = 0.173333 kg·m². The parallel-axis term adds m·d² = 2 × 0.25 = 0.5, so I_pivot = 0.673333. Then T = 2π√(0.673333 / (2 × 9.81 × 0.5)) = 2π × 0.261990 = 1.646116 s. The point-mass formula gives 2π√(0.5/9.81) = 1.418503 s: 13.8% short, at every amplitude, because it is missing the box's own inertia about its centre.

1.645967 s against 1.646241 — and each halving of the step divides the error by 4.02, 4.00, 3.98: second order in h, which is the period error of a symplectic integrator (8.1 §5), with the joint adding nothing of its own. The reference is the period at two degrees, not the small-swing limit, and that choice was forced by the first draft: against the limit, the "error" at 240 Hz came out +6.6 × 10⁻⁵ and refused to shrink further. Two degrees is not zero. A finite swing is slower than an infinitesimal one by an amount with a closed form, and an error that will not converge is almost always a reference that is wrong.

A large swing, and the loveliest formula in the lesson

The exact period at amplitude θ₀ is the small-swing period times (2/π)·K(sin θ₀/2), where K is the complete elliptic integral of the first kind — a function most people meet once and look up forever after. It has a much better form. Gauss showed that K(k) = π / (2·AGM(1, √(1 − k²))), where AGM is the arithmetic–geometric mean: replace two numbers by their arithmetic and geometric means, repeatedly, and they converge — quadratically — to a common value. With k = sin(θ₀/2), √(1 − k²) = cos(θ₀/2), and the π's cancel:

\[ T(\theta_0) \;=\; \frac{T_0}{\operatorname{AGM}\bigl(1,\ \cos(\theta_0/2)\bigr)} \] T(theta0) = T0 / AGM(1, cos(theta0/2))

Worked. At 90°: cos 45° = 0.707107. The means are (0.853553, 0.840896), then (0.847225, 0.847201), then equal to six figures at 0.847213. So a 90° swing takes 1/0.847213 = 1.180341 times as long as a small one: 1.646116 × 1.180341 = 1.942977 s. pendulum_period_at is those dozen lines.

The formula holds to 2.6 × 10⁻³ at every amplitude up to 120°, timed at 3840 Hz so that the swing is not losing energy while it is being timed. A pendulum in this engine visibly damps, faster the harder it swings, and that is the price of the default §6 chose. It is a price worth knowing exactly, because a player will notice a lamp that comes to rest in four swings, and the knobs that change it are the correction, the step, and — by far the most effective — §14's sub-steps.
