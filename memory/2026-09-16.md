Why One Explodes**, which opens Module 8.

Planned at 5 h, shipped at 6, so Module 7 went 46 h → 47 h and the course total 520 → 521. The index

## What the lesson is, in one line

The engine gets ears — and the interesting half is not the arithmetic (mixing is addition, a
distance curve is one divide, panning is two trig functions) but the fact that **this is the first
subsystem whose output cannot be looked at**, which forces every claim to be measured rather than
observed.

## The findings worth keeping

**Energy adds, amplitudes do not — and this is one fact wearing four hats.** The √N growth of N
uncorrelated voices, the 3.01 dB hole in the linear pan law, the 20 rather than 10 in the decibel
formula, and the 1/d rather than 1/d² of distance attenuation are all the same statement about
squares. Measured: RMS lands on **0.577·√N exactly** for N = 1…64, with a control mixing the *same*
voice N times that lands on **N exactly** — which is what makes the first result mean anything.

**But the peak follows neither law, and it keeps growing with how long you listen.** 16 voices peak
at 6.98× one voice after ten milliseconds and **10.46× after eight seconds**, because a peak is the
largest coincidence that happened to occur rather than a property of the signals. *Headroom is a
bet, not a bound.* `1/N` is a bound bought with 12 dB of signal; `1/√N` is what engines ship.

**The gain is a lossy reconstruction too** — 7.7's rule, in a subsystem that had never heard of
animation. Sampled at 60 Hz by the game, reconstructed at 48 kHz by the mixer: a zero-order hold
injects **0.100745 in one sample, 7.70× the largest step the waveform itself ever takes**, and only
**1.2%** of that energy sits at the tone's own frequency. The other 98.8% is what a click *is*. Four
lines of per-sample ramp reduce it to 1.00× — i.e. to the waveform's own motion.

The same yardstick settles the content half: a sound starting on a peak steps 0.2500 (38.2×), and a
5 ms fade brings it to 0.0065443, which **is** `A·2πf/f_s`. The fade does not make the step small; it
removes the step, and what is left is the sound.

## The control that carries the most weight, and the one that was wrong

Section G holds the **gain constant**, where a ramp has nothing to interpolate and the two paths must
agree bit for bit — measured 8.941e−09. So the 7.70× in G.2 is a difference between two
*reconstructions*, not between two code paths.

And the instrument that was wrong is the more useful story. `scratch/devcheck_78.cpp` — four lines
that open a real device, the one thing the offline harness cannot test — reported **28 of 58 buffers
"starved"** on a mix at 0.2% load. The counter incremented when `SDL_GetAudioStreamQueued()` was 0 at
callback entry, which in SDL3's **pull** model is the steady state, not a failure. Renamed to
`queue_empty`; the real question is now `late`, which compares the mixer's own time against its own
deadline and needs nothing from the driver. **A control that fires on the healthy case is not a
control.**

## The design decision that paid for everything

`mix_into()` is **public** and `open_offline()` builds the voice table with no device. The whole
harness — nine sections, twelve controls — runs on a machine that was never asked whether it had
speakers, and `demos/audio --shot` uses the same entry point, so a screenshot is silent and
deterministic. *Real-time code you cannot call from a test is code you cannot debug.*

# 2026-09-16 (session 2) — Lesson 8.1: the determinant that opens Module 8

**84 of 107 published. MODULE 8 IS OPEN — 1 of 13, ~6 h of ~69.** `next:` is now **8.2 — Forces,
Gravity, and Linear Rigid Bodies**.

Planned at 5 h, shipped at 6, so Module 8 went 68 → 69 h and the course total 521 → 522. Module 8's
index badge went from nothing to `in progress`.

## What the lesson is, in one line

Eighty-three lessons in, nothing in this engine moves on its own — and the two lines everybody writes
first to fix that are **unconditionally unstable**, which is a statement about a determinant rather
than about accuracy.

## The findings worth keeping

**One step is a matrix, and the whole lesson is its determinant.** For `a = −ω²x` every rule is a 2×2
map: explicit Euler `1 + h²ω²` (>1 always), semi-implicit `exactly 1`, backward Euler
`1/(1 + h²ω²)` — the exact reciprocal, so it is the same disease with the sign flipped, which is why
it damps rather than explodes and why nobody ships it. Measured at **1.010966377 against a predicted
1.010966182**. And **area is energy** (an orbit encloses `2πE/ω`), so `(1.010966229)^3600 = 1.1270e17`
is not a model of the blow-up, it *is* the blow-up — measured to every digit printed.

**The standard yardstick is blind to all of it, and its good news was an accident.** Order of accuracy
reads explicit 1.089 and semi-implicit **2.002** — and that 2 is a fact about the harmonic oscillator,
not a promotion: the amplitude error is exactly zero (which is the property under investigation), so
only a frequency shift survives and that is O(h²). Order is the `h → 0` question at fixed `t`; a game
asks `t → ∞` at fixed `h`. Fix h at 1/60 and the columns separate immediately.

**What semi-implicit Euler conserves is not energy.** The true energy wobbles by exactly `hω` peak to
peak — predicted 0.104720, measured **0.104723** — and the conserved quantity is the sheared cousin
`½(v² + ω²x² − hω²·xv)`, held to **4.6e−6** over 3,600 float steps. Checked by hand on one step too:
39.478418 → 39.478410 while the true energy dropped 1.08%. **And its being positive-definite is the
stability limit**: `det[[1, −ε/2], [−ε/2, 1]] > 0 ⟺ hω < 2`. Two determinants, two stability results.
Bisected at 0.3182939 against 0.3183099.

**Bounded is not right.** At `hω = 1.95` the orbit is perfectly stable and swings ×40 — measured
against `1/(1 − hω/2)` to three digits at every step size. Budget `hω ≤ 0.4`, which at 60 Hz admits a
**3.8 Hz spring**. A contact is hundreds of Hz. That gap is the argument, made nine lessons early,
for why 8.9/8.10 solve impulses rather than penalty springs.

**And gravity forgives everything**, which is why the bug ships: all three rules agree on velocity to
the last bit and err in position by exactly `∓½·a·h·t`, *linearly* — 8.2 cm after a second — with
velocity Verlet exact. The relative error even *improves* with time (1.7% → 0.21%), because the error
grows like `t` and the distance like `t²`.

## The instruments that were wrong, and the ones that caught it

**A microbenchmark measuring the optimiser.** Passing the rule as a runtime enum made explicit 15%
faster in one run and 30% slower in the next — loop unswitching is a mood. A template non-type
parameter made each loop monomorphic and the spread dropped to 0.3%, which is what let the actual
finding stand: **0.994 ns against 0.963 ns**, i.e. the correct rule is free. Also counter-intuitive:
the *non-template* overload in the .cpp is the slowest path (1.222 ns), because it cannot inline
across `libengine.a`.

**A bisection that answers when the answer is "none".** Run on explicit Euler it returns 3.4 ms,
which looks like a stability limit and is a fact about how long the test ran. `max_stable_step`
returns **0.0** instead — a caller scaling it gets a simulation that does not move, which beats one
that explodes in a playtest.

**A tiny probe that threw away three digits.** Section B's parallelogram started at ε = 1e-3 on the
reasonable-sounding grounds that a derivative wants a small step; the map is *exactly linear*, so
size cancels algebraically and the only effect was cancellation between near-equal floats. ε = 0.5
took the control from 0.999974 to 1.000000119.

# 2026-09-16 (session 3) — Lesson 8.2: the engine gets bodies

**85 of 107 published. Module 8 is 2 of 13, ~11 h of ~70.** `next:` is now **8.3 — Angular
Dynamics: Torque and the Inertia Tensor**.

Planned at 4 h, shipped at 5, so Module 8 went 69 → 70 h and the course total 522 → 523. Index
prose, both hero stats and the module subtotal moved together; `check-curriculum.py` green, and
checks 1, 2, 10 and 11 were all clean on the first run — the first time since check 11 existed.

## What the lesson is, in one line

8.1 taught the engine to advance a state through time and left it unable to tell a crate from a
pebble, and the fix — `F = ma` — is furniture; **everything interesting is what the engine has to
decide in order to make it usable**.

## The findings worth keeping

**The engine changed its mind about gravity, and a measurement made it.** Weight *is* a force, so
the first draft put `m·g` into the accumulator with everything else — the honest reading, and the
one the header was written around. It needs a **float divide per body per step** to recover a mass
the body deliberately does not store, and that divide is **15% of the whole update** (b/a = 0.845,
0.848, 0.847 over three runs) — *more than 8.1's entire semi-implicit step at 0.963 ns*. Gravity now
goes in after the division, which is also exact by construction where the other route is exact only
by luck. Box2D makes the same choice in the same place. **What it cost is named in §12**:
`rigid_body::force` no longer contains weight, so a force overlay draws every push except the one
that is always there.

**And the opposite result, kept rather than buried.** Comparing squared speeds to avoid a `sqrt` per
body — the far more famous optimisation — measured **1.036, 0.968, 0.969**: a tie, once a loss. A
pipelined `sqrtss` off the critical path is free; a `divss` is not. The `sqrt` stays, because when a
measurement is a wash the version that reads as what it means wins by default. *Two optimisations,
and the famous one was the tie.*

**Hand-picked test data agrees with the code by construction.** Seven masses — 0.001, 0.1, 1, 7,
100, 1000, 1e6 — all recovered `g` exactly through `(g/w)·w`, which looked like a proof. A sweep of
**a million** log-uniform masses found **15.9937% that do not**, worst 1.00 ulp, and the first
failure is **1.000145 kg** rather than anything exotic. The follow-up is the better half: through
the force route two such bodies differ by 1 ulp after one step, 1 ulp after sixty, and are
**identical again after six hundred** — the error *wanders* rather than accumulating. Invisible in a
fall; fatal to a replay, a lockstep session or a golden test.

**A control can have a bug and not fail — it produces a third number.** E.4 holds a force for
`round(rate/60)` steps, which at 144 Hz is 13.9 ms rather than 16.7, so the "control" for the
impulse/force comparison reported a value that matched neither arm and invited an explanation.
Rescaling the force to the duration actually held fixed it.

**Print the discrete prediction, not only the continuous one.** Damping's terminal speed is `g/k` =
19.6200 in the limit and the loop settles at **19.5383**. No bug: our step applies gravity and then
damps, so its fixed point is `g·h·e^(−kh)/(1 − e^(−kh))` = **19.5384**. A 0.42% offset far too
consistent to be noise, and a harness printing only `g/k` would have sent somebody bug-hunting.

**And the frame half, which is where the lesson's title lives.** A body integrated under a parent
scaled by 2 falls at **2 g**. The trap is the panel in between: a non-uniform scale of (2,1,1)
reports gain **1.0000** and tilt **0.000°** purely because gravity points along y and the scale is
on x — so a diagnostic built only from "where does gravity end up" signs it off, and the first body
to be *rotated* in it falls at **1.5811 g, 63.435° off vertical**. The number that catches it is
`out_of_square`, built in **7.5** to report what a `quat` could not hold and uncalled for three
lessons. Worst of all a *spinning* parent passes every test a matrix is capable of answering — **a
matrix has no time derivative** — while putting a force-free body **3.7963 m** off a straight line
in one second.

## Alternate the arms or measure the machine

§11's first draft timed four loops one after another and reported the *same* arrangement at
**2.625, 1.758, 1.476 and 1.459 ns/body** on four consecutive runs of one binary: the machine was
still settling and whichever arm ran first always lost. 5.6's `bench_compare` runs them one rep
each, alternately, and the **ratio of medians then repeats to three decimals**. Also worth knowing:
`bench_result::spread()` over 2000 reps is dominated by a handful of scheduler outliers and reads as
1–4 on a machine behaving perfectly, so the report prints **min beside median** and quotes the
ratio.

# 2026-09-16 (session 4) — Lesson 8.3: the engine learns to turn

**86 of 107 published. Module 8 is 3 of 13, ~17 h of ~70.** `next:` is now **8.4 — Collision
Primitives: Spheres, AABBs, OBBs, and the SAT**.

Planned at 6 h and **shipped at 6** — the first lesson since 7.3 to land on its estimate — so no
module subtotal and no course total moved. Still 107 lessons, ~523 h. `check-curriculum.py` green,
with checks 1, 2, 10 and 11 clean on the first run for the second lesson running.

## What the lesson is, in one line

8.2 answered "how hard is it to push?"; this answers "how hard is it to **turn**?" — and the answer
needs nine numbers, does not commute, is not conserved, and contains a term that is not physics.

## The findings worth keeping

**The expression the derivation hands you is the one you must not ship.** `|r|²·1 − r⊗r` is what
falls out of the triple product, and its diagonal is `|r|² − x²` — a sum of three squares with one
subtracted straight off again. For `r = (1000, 0.001, 0)` a `float` computes `1000000 − 1000000` and
returns **exactly zero** where the answer is `1e−6`: 100% wrong, and the `1e−6` was never *in* the
sum (ulp at 1e6 is 0.0625). `−[r]ₓ[r]ₓ` is the same algebra, never forms the cancelling sum, and is
nine multiplies instead of two matrix products. The consequence is *behavioural*: a zero principal
moment is a singular tensor, so every plank, rail and sword silently refuses to spin about its own
length. **Third appearance of this class** after 6.16's `perspective()` and 8.2's mass round trip —
a derivation says *what* to compute, not *how*.

**The gyroscopic term is not physics.** It appears on the line where you differentiate
`L = I(t)ω` and *insist that ω be the state*. Four treatments, all measured over 60 torque-free
seconds: explicit is 8.1's determinant in the last place this engine integrates explicitly
(**3.2967e+11** drift at 30 Hz); implicit — one Newton step in the body frame, Bullet's — is 8.1's
*other* determinant, the exact reciprocal, so it damps instead and **loses 35%**; and the momentum
formulation, which stores `L` and derives `ω`, makes the term vanish from the equations for
**8.0e−04** and less code.

**And that column gets worse as the step shrinks** — 3.86e−4, 8.05e−4, 3.01e−3, 1.18e−2 at
30/60/240/960 Hz. The only table in this course that does. Its error is not truncation: nothing in
the momentum formulation approximates `L`, so what is left is the rounding of a round trip the
engine performs once per step (it stores `ω`, not `L`), which grows with the **number** of steps
rather than their size. Storing `L` is named as debt.

**Conserving the right quantity is not enough.** `momentum` conserves `L` *by construction* whatever
it does with the orientation — so `|L|` cannot tell you the body is wrong. The *second* conserved
quantity can: paired with a start-of-step `ω`, kinetic energy drifts **97.1%** and `|ω|` reaches
8.0350 where §8 **derives** a bound of 4.0524…4.4880. One extra half step: **1.198e−04**, and
4.0525…4.4879, inside the bound. A factor of **8,100** for one rotation matrix.

**The bound is derived, not observed**, and that is the strongest check in the lesson. Write
`uᵢ = Lᵢ²` and *both* conserved quantities are linear in `u`, so the reachable set is a segment,
`|ω|²` is linear on it, and a linear function on a segment extremises at the ends — three
candidates, one infeasible. Predicted 4.0524…4.4880, measured 4.0527…4.4924.

**The tennis racket theorem, predicted and measured.** σ = 4.8038 s⁻¹ against 4.9058 / 4.8310 /
4.8128 / **4.8084** at 120/480/1920/7680 Hz — each refinement roughly quarters the error, which is
the midpoint pairing's second order showing. Flips every **2.6651 s**, forever. And the control
carries the argument: term off, the perturbation stays at 1.0000e−04 for forty seconds and never
moves. No flip, ever.

## The instruments that were wrong

**A fit that missed by 20% because of a frame.** §10's first version read **3.8498** against a
predicted 4.8038 and did not converge. Euler's equations are *body*-frame equations; the engine
stores `ω` in world space; and for a spin about `y` the two perturbation components are carried
around `y` at the spin rate — so a world-space `ω.x` oscillates at 10 Hz with a growing envelope,
and sampling it at a threshold crossing samples the oscillation. **The tell was that the
oscillation was at the spin rate** — a perturbation has no reason to know about it.

**A benchmark that measured a `memcpy`.** Each arm was `body_world w = seed; … w.step(h)`, so both
arms would start from identical state — a reasonable instinct that put a 557 KB pool copy *inside*
the timed region. Every arm read 34–58 ns/body against 8.2's 1–2 and every ratio came out near 1,
**including the control**, which is the shape of a benchmark measuring something both arms share.

**And a first fit window that caught a saturated signal.** The perturbation grows exponentially and
then saturates (the body flips instead), so taking the *last* crossing rather than the first fitted
0.469 against 4.804. Fixed by fitting over the first decade only.

## The optimisation, which is not an algorithm

The step came out at **33.793 ns/body**, twenty times 8.2's. Breaking it down: `mat3_from_quat` at
7.843 ns, called **twice from the same quaternion** twenty lines apart — once inside
`world_inverse_inertia`, once inside `angular_momentum` — and `angular_momentum` itself at 13.418 ns,
**40% of the whole step**, for a diagnostic computed whether or not anybody reads it.

Storing the forward tensor beside its inverse and hoisting the rotation matrix took it to
**18.778 ns/body**, 1.80×, with no number on the page changing. The hoist is A/B'd *inside one run*
(0.764) so it is a fact rather than a before-and-after story. `sizeof(rigid_body)` 60 → 172 bytes,
one cache line → three, which is the number 8.10's solver will care about.

## What shipped

`engine/phys/inertia.{hpp,cpp}` — a new file pair, and the split **is** the dependency direction 8.2

## What the lesson is, in one line

8.3 answered "how hard is it to turn?"; this answers "are these two touching?" — and the answer is
one genuinely beautiful idea (a gap in any shadow proves them apart) plus a pile of consequences
nobody warns you about.

## The findings worth keeping

**I wrote the folklore into the shipped doc comments before measuring it, and had to go back and
correct them.** "Guard the cross products or objects fall through each other" is **true of the
unnormalised absR formulation** (188 false separations in 400) and **false of the normalised one**
(zero, with the guard removed entirely). The reason is the theorem read backwards: if two convex
bodies overlap then *no* direction separates them, so an axis whose direction is pure rounding error
reports an overlap like any other. Rounding cannot invent a gap where no direction has one. The
guard we ship is for skipping redundant work, keeping the MTV's direction honest, and avoiding
`0/0` — not for correctness. **Measure first, then write the comment.**

**The first two fixtures found nothing, for two different reasons, and both are transferable.** A
tiny rotation about a *world* axis leaves the cross products **exact** — their terms are products
with zero, so nothing cancels and nothing rounds. Then with generic orientations, a *cube* still
could not fail, because a cube's projected radius is ≥ 0.5 in every direction and a spurious axis
cannot produce a gap at all. The configuration that fails is two similar boxes meeting at a
**corner** with a shared axis — which is also the arrangement every scene is full of.

**And the degenerate pair is the default, not an exotic case.** Two objects standing on the same
floor have parallel up axes at *every* yaw, and never exactly: both arrive through
`mat3_from_quat`, so the cross comes out at **1.22e−07** rather than zero. A *true* zero is
harmless (`0 > 0` is false, which is right; and `0/0` = NaN compares false against everything, so
even that fails safe). The damage is done by the axis that is almost zero.

**A guard that never fires on random data is not a guard that is never needed.** 0 firings in
1,800,000 candidate axes at random orientations. Random test data and a real scene disagree
completely about which inputs are typical — and 8.6's uniform grid will have exactly this property.

**Six axes are not enough, and the counterexample is built rather than found.** Pick
`L = normalise(a0 × b0)` *first*, then place the second plank along it at reach + 5 cm. All six face
normals report overlaps of 0.118 to 3.128 m; `a0 × b0` reports **+0.05000**. On 200,000 random pairs
**5,727** are separated only by an edge axis — 4.6% of everything six axes call a collision — and
when boxes genuinely overlap the MTV is on an edge axis **49.8%** of the time. (Uniform random
orientations; a real scene of things resting on floors is face-dominated, so 49.8% is the upper end.)

**An answer that carries its own proof is worth designing for.** Returning the axis instead of a
`bool` is what let the harness **re-prove 179,056 separations in double precision** from the shape
data alone — no second implementation to be wrong in the same way. Overlap does not certify itself
so cheaply, so the other half is a 41³ witness hunt: 364 of 365, and the miss is a **4.485e−03 m**
sliver, which is an instrument out of resolution rather than a wrong answer. Reporting the depth of
the miss is what lets you tell those apart.

**The precision floor is set by where things are, not by what you do to them.** A 1 mm gap is 2.3%
wrong at 1 km, unresolvable at 10 km, and reports **exactly zero** at 100 km. The control —
recomputing in double from the same float centres — shows the centres lost it before `collide` was
called. Every quantity in a collision test should be a difference taken as early as possible, and
8.7's manifolds must store offsets rather than world points.

**The two formulations are a deliberate pair, and the ratio is all square roots.** `collide()`
normalises because the depth must be comparable across candidates; `overlaps()` does not because the
boolean is scale-invariant. **79.258 ns against 10.458 ns**, 7.58×, same answers on 200,000 pairs.
