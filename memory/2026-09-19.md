## The findings worth keeping

**The penalty method is not unstable. It is TUNED, and §1's first draft was wrong about it.** Given
164 settings (4 stiffnesses × 41 dampings, 3 s each), the best lands the crate with *zero* residual
bounce — at **108.60 mm** of penetration. The shallowest stable setting sits at 12.55 mm and bounces
**366.75 mm forever**. At 60 Hz you get one or the other. The impulse solver takes both (12.553 mm,
0.000 mm) with no knob at all. What actually kills penalty contacts is **transfer**: the same spring
under a 200 kg crate lets it **962 mm** into a half-metre floor. That is a better argument than the
one the section set out to make.

**The static stiffness is the optimistic number.** `k = m·g/x` gives `ω = √(g/x)` with the *mass
cancelled*, so the allowed sink alone sets the frame rate — 1 mm needs ≥ 49.5 Hz, and 60 Hz can do
no better than 0.681 mm. But a *moving* crate's sink is `v√(m/k)`: 20.0 mm predicted, **44.06 mm**
measured, on a floor tuned for one. Getting 1 mm out of that impact wants `k = 7.85 × 10⁷` and
**990 Hz**.

**The rearranged effective mass proves its own safety.** `dot(d, cross(I⁻¹(r×d), r))` becomes
`dot(w, I⁻¹w)` by the scalar triple product, which is manifestly non-negative for a positive
semi-definite tensor and therefore proves `k ≥ 1/mₐ + 1/m_b > 0`. Over 200,000 random contacts the
two forms agree to **5.016e-07** and the gap **never once went negative** — a check the agreement
could not have made, because both forms would carry the same sign error.

**Momentum is a tautology and energy is the check.** `apply_impulse_pair` uses the same `J` twice
with opposite signs, so momentum reads 1e-7 even when the impulse is wrong. The control settles it:
halve the effective mass on purpose and momentum still reads **1.91e-07** while energy reads
**9.55e-01**.

**A ball that bounces forever, with its height in closed form.** Semi-implicit Euler applies gravity
before the solver looks, so the approach speed is too large by `g·h` = 16.35 cm/s. The error is
*additive* and the bounce is *multiplicative*, so the bounce has a fixed point at `e·g·h/(1−e)`:
predicted 0.1635 / 0.6540 / 3.1065 m/s at e = 0.5 / 0.8 / 0.95, measured **1.000×** at the first
two. That is a 2.2 cm hop at 0.8 and 58 cm at 0.95. And the naive fix — "subtract g·h" — is wrong
for two dynamic bodies, since gravity gave both the same velocity; the control reads
3.066632748 m/s with and without, identically.

**The restitution threshold turns out to be the same fix made approximately**, which nothing I have
read says out loud. Keep both anyway: a 300 N thruster the solver was never told about brings the
buzz back at 9.90 mm and only the threshold catches it.

**Box friction is 42.68% direction-dependent and the cone is 0.00%**, over 361 sliding directions,
with the extreme ratio **0.70085** against a predicted `1/√2 = 0.70711` — and the basis it is
anisotropic in came out of the *floor's normal*. The cone costs 6.8%.

**The slope test disagreed with `atan(μ)` by 3.75° and the fixture was at fault.** A block has two
critical angles and does whichever comes first; a cube tips at 45° and μ = 1 wants to slide at 45°.
A 10:3 slab tracks the formula to **0.43°**. The tell was that the error *grew with the parameter*
(0.0036° at μ = 0.2) — a wrong constant would have been wrong at both ends — and the control was the
same instrument pointed at the other formula: μ = 5 lets go at **45.0032°**.

**The combine-rule default is `minimum`, which Box2D, Bullet and PhysX do not ship.** Five surface
pairs with all three coefficients published: 23.0% mean error against the geometric mean's 119.6%,
and under ±30% perturbation of every number, 20,000 trials, `min` wins **100.00%**. What it costs is
named in the header: `min` is insensitive to the grippier material.

**A sliding sphere rolls at exactly `v₀/(1+c)` and μ cancels** — and the 0.06% the simulation misses
5/7 by is *predictable*, not noise: 8.7 puts the contact point midway between the surfaces, so the
lever arm is `R − depth/2`. The corrected form tracks the measurement to **7.75e-07** across a 150×
sweep the prediction had not seen.

**At one pass the wrong solve order has no friction at all.** Not less: none. 51.2602 mm of slide,
*exactly* what the same slab does at μ = 0, because every frame starts with a zero accumulator and
every cone therefore has radius zero. Two things hide it: iteration, and warm starting (which hands
the cone last frame's radius). And it is invisible on a flat floor.

**And the honest ending: one pass over four contact points does not hold a crate up.** It sinks
**274.57 mm in fifteen seconds** and never stops. Thirty-two passes converge and the depth *freezes*
at 7.2252 mm — the penetration it arrived with. Two different problems; iteration fixes one and
cannot touch the other.

## The two mistakes that cost the most time

**Warm starting inside the iteration loop presented as friction failing.** With sixteen iterations
the inherited impulse was applied sixteen times, and a crate slid **810 mm** down a slope it should
have gripped. The symptom named the wrong subsystem entirely — the spurious normal impulses launched
the crate off the surface, so it looked like the Coulomb clip. Hence `warm_start_contacts` as its
own once-per-step call.

**And a four-degree error in 8.6's EPA that five lessons had not seen.** Two spheres head on: the
manifold normal is **4.2602° off** the line of centres, *constant* across four decades of overlap,
so not convergence noise. It is `epa_config::max_iterations = 32` — 8.6 set it on the evidence of a
worst case of 21 expansions over 200,000 pairs *of boxes*, and a sphere has no flat face to
terminate on. At 64 it drops to 1.06°. 8.7 hides it whenever one shape is a box, because the
manifold takes the reference *face's* normal. Reported and **not fixed**: it is 8.6's knob and
pricing the change needs 8.6's own measurement rerun. It is in the handover.

## The findings worth keeping

**The contraction is 0.53 per sweep, constant over four decades**, measured on ONE crate. A
four-point manifold is already a system: on a 0.25 m cube three quarters of the effect of an
impulse at a corner is *rotation*, and rotation is what the four points share. The control is a
sphere — one point, nothing to iterate against, 2.5e-13 at one pass and identical at sixteen. The
two rows below 1e-07 are excluded from the mean, because there the residual has stopped measuring
the solver and started measuring `float`: including them reports 0.64 where the solver is 0.53.

**Warm starting is worth 89 iterations against more than 400**, and at the shipped eight the cold
five-crate tower does not settle badly, it *collapses*: 2,020 mm of sink against 40. Match rate
97.62% over 600 frames. And **the ×5 control is the warning that goes with every convergence
number in the lesson** — 52 iterations, a beautiful residual, and a tower moving at 1.54 m/s.
*The residual measures consistency, not correctness.*

**A cached number is meaningless without the frame it was measured in, and that bug was three
lessons old.** `contact_point::tangent_impulse` is two *coordinates* in a basis that was never
stored, and `tangent_basis` re-chooses the basis every frame from the normal's smallest component
— which on a near-vertical contact is |n.x| against |n.z|, two numbers around 1e-05 that cross
constantly. **113 of 11,830 manifold-frames rotate by more than 30°, worst 134.6**, and last
frame's friction is applied sideways. Fix: store the basis on the manifold, rotate on read. Two
dot products, exact, 380 mm of drift → 155. Nothing in the type system was ever going to catch it
and the symptom names no subsystem; what found it was a **probe**, not a test.

**The arrival depth is a ceiling, not an equality** — §6's first draft claimed the equality and
was refused by up to 81%. The body crosses the surface *inside* a step and is sampled a whole step
later, so over 200 finely spaced drops per row the depth is **uniform on [0, v·h]**: mean 0.486 /
0.489 / 0.505, largest 0.9725, smallest 0.002. And it is frozen: **3.09%** across a 64× range of
iteration count while the residual moves six orders of magnitude.

**Baumgarte adds energy and split impulse does not, at the same rate.** `τ = −h/ln(1−β)` measured
at 333.33 / 166.67 / 83.33 / 33.33 ms against 324.93 / 158.19 / 74.69 / 32.63 — every one the
prediction rounded up to a whole frame, which is the finest a once-a-frame measurement can be. A
crate 100 mm deep departs at **1.14006 m/s** and 6.50 J from nowhere, against **0.00000**. With
gravity on it is mostly *hidden*, which is why it survives: below a 200 mm start the push cannot
beat the climb. At 200 mm Baumgarte throws the crate **99 mm clear of the floor**.

**The decay fixture had to have gravity in it**, and the tell was the *shape* of the error: 33%
fast at β = 0.05 and exact at 0.40. An error that grows as a parameter shrinks is a second effect,
and the second effect was the section's own subject — an unheld crate keeps the correction
velocity and coasts out at the largest bias it ever saw.

**The slop refused its own folklore.** A *single* resting crate is bit-for-bit motionless at a slop
of zero under either correction, because a geometric correction never reaches zero, so the contact
never separates and there is nothing to oscillate. The folklore describes β = 1, which nobody
ships. What needs the slop is contacts that **compete**: a five-crate tower moves 1801 µm p-p at
zero, 9086 at 0.5 mm, and 0.00 at 2 mm and above. And the cost is exactly legible — the resting
depth *is* the slop.

**Islands: 20 against 1, on the same contacts.** A body that cannot move is not a bridge, and it is
*safe* rather than convenient: an impulse applied to a fixed body changes nothing any other
contact can read. 2.48 ns per body for the whole partition, allocating nothing, because the
union–find uses its own output array as the parent array.

**The sleep test cannot run before the solve, and the reason is g·h.** Every dynamic body is
travelling at 0.1635 m/s before the solver looks — 3.3× the default threshold — so a pre-solve
test never fires at any setting and looks exactly like thresholds that are too tight. Measured on
one frame of a scene motionless for six seconds: **0 of 100 quiet before, 96 of 100 after**.
Waking is the opposite and must run *before*, hence two functions at opposite ends of `solve`.
This is 8.9's restitution artifact wearing a different hat.

**Sleeping is per island because a slept body is an immovable body.** Crate thrown at the bottom of
a sleeping three-crate tower: the island rule drops the top crate **0.500 m**, the per-body rule
**0.0000** — it hangs in the air. And the fair experiment is a *collision*; a support removed by
teleport is invisible to both rules, which is what `wake` is for.

**The `body_world::step` split is bit-identical**, 400 of 400 bodies over 120 steps, worst position
difference 0.000e+00 — checked against a private copy of the monolithic function rather than
asserted. Solving one step late costs **2.72500 mm** against a predicted 2.72500, ratio 1.0000.
The split costs a second `mat3_from_quat` per body (+37.1%), undoing half of 8.3 §12's largest
saving; sleeping pays it back 15.7× on the same scene.

**And the honest ending.** 2 crates need 2 sweeps, 3 need 2, 5 need 4, 10 need **20**, and 15 need
more than 64. The cost is linear in the count, so a tall stack is quadratic. An under-swept tower
does not sag, it *leans*.

## The two mistakes that cost the most time

**The island label encoding.** `−label − 1` sends label 0 to `−1`, which is already the sentinel
for "no island" — so the **first island in every scene silently ceased to exist**, its contacts
were never grouped and never solved, and a tower whose bottom crate landed in it fell through the
floor while every other tower stood. It is `−label − 2`. What found it was not the falling crate
(a hundred plausible causes, five lessons of geometry among them) but **`stats.points == 0` on a
frame where `deepest()` read 57 mm** — two instruments disagreeing. Keep enough counters that they
can contradict one another.

**The §13 timing sweep changed the iteration count on a running scene**, so the zero-iteration row
dropped the whole yard through the floor and then measured the "cost" of a scene with no contacts
left in it, coming out *faster* than eight. A timing sweep over a parameter that changes the
simulation has to restore the simulation — **and the snapshot has to include the manifold cache**,
because warm starting is precisely the feature that makes a physics step depend on more than its
bodies. §B's first sweep was non-monotone for exactly that reason and it took twenty minutes.
